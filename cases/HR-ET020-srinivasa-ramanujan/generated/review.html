Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Recent-death rule. He died in 1920, so the rule does not apply. The record still treats the reported suicide attempt and his loneliness as sensitive.
  2. Name. In a Tamil name of this kind there is no family name: “Ramanujan” is his own given name, and “Srinivasa” is his father’s given name placed first as a patronymic, often written as the initial “S.” (CLM-0002). Splitting “Srinivasa Ramanujan” at the last space would code “Ramanujan” as a family name and “Srinivasa” as his given name, which is wrong. `identity.name_parts` therefore codes given “Ramanujan” and places the patronymic “Srinivasa” in the required family element with `family_first` true, so the C-CDA name reads in his own order, `<family>Srinivasa</family><given>Ramanujan</given>`, and the FHIR name has given “Ramanujan,” family “Srinivasa,” and `text` “Srinivasa Ramanujan.” Neither standard has a plain patronymic element, so the family slot holds the inherited-position name, as receiving systems expect a family value. The notes call him “Mr. Ramanujan,” the form his contemporaries used and the given name by which Tamil usage addresses a person (SOC-007). The alias “S. Ramanujan” appears in no other record; “Ramanujan” alone was not made an alias.
  3. What could not be verified and was left out. Kanigel’s book The Man Who Knew Infinity and Young’s full article were not read. The roster’s “severe vitamin deficiency” as a treated diagnosis rests only on Wikipedia; the Oxford DNB lists vitamin deficiency among diagnoses advanced later, so CLM-0012 records it as an R claim, not as treatment he received. Details of the suicide attempt beyond the Oxford DNB’s words (a train driver stopping, an arrest, police released at Hardy’s request) come from later recollection quoted in secondary pages and are not used. Wartime rationing is described in the cited sources only as making his foods harder to get, and is stated that way. The lives of his wife and family, and his religious practice in detail, are not used.
  4. Conflicts. Death place: Kumbakonam (MacTutor) or Madras (Oxford DNB); CLM-0015 keeps both and the record does not choose. Fellowship of Trinity College: 10 October 1918 (MacTutor; Wikipedia gives 13 October); the claim gives the month. The operation: April 1909 after an illness in 1908 (MacTutor; Wikipedia describes a hydrocele operation in January 1910); PMH-003 uses MacTutor. The dysentery dates are approximate (“11 and 8 years before” his illness in England), so PMH-002 is dated about 1906 with year precision. The diagnosis itself is the main conflict: tuberculosis (CLM-0010, what his physicians provisionally accepted) against hepatic amoebiasis (CLM-0011, Young 1994), with other proposals (CLM-0012). All three are R and “disputed” or “possible,” and the modern record does not settle them.
  5. Index age and dates. The index visit on January 12, 2026 maps to January 12, 1917, when he was 29, in Cambridge. The roster suggested 29 or 30 at the start of the illness. The illness is documented from 1917, with admission to a nursing hostel in May 1917 and sanatoria and nursing homes until March 1919, so a modern outpatient episode spread over 2026 would place clinic visits during those stays. The schedule was therefore compressed: all seven visits, January 12 to April 20, 2026, map to January 12 to April 20, 1917, before the nursing hostel admission. The synthetic date of birth is December 22, 1996 (shift 109 years). The ultrasound on January 13, 2026 maps to the next day. A small tension remains: MacTutor and IMSc say his health was reasonably good during his first three years (to about April 1917), and the modern symptoms begin in December; this is the cost of placing the episode before the documented stays, and it is synthetic. Past medical history includes only smallpox (1889), the dysentery (about 1906, with a second episode later) and the 1909 operation. No family history is recorded: his parents’ health is not documented in the cited sources.
  6. The single modern diagnosis. The synthetic diagnosis is an amebic liver abscess, chosen because it fits the documented history better as a teaching case: dysentery years earlier in South India, fevers, and a liver-centered illness, the clues Young emphasized; and because it lets the episode teach the step that history missed, testing for tuberculosis and amebiasis side by side. Tuberculosis is not ignored: the IGRA is positive, active disease is excluded by the radiograph, three smears, NAAT and cultures, and the latent infection is treated with four months of rifampin. The modern result is synthetic and does not decide what he had.
  7. Following the presenting-problem pattern. Intake records fever (R50.9), weight loss (R63.4) and right upper quadrant pain (R10.11), resolved at the second visit when the ultrasound and serology gave the diagnosis (A06.4 recorded then). The positive IGRA (R76.12) is recorded at the second visit and resolved at six weeks, when negative cultures allowed the diagnosis of latent infection (Z22.7).
