# Teaching Note — Srinivasa Ramanujan pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Srinivasa Ramanujan was born on 22 December 1887 in his grandmother's house in Erode, in what is now Tamil Nadu, and grew up in Kumbakonam. His father, Kuppuswamy Srinivasa, was a clerk in a cloth merchant's shop, and his mother, Komalatammal, sang devotional songs; the family was Brahmin and poor. He had no family name in the Western sense. In Tamil usage the father's given name comes first as an initial or prefix, so "Srinivasa" is his father's name and "Ramanujan" is his own given name, the name by which he was known and addressed. He had smallpox in December 1889, fell ill after about three months at a college in Madras in 1906, and had a serious illness in 1908 followed by an operation in 1909 and a long recovery. He had two episodes of dysentery in India, about eleven and eight years before his illness in England. He married Janaki in July 1909, worked as a clerk at the Madras Port Trust from March 1912, wrote to G. H. Hardy at Cambridge in January 1913, sailed for England in March 1914, and moved into rooms at Trinity College, Cambridge, at the end of April 1914. An orthodox Brahmin, he was a strict vegetarian. He had trouble with his diet from the start in England; the war that began a few months after his arrival made the foods he needed harder to get, and he cooked for himself, sometimes only once a day or every other day. In wartime England he was lonely, and letters from home were often absent. His health was reasonably good during his first three years in Cambridge. He fell seriously ill in 1917: from May 1917 he was in a nursing hostel in Cambridge, then a sanatorium in the Mendip Hills in October 1917, Matlock House in Derbyshire from November 1917 to June 1918, Fitzroy House in London from June to December 1918, and Colinette House in Putney at the end of December 1918. He was treated mostly for tuberculosis. A gastric ulcer was considered and given up, and in February 1918 Hardy wrote that tuberculosis was the provisionally accepted theory. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in May 1918 and a Fellow of Trinity College in October 1918. He sailed for India on 27 February 1919, arriving on 13 March 1919, and died on 26 April 1920, at 32, in Kumbakonam according to one biography or in Madras according to the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography.
- **R:** Retrospective interpretation: his illness in England was tuberculosis, as his physicians provisionally concluded. Retrospective interpretation: in 1994 D. A. B. Young, reviewing his medical history, proposed that the illness was hepatic amoebiasis, a liver infection following his earlier dysentery, then regarded as a tropical disease and hard to diagnose, and treatable once diagnosed. Retrospective interpretation: other diagnoses advanced over the years include vitamin deficiency, lead poisoning and cancer. Retrospective account: in the period of his illness, ill health, the absence of letters from home and loneliness are said to have driven him to a suicide attempt in the London Underground, from which he came away with only a bloodied shin. The account rests on later recollection; the date is given as late 1917 or early 1918.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
