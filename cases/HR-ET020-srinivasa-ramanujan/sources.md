## Historical evidence

1. O’Connor JJ, Robertson EF. “Srinivasa Aiyangar Ramanujan,” MacTutor History of Mathematics, University of St Andrews. Supports his birth on 22 December 1887 in his grandmother’s house in Erode and his childhood in Kumbakonam; his father’s work as a clerk in a cloth merchant’s shop; smallpox in December 1889, illness in 1906 at a Madras college, and a serious illness in 1908 with an operation in April 1909; his marriage on 14 July 1909; the Madras Port Trust post from 1 March 1912; the letter to Hardy in January 1913; sailing on 17 March 1914 and moving into Trinity College on 30 April 1914; Hardy’s description of him as an orthodox Brahmin and a strict vegetarian; diet problems from the start, made worse by the war; falling seriously ill in 1917 and spending most of his time in nursing homes; Hardy’s letter of February 1918 that gastric ulcer had been given up and “tubercle has been the provisionally accepted theory”; election to the Royal Society on 2 May 1918 and to a Trinity fellowship on 10 October 1918; sailing for India on 27 February 1919 and arriving on 13 March 1919; and his death on 26 April 1920 in Kumbakonam. https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Ramanujan/
2. Kanigel R. “Ramanujan, Srinivasa (1887–1920), mathematician,” *Oxford Dictionary of National Biography* (Oxford University Press, 2004), as reproduced by MacTutor. Supports the heading and his father’s name, Kuppuswamy Srinivasa; his mother Komalatammal; a Brahman family, but poor; “a scrupulous vegetarian”; that he “was treated mostly for tuberculosis” and “went from one sanitorium to another”; that “cancer, vitamin deficiency, lead poisoning, and hepatic amoebiasis have been among the diagnoses advanced over the years”; that poor health with the absence of letters from home and loneliness in wartime England “drove him to a suicide attempt in the London Underground,” from which he came away with a bloodied shin; and his death on 26 April 1920 in Madras. https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DNB/Ramanujan.html
3. Institute of Mathematical Sciences, Chennai. “Ramanujan as a Patient” (Hill Grove). Supports that his health was reasonably good during his first three years in Cambridge; the nursing hostel in Cambridge from May 1917 for five months, the Mendip Hills for two to three weeks in October 1917, Matlock House from November 1917 to June 1918, Fitzroy House in London from June to December 1918, and Colinette House in Putney at the end of December 1918, until his departure in March 1919; treatment for tuberculosis; and his strict vegetarian diet, food shortages and “cooking only once a day or two.” https://www.imsc.res.in/~rao/ramanujan/newnow/Hillgrove.htm
4. Institute of Mathematical Sciences, Chennai. “Life and Work: Illness,” a summary of D. A. B. Young, “Ramanujan’s illness,” *Current Science* 1994. Supports the case for hepatic amoebiasis contracted in India; his two episodes of dysentery “11 and 8 years before”; that hepatic amoebiasis was regarded in 1918 as a tropical disease; that the specialists consulted were experts in tuberculosis or gastric medicine; and that the diagnosis was difficult then and remains so. https://www.imsc.res.in/~rao/ramanujan/newnow/Medicography.htm
5. Young DA. Ramanujan’s illness. *Notes and Records of the Royal Society of London* 1994;48(1):107–119. doi:10.1098/rsnr.1994.0009. PMID 11615274. The citation was verified in PubMed; the full text was not read, and the argument is taken from SRC-0004 and SRC-0006. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11615274/

## Editorial resolutions

- **Recent-death rule.** He died in 1920, so the rule does not apply. The record still treats the reported suicide attempt and his loneliness as sensitive.
- **Name.** In a Tamil name of this kind there is no family name: “Ramanujan” is his own given name, and “Srinivasa” is his father’s given name placed first as a patronymic, often written as the initial “S.” (CLM-0002). Splitting “Srinivasa Ramanujan” at the last space would code “Ramanujan” as a family name and “Srinivasa” as his given name, which is wrong. `identity.name_parts` therefore codes given “Ramanujan” and places the patronymic “Srinivasa” in the required family element with `family_first` true, so the C-CDA name reads in his own order, `<family>Srinivasa</family><given>Ramanujan</given>`, and the FHIR name has given “Ramanujan,” family “Srinivasa,” and `text` “Srinivasa Ramanujan.” Neither standard has a plain patronymic element, so the family slot holds the inherited-position name, as receiving systems expect a family value. The notes call him “Mr. Ramanujan,” the form his contemporaries used and the given name by which Tamil usage addresses a person (SOC-007). The alias “S. Ramanujan” appears in no other record; “Ramanujan” alone was not made an alias.
- **What could not be verified and was left out.** Kanigel’s book *The Man Who Knew Infinity* and Young’s full article were not read. The roster’s “severe vitamin deficiency” as a treated diagnosis rests only on Wikipedia; the Oxford DNB lists vitamin deficiency among diagnoses advanced later, so CLM-0012 records it as an R claim, not as treatment he received. Details of the suicide attempt beyond the Oxford DNB’s words (a train driver stopping, an arrest, police released at Hardy’s request) come from later recollection quoted in secondary pages and are not used. Wartime rationing is described in the cited sources only as making his foods harder to get, and is stated that way. The lives of his wife and family, and his religious practice in detail, are not used.
- **Conflicts.** Death place: Kumbakonam (MacTutor) or Madras (Oxford DNB); CLM-0015 keeps both and the record does not choose. Fellowship of Trinity College: 10 October 1918 (MacTutor; Wikipedia gives 13 October); the claim gives the month. The operation: April 1909 after an illness in 1908 (MacTutor; Wikipedia describes a hydrocele operation in January 1910); PMH-003 uses MacTutor. The dysentery dates are approximate (“11 and 8 years before” his illness in England), so PMH-002 is dated about 1906 with year precision. The diagnosis itself is the main conflict: tuberculosis (CLM-0010, what his physicians provisionally accepted) against hepatic amoebiasis (CLM-0011, Young 1994), with other proposals (CLM-0012). All three are R and “disputed” or “possible,” and the modern record does not settle them.
- **Index age and dates.** The index visit on January 12, 2026 maps to January 12, 1917, when he was 29, in Cambridge. The roster suggested 29 or 30 at the start of the illness. The illness is documented from 1917, with admission to a nursing hostel in May 1917 and sanatoria and nursing homes until March 1919, so a modern outpatient episode spread over 2026 would place clinic visits during those stays. The schedule was therefore compressed: all seven visits, January 12 to April 20, 2026, map to January 12 to April 20, 1917, before the nursing hostel admission. The synthetic date of birth is December 22, 1996 (shift 109 years). The ultrasound on January 13, 2026 maps to the next day. A small tension remains: MacTutor and IMSc say his health was reasonably good during his first three years (to about April 1917), and the modern symptoms begin in December; this is the cost of placing the episode before the documented stays, and it is synthetic. Past medical history includes only smallpox (1889), the dysentery (about 1906, with a second episode later) and the 1909 operation. No family history is recorded: his parents’ health is not documented in the cited sources.
- **The single modern diagnosis.** The synthetic diagnosis is an amebic liver abscess, chosen because it fits the documented history better as a teaching case: dysentery years earlier in South India, fevers, and a liver-centered illness, the clues Young emphasized; and because it lets the episode teach the step that history missed, testing for tuberculosis and amebiasis side by side. Tuberculosis is not ignored: the IGRA is positive, active disease is excluded by the radiograph, three smears, NAAT and cultures, and the latent infection is treated with four months of rifampin. The modern result is synthetic and does not decide what he had.
- **Following the presenting-problem pattern.** Intake records fever (R50.9), weight loss (R63.4) and right upper quadrant pain (R10.11), resolved at the second visit when the ultrasound and serology gave the diagnosis (A06.4 recorded then). The positive IGRA (R76.12) is recorded at the second visit and resolved at six weeks, when negative cultures allowed the diagnosis of latent infection (Z22.7). The B12 and vitamin D deficiencies and the adjustment disorder are known at intake from that day’s results and interview.
- **Synthetic clinical picture.** All laboratory values, imaging, cultures, medicines, scores and vital signs are synthetic. Qualitative results (radiograph, ultrasound, serology, stool tests, blood cultures, smears, NAAT, cultures, IGRA, HIV and hepatitis tests, hepatitis A antibody) appear only in note text. Height (165 cm) and weights are synthetic; the eGFR uses CKD-EPI 2021 without a race term.
- **Culture and diet.** The diet plan works inside his rules: lacto-vegetarian protein from pulses and dairy, B12 from dairy, fortified foods and a supplement, batch cooking once a day, foods he already eats, and an Indian grocery delivery. Medicines were checked for animal-derived ingredients: the vitamin D tablet has no gelatin and its lanolin source was explained with an alternative; the paromomycin capsule shell is gelatin, and he chose, after discussion, to take it as prescribed. The record attributes no religious ruling to him.
- **Mood, isolation and safety.** The loneliness and absence of letters from home are documented (CLM-0007, sensitive). The modern PHQ-9 of 12 and the adjustment disorder are synthetic; because the code is F43.21 and the PHQ-9 is 10 or more, `clinical.safety_assessment` is present, records no ideation, and includes the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline; safety is reviewed at every later visit, including the sick and preventive visits. The reported suicide attempt (CLM-0014, R, possible, sensitive) is history only: it is dated after the modern episode, linked only to its timeline event, absent from the intake documents and the care plan, and written without method detail beyond the source’s own words.
- **Setting.** The roster places him as a student far from home in England. The record follows the project’s U.S. conventions (SSN, CPT, CDC and USPSTF guidance, the 988 Lifeline), so the modern setting is left unnamed (“a university here”); the elements that matter for teaching, a young man from South India far from his family, are kept. The 988 Lifeline is replaced by a crisis contact in India for his trip.
- **Real people.** His parents, his wife, G. H. Hardy and D. A. B. Young appear only in documented history and the retrospective claims. In the modern episode his wife and family are mentioned only as people he calls and will visit; no modern decision, statement or health fact is attributed to any real person.
- **Sick visit.** A same-day visit for a viral upper respiratory infection three weeks into rifampin and six weeks after the abscess treatment, chosen because any fever after a liver abscess raises the question of relapse: the examination, white count, alkaline phosphatase and a CRP of 11 mg/L answer it without imaging, and rifampin continues. It insinuates no real diagnosis.
- **Preventive visit.** A periodic adult preventive visit at 29 (CPT 99395 with 99497 in plain text), with pre-travel advice for a visit to South India: injectable typhoid vaccine (the oral live vaccine is avoided with rifampin), hepatitis A antibody checked (positive at the next visit), malaria advice from a travel clinic, Tdap and the first hepatitis B dose; HPV vaccination declined after shared decision-making; lipid screening due.
- **Focused visits.** The four follow-ups (two days, twelve days, six weeks and three months) are documented as focused visits on the CDC and OFID amebiasis guidance, the ATS/IDSA/CDC tuberculosis diagnosis and treatment guidelines, the NTCA/CDC 2020 latent infection guideline, the CMID anemia and depression monographs, and the VA/DoD suicide-risk guideline. One deviation is recorded instead of an invented result: the reticulocyte count one week after starting vitamin B12 (CMID anemia monograph) was not ordered; the rising hemoglobin at twelve days and six weeks and a normal neurologic examination showed no harm, and the replacement order set now includes it. Repeat liver imaging is recorded as not indicated with clinical recovery.
- **Consultation.** The infectious diseases referral and consultation by Priya Raghunathan, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the second visit, are **S**. The consultant answers the six referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: an amebic rather than pyogenic abscess, and active tuberculosis very unlikely (confirmed by negative cultures at six weeks); no aspiration (fever gone by day 3); metronidazole then paromomycin without overlap (done); latent infection treated with four months of rifampin once cultures were negative and liver tests normal (started at six weeks, by message with the consultant); no tuberculosis notification and no contact screening; no repeat imaging (none done).
