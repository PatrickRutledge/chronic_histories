Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Roald Engelbregt Gravning Amundsen was born on July 16, 1872, at the farm Hvidtsten in Borge, Østfold, Norway. [D]
  • He wrote that as a young man he was near-sighted, a defect that had gradually improved but was not wholly corrected, and that he had refused to wear the glasses prescribed for him so that it would not keep him out of military training. [D]
  • On a winter ski crossing of the Hardangervidda plateau in his early twenties he nearly died in a snow cave during a rest stop, and he wrote that he and his companion both got their fingers badly frostbitten while breaking into a nailed-up hut. [D]
  • As first mate of the Belgian Antarctic expedition in the Belgica (1897 to 1899), he spent thirteen months in the pack ice. He wrote that every member of the ship's company was afflicted with scurvy and all but three were prostrated by it; once fresh seal and penguin meat was eaten, the men plainly improved within a week. The expedition's physician had encouraged the crew to eat seal and penguin. [D]
  • A physician historian argues that the heart failure seen on the Belgica expedition, described at the time as 'polar anaemia', was almost certainly wet beriberi from thiamine deficiency, and that most sufferers were probably also deficient in vitamin C. [R]
  • He led the first voyage through the Northwest Passage in a single ship, the Gjøa (1903 to 1906), and on December 14, 1911, he and four companions were the first to reach the South Pole. In 1925 he led a flight by flying boat toward the North Pole from Ny-Ålesund, and in 1926 the flight of the airship Norge across the Arctic. [D]
  • On the Maud expedition, in the autumn of 1918, he fell down the ship's runway onto his right shoulder; the fracture was set aboard by a crewman trained in first aid, and he kept to his bed for eight days. On November 8, 1918, a polar bear struck him on the back, leaving minor gashes. He wrote that he could not at first lift his right hand to use a pencil, worked the arm daily for months, and that a surgeon in Seattle who X-rayed it in 1921 found the shoulder in a condition that theoretically would make the arm unusable. [D]
  • About the New Year of 1918 to 1919, working in the small, unventilated observatory of the Maud with a kerosene vapour lamp, he became drowsy, felt his heart beating unnaturally, and nearly lost consciousness before he reached fresh air. He wrote that the violent palpitation lasted several days, that for months strenuous exercise set his heart pounding, and that in February he was exhausted by climbing a fifty-foot hill. [D]
  • He wrote that in February 1922 a heart specialist in London told him "No more expeditions!" and to avoid all strenuous exercise; nine months later he travelled about 800 miles on foot through the snow from Point Barrow to Nome, and five years later he wrote that he had had no unpleasant results from his exertions. [D]
  • The Norwegian encyclopedia describes the observatory episode as carbon monoxide poisoning. [R]
  • He bought the house Uranienborg at Svartskog, on the Bunnefjord near Oslo, in 1908 and lived there for twenty years. His study had an exercise spring installed in the floor for his strength training, and a pump brought salt water from the fjord for his morning bath. [D]
  • After his 1927 lecture tour of Japan he returned by the Trans-Siberian railway; letters place him in Oslo in August 1927 and again in May 1928. [D]
  • The Norwegian encyclopedia writes that by the end of his career he felt tired, lonely and bitter; he was 55, with persistent money problems and no more poles to conquer. He had declared himself personally bankrupt. [D]
