History of Present Illness

Mr. Amundsen, a 55-year-old polar explorer, is seen at the request of Dr. Solheim-Achebe for burning pain and numbness in the fingers of both hands brought on by cold. The attacks have come every winter for many years and have been worse this winter, lasting up to an hour after he comes indoors, with the fingers turning white, then blue, then red. Between attacks the fingertips are slightly numb. There is no weakness, clumsiness apart from the cold, foot symptoms, neck pain, or night-time tingling that wakes him in warm weather. He had frostbite of the fingers of both hands in his early twenties. He has never smoked, drinks rarely, and takes no regular medicines. He drives, flies as a passenger and navigator, and would rather avoid tablets, and certainly any that make him drowsy. [S]

Assessment

  • Post-frostbite small-fiber neuropathy and cold-induced vasospasm of the fingers (M79.2). The history of frostbite, the fingertip distribution, the loss of pinprick and cold sensation with cold allodynia, the normal large-fiber function, and the cold-induced color change fit a late small-fiber neuropathy with vasospasm after frostbite, which is recognized as a long-term consequence. Normal nerve conduction studies exclude carpal and cubital tunnel syndromes and a large-fiber polyneuropathy, and the blood tests exclude diabetes, B12 deficiency, thyroid disease and kidney disease. No further tests are needed. Skin biopsy for small-fiber density would not change management. [S]
  • Treatment. He wishes to avoid oral treatment, and the pain is localized to the fingers, so capsaicin cream is reasonable first under NICE CG173. If it is not enough, duloxetine is the best of the first-line oral drugs for him, because amitriptyline, gabapentin and pregabalin are more likely to cause drowsiness; tramadol should be avoided, both because it is not for long-term use and because it interacts with duloxetine. [S]
  • Cold-induced vasospasm. Secondary to the old cold injury. No ulcers or signs of critical ischemia. Cold protection is the main treatment; a calcium-channel blocker would be considered only if attacks remained frequent and severe despite it. [S]

Plan of Treatment

  • Capsaicin 0.075% cream to the painful fingers four times a day, washing the hands after use; it may sting for the first week. [S]
  • If pain remains troublesome after four weeks, duloxetine 30 mg daily for a week, then 60 mg daily, with review of pain, adverse effects, mood, sleep and blood pressure, a sodium and liver enzyme check in the first weeks, no tramadol, and no abrupt stopping. [S]
  • Cold protection: liner gloves under mittens, heated gloves and hand warmers, warming the hands before fine work, no cold-water hand washing, and checking the skin for injury. [S]
  • No calcium-channel blocker for now; reconsider if attacks of vasospasm stay frequent and severe. [S]
  • Previously frostbitten fingers are more vulnerable to cold injury: in cold work, extra insulation, frequent checks, and early rewarming. [S]
  • No further tests; neurology review only if weakness, spreading sensory loss, or ulceration develops. [S]
