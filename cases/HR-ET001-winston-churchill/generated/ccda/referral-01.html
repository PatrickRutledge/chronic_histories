Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Winston Churchill was born on 30 November 1874 at Blenheim Palace, Oxfordshire, and died in London on 24 January 1965. He was Prime Minister from 1940 to 1945 and from 1951 to 1955, when poor health forced him to resign. [D]
  • In December 1941, during a visit to the White House, Churchill developed dull chest pain after opening a window with great effort. His physician interpreted it as a heart attack, but a cardiologist found no evidence of heart disease, and a later review judges the symptoms probably musculoskeletal. [R]
  • His most critical episode of pneumonia was in December 1943 in Tunis; it was treated with sulfonamides, which proved effective. [D]
  • Churchill had his first stroke in 1949, a less serious attack in August 1949 while staying at Cap d'Ail that his physician described as spasm of a small artery. He was seen on multiple occasions from 1950 to 1952 for further cerebrovascular episodes, including transient ischemic attacks. [D]
  • On the evening of 23 June 1953, while hosting a dinner at 10 Downing Street, Churchill had a stroke consisting of a left hemiparesis, with sagging of the left side of the mouth and impaired articulation. The deficit was greatest about three days after onset, then fluctuated and gradually recovered; the course is consistent with a small lacunar infarct. [D]
  • The 1953 stroke was concealed at the time: no member of the Cabinet noticed anything wrong at the dinner, and nothing about it was published until Churchill mentioned it about a year later. It has not been a secret since his physician's account was published in 1966. [D]
  • During the convalescence he was described as very despondent, with spells of apathy, and he wept readily when moved, for example by poetry. [D]
  • His physician diagnosed clinical depression, which became accepted lore as Churchill's "black dog"; the 2020 study by Vale and Scadding argues convincingly against this diagnosis. [R]
  • Smoking and alcohol were long-standing risk factors. A 1943 fluid chart listed champagne 10 oz, brandy 2 oz, and whisky and soda 8 oz, and accounts of the 1953 convalescence describe his usual cigar and whisky and soda. [D]
  • His father, Lord Randolph Churchill, died in 1895 aged 45 after a progressive illness with speech and hearing problems, dizziness, palpitations, and numbness of the hands and feet. Syphilis was long assumed; a brain tumor has been argued as more likely, and the cause cannot be determined with certainty. [R]
