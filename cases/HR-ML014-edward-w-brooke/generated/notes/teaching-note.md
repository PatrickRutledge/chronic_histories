# Teaching Note — Edward W. Brooke pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Edward William Brooke III was born in Washington, D.C., on October 26, 1919. Brooke was elected attorney general of Massachusetts in 1962 and re-elected in 1964, and in 1966 became the first African American elected to the U.S. Senate by popular vote; he served as a Republican senator from Massachusetts from January 3, 1967, to January 3, 1979. In a CNN interview aired on June 11, 2003, Brooke said that in September 2002 he felt discomfort in his right breast, which he dismissed as aging, possible muscle strain, or gas; that he then had a mammogram, a sonogram, and a needle biopsy, which found a malignant tumor; that his breasts had never before been examined by a doctor; and that he had not thought men got breast cancer. In the same interview Brooke said he had to be operated on for breast cancer, that the cancer had spread to his lymph nodes, and that all of the lymph nodes on his right side were removed. An Associated Press report of June 11, 2003, said that Brooke, 83, had been diagnosed with breast cancer in September, had a double mastectomy, and was free of the cancer, and that he was encouraging men to examine themselves and urging insurers to cover mammograms for men. Brooke said he kept the diagnosis private for several months and then, in June 2003, began speaking publicly about breast cancer in men, saying 'Men have breast tissue like women, and they can have breast cancer, and they do,' and urging men 'not to be so macho about this whole thing'; he called for national awareness, early detection, and insurance coverage of mammograms for men. In the 2003 interview Brooke described himself as not a smoker or a drinker and said he exercised and ate fairly good food. Brooke died at his home in Coral Gables, Florida, on January 3, 2015, aged 95; his former chief counsel said he died of natural causes.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
