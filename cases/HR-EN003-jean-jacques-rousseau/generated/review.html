Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Birth date and calendar. He was born at Geneva on June 28, 1712 (1911 Britannica). Geneva had followed the Gregorian calendar since its council's resolution of December 31, 1700 (source 12), so the date is Gregorian and is not converted. England had moved to the Gregorian calendar in 1752, so the dates of the Wootton stay are Gregorian as well.
  2. Index age and dates. He lived at Wootton from March 22, 1766, to May 1, 1767, and turned 54 in June 1766. The index visit on January 20, 2026 maps to January 20, 1767, at Wootton, after the quarrel and before his flight, when he was 54. The synthetic date of birth is June 28, 1971 (shift 259 years). The other visits (February 3, March 10, April 21, June 9, July 14, and September 22, 2026) map to the same days of 1767; the last four fall after his documented flight to France in May 1767. The true-frame dates lie within his lifetime and assert nothing about where he was; the modern episode does not reproduce the flight.
  3. Not naming the man who arranged his stay in England. That man has his own record in this dataset, so he is referred to only as a Scottish philosopher who arranged his stay in England, and the quarrel appears only as documented history.
  4. Persecutory beliefs. His beliefs and conduct (the conviction that his host was plotting against him, the fear of being spied on, the Dialogues, and the opening of the Reveries) are D, from his own writings and reference works. Calling them a delusion is R (possible), from the psychiatric tradition summarized in source 4, which also stresses that he had real enemies, his books were condemned, and his house was stoned. The modern episode gives him a synthetic diagnosis of delusional disorder, persecutory type, linked to both, and the notes acknowledge his real conflicts rather than treating every grievance as delusional.
  5. Modern diagnosis decided. Delusional disorder, persecutory type, rather than paranoid personality disorder (the beliefs are fixed and specific), schizophrenia (no hallucinations, disorganization, or negative symptoms), a mood disorder with psychotic features (mild depressive symptoms that followed the beliefs), or a medical or neurocognitive cause (normal examination, cognition, and tests over a long, stable course, so no brain imaging). The insomnia is recorded as part of the disorder rather than as a separate problem.
  6. Antipsychotic. Decided: aripiprazole 5 mg was offered at the consultation and declined; he declined again at the third visit, and chose it himself at the fourth visit, after urethroplasty, for sleep and worry. Aripiprazole was chosen for its low anticholinergic effect in a man with urinary retention. The outcome is a partial response, with less conviction and distress but persistent beliefs, which is realistic. No compulsory treatment was considered, because he was never at risk. The APA guideline was written for schizophrenia; its recommendations on antipsychotics, CBT for psychosis, and monitoring are applied to delusional disorder as a schizophrenia-spectrum condition, and the CMID depression and mood disorders monograph supplies the antipsychotic monitoring schedule.
  7. Urinary disease. His lifelong retention, self-treatment with bougies, the broken probe of 1761, and the 1758 rupture are D, from the Confessions and his letters as quoted. The cause is R (disputed): congenital narrowing, a penile stricture, a bladder-neck valve, psychogenic retention, and acute intermittent porphyria have all been proposed, and the autopsy found no lesion of the urinary tract (D). The modern episode gives him a synthetic 1.5 cm bulbar urethral stricture, idiopathic and probably congenital, treated by excision and primary anastomosis urethroplasty. This differs from the prostatic retention in another record in the dataset. The problem is linked to the documented and disputed claims, and its provenance says the stricture is not his historical diagnosis.
  8. Hypospadias not used. The brief for this record suggested a congenital anomaly such as hypospadias. No fetched source proposed hypospadias, so it is not asserted; the synthetic cystoscopy records a normal meatus, which teaches its exclusion on examination.
  9. Hernia. The documented "rupture" of 1758 does not give a site. The modern right inguinal hernia is synthetic and echoes it; it is managed by watchful waiting.
  10. Suicide history. Source 4 says he thought of suicide after the probe broke in 1761. This is recorded once, as past history in a sensitive claim, without detail, and the safety assessment counts it as a remote risk factor. The 1778 suicide rumors are recorded with the evidence against them (the certificate of apoplexy and the 1897 skull examination). No current suicidal or violent ideation is recorded at any visit.
  11. Children. His five children left at the foundling hospital are documented history from his own account (with the 1911 Britannica's doubt noted). They appear only in a claim and the timeline, with no health facts and no part in the modern episode.
  12. Real people. No real relative, companion, host, or physician is the source of any modern statement or decision. His long-term partner appears only as an unnamed member of his household; he declined permission for collateral history. Thérèse Levasseur, Richard Davenport, and the physicians of his time are named only in documented claims.
  13. Cause of death. His death is recorded as documented: sudden, at Ermenonville on July 2, 1778, with a certificate of apoplexy. Later suggestions of uremia or porphyria are not used.
  14. Not verified or not used. The Bibliothèque de Genève pages and Leo Damrosch's biography were not fetched, so nothing is taken from them. A 2017 article on a congenital stenosis of the posterior urethra was seen only in a search summary and is not cited. Peer-reviewed psychiatric papers on his paranoia were found only as titles without abstracts (for example a 1965 N Y State J Med paper and a 1951 Psychoanal Q paper) and are not cited.
  15. Sick and preventive visits. Two visits were added: an unscheduled sick visit on June 9, 2026 (true frame June 9, 1767) for an acute low back strain after splitting firewood, and an adult preventive visit on September 22, 2026 (true frame September 22, 1767), at 55.   16. Focused visits. The urine culture before surgery, the pathology, and the PHQ-9 scores recorded by the psychologist are reported in prose, not as observations.   17. Consultation. The psychiatry referral and the consultation by Ambrose Lindqvist-Hale, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the second visit, are S. The consultant answers the five referral questions: delusional disorder, persecutory type, with the differential reasoned out and no brain imaging; low risk of suicide and violence, with sudden flight, isolation, insomnia, and any wish to confront someone as the signs to watch; aripiprazole offered for its low anticholinergic effect and declined; CBT for psychosis accepted; and capacity to consent to surgery present. What happened afterwards agrees: CBT began the next week, he declined aripiprazole again at the third visit and chose it at the fourth, primary care started it without a further psychiatry visit, as the consultant allowed, and the monitoring schedule was followed apart from the recorded deviation.
  18. Rousseau died in 1778, so the recent-death review rule does not apply. Ordinary clinical, provenance, and safe-messaging reviews remain required.
