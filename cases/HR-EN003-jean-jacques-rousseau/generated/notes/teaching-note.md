# Teaching Note — Jean-Jacques Rousseau pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Jean-Jacques Rousseau was born in Geneva on June 28, 1712. Geneva had followed the Gregorian calendar since 1701, so the date needs no conversion. His mother, Suzanne Bernard, died nine days after his birth. In the Confessions he wrote that a defect in the bladder caused him an almost continual retention of urine in his early years; that he reached thirty almost without feeling this original infirmity, apart from frequent heats in the bladder; that burning and pain in the loins returned at Venice; and that after a hot summer of journeys on foot he had a violent nephritic colic, after which he never recovered his former health. In the Confessions he wrote that the surgeon Morand advised him to go to Daran, whose bougies were the only ones that gave him any relief; he bought a great number of them so that he would never be without, and used them often for eight or ten years. His letters show that he spoke of urinary retention from 1748, at 36. In 1761 a probe broke in his urethra and he feared it would cost him his life; he wrote that he could not stop using his probes for a single day and that he had carried a bladder disease since childhood. A surgeon, Brother Côme, succeeded in passing a probe and ruled out a stone. He described his trouble as a habitual state: he never passed urine with a full stream, the flow was never wholly stopped, and he judged that the stream grew thinner from year to year and would one day stop altogether. In 1758 he suffered frequent retentions together with a rupture, and passed the year in a state of languor that made him think he was near the end of his life. In a will dated January 29, 1763, he asked that his body be opened after death, to show that his urinary disorder did not come from a dissolute life. By his own account in the Confessions he had five children with Thérèse Levasseur, and all five were taken to the foundling hospital shortly after birth. His books were condemned in 1762; stones were thrown at his house at Môtiers in September 1765; and he came to England in January 1766 at the invitation of a Scottish philosopher. He lived at Wootton, in the parish of Ellastone in rural Staffordshire, from March 22, 1766, to May 1, 1767, in a house belonging to Richard Davenport, and wrote the first part of the Confessions there. In England he became wrongly convinced that the Scottish philosopher who had arranged his stay was at the center of a plot against him, and he feared that the servants were spying on him. The two men quarreled violently, and the 1911 Britannica called his letters of this time those of a lunatic. In May 1767 he fled from England to France, and he later lived in France for a time under the name Renou. From 1772 he wrote the Dialogues (Rousseau, Judge of Jean-Jacques), a work born of his sense of persecution and long read as evidence of growing paranoia; he meant to leave the manuscript on the high altar of Notre-Dame in Paris but found the chancel fenced off by a locked railing. His last work, the Reveries of the Solitary Walker (written 1776 to 1778), opens: "Me voici donc seul sur la terre" (So here I am, alone on earth), and says he has been in this strange position for more than fifteen years. The autopsy done the day after his death found no stone and no lesion of the kidneys, ureters, bladder, or urethra. He died suddenly at Ermenonville on July 2, 1778, less than two months after moving to the estate of the Marquis de Girardin; a local inquiry the next day certified death from apoplexy. Rumors of suicide circulated, but an examination of his skull in 1897 found no trace of a bullet wound. He distrusted the medicine of his time: in Emile he wrote that he would never call a physician for himself unless his life were in evident danger, and in the Confessions he wrote that the physicians he consulted gave him no relief and weakened him. In a letter he wrote that when snow and frost shut him in he split wood to make himself sweat twice a day, the only remedy that had ever given him the least relief.
- **R:** The French psychiatrists Sérieux and Capgras classed him among patients with a delusion of interpretation, and a 2023 history-of-medicine paper judges that by current criteria he had a paranoid delusion that appeared around the age of 45 and grew afterwards, while noting that he had real enemies and real persecution (his books condemned, and expulsion by the Swiss authorities). The cause of his urinary disorder is disputed. Proposed causes include a congenital narrowing of the urethra (Poncet and Leriche, and his own phrase, a defect of conformation), a stricture of the penile urethra (judged most probable in a 2023 paper), a muscular valve at the bladder neck (Mercier), urinary retention of psychological origin (Janet), and acute intermittent porphyria (Androutsos and Geroulanos, 2000).
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
