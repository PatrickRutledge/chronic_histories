# Teaching Note — Barbara Jordan pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Barbara Jordan was born on February 21, 1936, in Houston, Texas, the youngest of three daughters, and grew up in Houston's Fifth Ward. She graduated magna cum laude from Texas Southern University in 1956 and earned a law degree from Boston University in 1959. In 1966 she was elected to the Texas Senate, the first Black state senator since 1883. In 1972 she was elected to the U.S. House of Representatives from Houston, the first Black woman from a Southern state to serve in Congress. As a member of the House Judiciary Committee during the 1974 impeachment hearings she said, "My faith in the Constitution is whole, it is complete, it is total." In 1976 she gave the keynote address at the Democratic National Convention, the first woman and the first African American to do so. She did not seek a fourth term and left the House in 1979, downplaying reports about her poor health and citing her "internal compass." She became the Lyndon Baines Johnson Public Service Professor at the LBJ School of Public Affairs of the University of Texas at Austin, where she taught until the early 1990s. In her later years in the House she walked with a cane, which she attributed at the time to "a bum knee"; a disability-press account written after her death says this was the first adaptive equipment her multiple sclerosis required, and that she did not openly acknowledge the disease for years. It was later revealed that she had been diagnosed with multiple sclerosis around the time she left Congress. The year of the diagnosis is not stated in the obituaries and reference works consulted. She spoke from a wheelchair at the Democratic National Convention in 1988, and in 1992 she gave a second convention keynote address from a wheelchair, amid a lengthy battle with multiple sclerosis. Accounts differ on when her multiple sclerosis became public. A broadcaster's profile says it was announced after she left Congress; a disability-press article says her doctors made it public only after she nearly drowned in her pool during physical therapy, which it dates to 1994 (the Texas State Historical Association dates the near-drowning to 1988 and does not link it to the disease); a reference work says her obituaries explained much she had kept private, confirming the multiple sclerosis. Her obituaries reported that she had battled multiple sclerosis for several years and moved about in a wheelchair or with a walker. She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1994. She died in Austin on January 17, 1996, at 59, of pneumonia as a complication of leukemia, and is buried in the Texas State Cemetery.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
