Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Barbara Jordan was born on February 21, 1936, in Houston, Texas, the youngest of three daughters, and grew up in Houston's Fifth Ward. She graduated magna cum laude from Texas Southern University in 1956 and earned a law degree from Boston University in 1959. [D]
  • In 1966 she was elected to the Texas Senate, the first Black state senator since 1883. In 1972 she was elected to the U.S. House of Representatives from Houston, the first Black woman from a Southern state to serve in Congress.   • She did not seek a fourth term and left the House in 1979, downplaying reports about her poor health and citing her "internal compass." She became the Lyndon Baines Johnson Public Service Professor at the LBJ School of Public Affairs of the University of Texas at Austin, where she taught until the early 1990s. [D]
  • In her later years in the House she walked with a cane, which she attributed at the time to "a bum knee"; a disability-press account written after her death says this was the first adaptive equipment her multiple sclerosis required, and that she did not openly acknowledge the disease for years.   • It was later revealed that she had been diagnosed with multiple sclerosis around the time she left Congress. The year of the diagnosis is not stated in the obituaries and reference works consulted. [D]

Assessment

  • Active primary progressive multiple sclerosis (G35.B1). More than a year of gradually worsening disability without relapses, with T2 lesions in characteristic brain regions and two or more spinal cord lesions, meets the 2017 McDonald criteria for primary progressive multiple sclerosis without the need for spinal fluid. The enhancing lesion shows inflammatory activity, so the disease is active. B12, thyroid and HIV results and the absence of cord compression exclude the main mimics. At 44, still walking with a cane, with an enhancing lesion, she is the kind of patient most likely to benefit from therapy. [S]
  • Spastic gait and fall risk (R26.89). Spasticity contributes to her slowness and stiffness; strength is fairly preserved, so a careful baclofen titration should not weaken her. [S]
  • Neurogenic bladder (N31.9). Urgency with a low residual; safe to treat with a bladder-relaxing medicine, preferably one without anticholinergic effects on concentration. [S]

Plan of Treatment

  • Ocrelizumab, the therapy with evidence for primary progressive multiple sclerosis: 300 mg intravenously on day 1 and day 15, then 600 mg every 6 months, with methylprednisolone 100 mg, an antihistamine and acetaminophen beforehand and at least one hour of observation; twice-yearly infusions suit her teaching and travel. [S]
  • Before the first dose: she is not infected with hepatitis B but not immune, so hepatitis B vaccine; PCV20, recombinant zoster vaccine (two doses, the second after about a month), and Tdap; inactivated vaccines at least two weeks before the first dose and live vaccines at least four weeks before (none needed, as she is immune to varicella and measles); confirm no active infection on each infusion day. [S]
  • Monitoring: blood count with lymphocytes, liver tests and immunoglobulins before each dose; hepatitis B surface antibody after the series; prompt review of infections, persistent diarrhea or new neurological symptoms; standard breast cancer screening; no live vaccines during treatment; seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccines, ideally at least two weeks before an infusion. [S]
  • MRI: a re-baseline brain MRI three to six months after the first dose, then yearly, on the same scanner where possible and without routine gadolinium; spinal MRI only for new spinal symptoms. [S]
