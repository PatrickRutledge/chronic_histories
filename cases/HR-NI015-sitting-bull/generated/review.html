Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Sources. The case relies on the National Park Service, the Encyclopedia of the Great Plains, North Dakota Studies (the State Historical Society of North Dakota), Sitting Bull College (a tribal college at Standing Rock), a historian's article in American History, the Montana Cowboy Hall of Fame, and, for the 1856 date only, a reader's notes on Robert Utley's biography; the MPR archive is used only for the Lakota spelling. Utley's book and Nathaniel Philbrick's The Last Stand were not read. Britannica and the Smithsonian's National Anthropological Archives (which hold his pictographic autobiography) could not be fetched. Popular and fan sites found in searches were not used. No Lakota-authored biography was read; a Lakota-authored source was used only for the spelling of his name.
  2. Recent-death rule. He died in 1890, so the rule does not apply. A sick visit is included.
  3. Birth date. No record survives. The National Park Service and North Dakota Studies give 1831 to 1837, the Encyclopedia of the Great Plains the early 1830s, Sitting Bull College 1834, and the roster and the Montana Cowboy Hall of Fame 1831. The preferred birth is about 1831 (precision approximate), with 1834 kept as an unresolved alternative. Because the month and day are unknown, the synthetic date of birth uses July 1 as a placeholder: 1971-07-01, a shift of 140 years. The true-date variant therefore places his birth on July 1, 1831, which is a modeling artifact, not a claim. With a birth in 1834 he would have been about 52 in 1886, not 55.
  4. Index age and dates. The index visit on July 6, 2026 maps to July 6, 1886, when he was about 55 and living at his cabin on the Grand River at Standing Rock, the year after his 1885 tour and before the 1889 land loss. The visits on July 13, August 10, August 24, September 14, September 21 and October 26, 2026 map to the same days of 1886. His day-to-day whereabouts in 1886 are not documented in the sources read; nothing places him away from Standing Rock then. Past medical history includes only events up to 1883.
  5. Name. He is known as Sitting Bull; his Lakota name is Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake in the modern orthography (MPR archive), written Tatanka Iyotake by Sitting Bull College and Tatanka-Iyotanka by the National Park Service. He had no family name in the Western sense. identity.name_parts is used so that the C-CDA and FHIR names are not split into a given name “Sitting” and a family name “Bull”: his single name is placed in the family-name field (the usual practice for a person with one name) with no given name (the C-CDA carries the required given-name element as a null flavor, and the FHIR name has no given part); the FHIR name text and the display name are “Sitting Bull.” The notes call him Sitting Bull, as he asked in the modern episode, not “Mr. Bull.” The aliases are Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake and Tatanka Iyotake, which appear in no other record. His boyhood names (Jumping Badger, Slow) are in a claim, not aliases.
  6. Holy man. He is described as a holy man, a wichasha wakan (CLM-0004), in the historian's spelling, with the modern orthography wičháša wakȟáŋ added; the orthography was not checked against a Lakota language source. “Medicine man” is not used, following the historian's note that the equation is misleading; Sitting Bull College's use of the term is recorded only in this list of sources.
  7. Ceremony. The 1876 Sun Dance is documented (CLM-0007) and recorded only as religious history. It is not self-injury and not a medical event, and it is not coded or examined. No Lakota ceremony is described in the modern episode: traditional healing is offered through the clinic's traditional medicine program, at his choice, and the clinic asks nothing about his practices, following IHS policy. Ceremonial tobacco is not asked about; only commercial tobacco is.
  8. Foot wound. That a Crow warrior shot him in the foot and that he limped for the rest of his life comes from the Montana Cowboy Hall of Fame (no references), and the 1856 date and the shot glancing off his shield and cutting along the foot from heel to toe come from a reader's notes on Utley's biography (CLM-0005, D, probable). The left side was not stated in any source read; the roster brief and popular accounts give the left foot, and the modern episode uses the left foot (S). Other wounds: a wound through the hip and back in 1864 (CLM-0006, D, probable). The roster's “multiple combat wounds” is limited to these two.
  9. Retained fragments and lead. No source documents retained metal. The synthetic CT finds four small fragments in the soft tissue of the heel, outside any joint or bone, consistent with a wound that ran along the sole; a single blood lead (2.4 ug/dL, below the 3.5 ug/dL reference value used here) is checked because fragments were found, and the fragments are left in place, with repeat testing only for symptoms or migration into a joint, in line with the systematic review (higher lead with fractures and multiple fragments). The point-of-care test gives a same-day result.
  10. Diagnosis and presenting problem. Chronic left ankle and hindfoot pain with a limp (M25.572) is the presenting problem, resolved at the consultation when post-traumatic osteoarthritis of the subtalar and talonavicular joints (M19.172) and the retained fragments (Z18.12) were recorded. The raised blood pressure reading at intake (R03.0) resolved at the second visit, when home readings averaged 124/78 mmHg: no hypertension.
  11. Displacement and historical trauma. The pursuit of the bands, exile and hunger in Canada, the starving surrender, imprisonment at Fort Randall, the loss of the buffalo and reservation farming are documented (CLM-0008 to CLM-0010) and recorded as history and social history, not as symptoms. No trauma diagnosis is made; the PHQ-9 (6, then 4 and 3) is the only screening instrument, and his grief and anger are recorded as understandable responses to real losses. No safety assessment is required (no F-code, PHQ-9 below 10, PHQ-2 below 3).
  12. Food. The roster's commodity-ration diet is anchored to the documented loss of the buffalo and farming at Standing Rock (CLM-0010); the modern food insecurity, commodity diet and prediabetes (hemoglobin A1c 6.0%, then 5.8%) are synthetic. The Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations, which offers bison and other traditional foods, the Special Diabetes Program for Indians and the Community Health Representative program are real programs.
  13. Care setting. The project facility name (St. Posthumous Health Center) is kept; in the notes it is a clinic serving the Standing Rock Reservation, with a Lakota-speaking interpreter, a traditional medicine program and a visiting foot and ankle surgeon. These are fictional features of a fictional clinic.
  14. Clinician name. The assigned primary clinician name “Wenona Redcloud-Hastings, MD” was changed to “Wenona Talltree-Hastings, MD,” as the assignment allowed: “Red Cloud” appears in no other record, but it is the name of a real Lakota leader of the same era, and a fictional clinician should not carry it in this record. “Talltree” appears in no other record.
  15. Relatives and others. No relative is named in this record, and no modern statement, decision or health fact is attributed to any relative or historical person. His modern household, the interpreter, the orthotist, the physical therapist, the Community Health Representative and his health care agent are synthetic and unnamed. The Indian agent and the performer Cody appear only in claims. The other performers who toured with him are not named.
  16. Synthetic clinical picture. Everything in the modern episode is synthetic: the pain history, every score (pain rating, PHQ-2, PHQ-9; the Foot and Ankle Ability Measure is narrative), all laboratory values, the X-rays, CT and injection, the blood lead, the H. pylori, HIV, hepatitis and stool test results (narrative), the medicines, the missed therapy sessions and the outcomes. Height (178 cm) and weights are synthetic. The triamcinolone and lidocaine for the injection are recorded in the procedure, not as medication entries, because the generators have no intra-articular route.
  17. Clinical judgment calls. (1) The ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline and the CMID osteoarthritis monograph, written for the hand, hip and knee, are extrapolated to the hindfoot. (2) Topical diclofenac first, then a six-week naproxen course with food after normal kidney function and home blood pressure, stopped for dyspepsia. (3) No opioids, following IHS policy. (4) Fragments left in place with one normal blood lead. (5) An ultrasound-guided subtalar injection at five weeks, with a warning about a brief rise in glucose in prediabetes; a second injection booked no sooner than three months later. (6) Arthrodesis explained and declined by him; his decision is respected and the option left open. (7) No metformin for prediabetes; lifestyle and traditional foods through the prevention program.
  18. Preventive visit. A periodic adult preventive visit at about 55 (CPT 99396 and 99497 in plain text). Judgment calls: HIV, hepatitis C and hepatitis B screening (negative; not immune to hepatitis B); a stool immunochemical test (negative); PSA declined after shared decision-making; lung cancer and abdominal aortic aneurysm screening not indicated; PREVENT 10-year risk about 5 percent, no statin; influenza, PCV20 and the first zoster dose, with Tdap and the first hepatitis B dose at the sixteen-week visit at his choice; COVID-19 vaccine declined for now; advance care planning with an unnamed agent, recording that his own spiritual practices are to be respected as he directs, without describing them.
  19. Sick visit. Epigastric burning six weeks into naproxen, chosen because it follows from the plan and insinuates no undocumented diagnosis: no alarm features, an unchanged hemoglobin, an H. pylori stool test (negative), naproxen stopped, four weeks of omeprazole, and acetaminophen added; under the ACG and CAG guideline no endoscopy is needed. It is not a historical claim.
  20. Focused visits. The four chronic-care follow-ups (one week with the consultation, five weeks with the injection, ten weeks, and sixteen weeks) are documented as focused visits on the ACR/Arthritis Foundation guideline, the CMID osteoarthritis and depression monographs, the retained-bullet lead review, the IHS pain and traditional medicine policies and the USPSTF diabetes and hypertension screening recommendations. One deviation is recorded instead of invented results: two of the first four weekly physical therapy sessions were missed because he had no ride to a clinic an hour away; no harm resulted (he kept up the home exercises and improved), and the corrective action was rides through the Community Health Representative program and video sessions. Oral NSAID monitoring became not indicated when naproxen was stopped, and repeat lead testing and imaging are recorded as not indicated, with reasons. Synthetic findings introduced: the missed sessions, the dyspepsia, the response to the injection and its partial wearing off by sixteen weeks.
  21. Consultation. The orthopaedic foot and ankle referral and the consultation by Garrett Holmquist-Ellery, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the one-week visit, are S. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: post-traumatic arthritis of the subtalar and talonavicular joints without infection; fragments left in place with a low blood lead; a rigid ankle-foot orthosis, six weeks of therapy, topical diclofenac, a short naproxen course and a subtalar injection if pain stays above 4 of 10 (given at five weeks); arthrodesis explained as his choice (declined at ten weeks); and care kept close to home with video therapy and transport help.
