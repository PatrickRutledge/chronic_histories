Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Sitting Bull was born in the early 1830s, between 1831 and 1837 by the reckoning of different sources, into a prominent Hunkpapa Lakota family near the Grand River in present-day South Dakota, at a place called Many Caches. [D]
  • His boyhood name was Jumping Badger, and he was called Slow for his deliberate ways; as a young man he received his father's name, Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake (Sitting Bull), also written Tatanka Iyotake or Tatanka-Iyotanka. [D]
  • He became a leading Hunkpapa warrior, hunter and leader, admitted to respected warrior societies, and by about 1866 or 1867 was recognized as an important political and military leader of the Lakota bands resisting encroachment on their lands. [D]
  • He was also a holy man, a wichasha wakan (in the modern Lakota orthography wičháša wakȟáŋ), whose calling was confirmed in the Sun Dance; historians note that equating a wichasha wakan with a medicine man is misleading. [D]
  • In a fight with Crow warriors, about 1856 by his biographer's account, he was shot in the foot: the Crow chief's shot struck his shield and then cut a groove along his foot from heel to toe. The wound troubled him for the rest of his life and left him with a limp. [D]
  • In 1864 he was again wounded, through the hip and back, in an attack on a wagon train crossing Lakota territory. [D]
  • In June 1876, before the battle at the Little Bighorn (the Greasy Grass), he led a Sun Dance on Rosebud Creek, offering fifty pieces of flesh from each arm and fasting, and had a vision of soldiers falling into the camp; his village was attacked later that month, and he, beyond fighting age, did not fight himself. [D]
  • After the battle the army pursued the Lakota bands, and in 1877 he led his people north into Canada, where the buffalo were scarce, food was short, sickness spread and the winters were hard. [D]
  • Starving, he and the remaining Hunkpapa surrendered at Fort Buford, Dakota Territory, on July 20, 1881; despite promises that they would go to Standing Rock, he and his followers were held as prisoners of war at Fort Randall for nearly two years. [D]
  • In May 1883 he was allowed to join his people at the Standing Rock Reservation, where there were too few bison to support the Hunkpapa, he was sent to work in the farm fields, and he chose to live in a cabin on the Grand River near where he was born. [D]
  • He toured with Wild West shows in 1884 and 1885, spending four months of 1885 with William F. Cody's show, and then returned to Standing Rock, where the agent would not let him rejoin the show. [D]
