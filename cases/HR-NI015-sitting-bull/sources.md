## Historical evidence

1. National Park Service, Little Bighorn Battlefield National Monument. “Sitting Bull.” Supports his birth “into a prominent Hunkpapa Lakota family between the years of 1831-1837” near the Grand and Missouri Rivers; that he “would bear his father's name and was known as Tatanka-Iyotanka”; that he was “known as a spiritual leader”; the mid-June 1876 Sun Dance in which he “had 50 pieces of flesh sliced away from each arm and went without water for two days and two nights”; that, “beyond fighting age,” he did not take part in the combat at the Little Bighorn; the move to Canada in the fall of 1877, where “the buffalo were scarce, his people were plagued with sickness, and the winters were harsh”; the surrender at Fort Buford on July 20, 1881; the 1885 season with Cody's show, after which the agent refused to let him return; and his death on December 15, 1890, when “more than 40 Lakota Indian Police were sent to Sitting Bull's cabin” and he was “killed by a shot to the chest.” https://www.nps.gov/libi/learn/historyculture/sitting-bull.htm
2. Barrett CA (University of Mary). “Sitting Bull (ca. 1831-1890).” *Encyclopedia of the Great Plains* (University of Nebraska-Lincoln). Supports his birth in the early 1830s near Many Caches by the Grand River; his recognition “in 1866 or 1867” as “an important political and military leader”; the 1876 Sun Dance vision; refuge in Saskatchewan in 1877 where food was scarce; the 1881 surrender at Fort Buford and detention at Fort Randall despite promises of Standing Rock; that “in May 1883 Sitting Bull was permitted to join his people at Standing Rock”; Wild West tours in 1884 and 1885; that “the land was lost in 1889”; and his death in a fight at dawn on December 15, 1890, which also left followers and Indian police dead or dying. https://plainshumanities.unl.edu/encyclopedia/doc/egp.na.109.html
3. State Historical Society of North Dakota, North Dakota Studies. “Section 2: The Life of Tatanka Iyotanke, the Hunkpapa Leader known as Sitting Bull.” Supports his birth “sometime between 1831 and 1837 at a place the Dakotas called Many Caches”; his boyhood name Jumping Badger and the name Slow; the Greasy Grass; crossing into Canada in May 1877 and staying until July 1881; that, “starving, the Hunkpapas surrendered at Fort Buford”; two years at Fort Randall; that at Standing Rock “there were too few bison to support the Hunkpapas” and “he was sent to work in the farm fields”; four months with Cody's show; and his death at his cabin on the morning of December 15, 1890, when the Indian police came to arrest him. https://www.ndstudies.gov/gr8/node/132
4. Sitting Bull College (Standing Rock). “Who Was Sitting Bull?” Supports the name “Tatanka Iyotake,” a birth on the Grand River in 1834, two years at Fort Randall, the return to Standing Rock in 1883, and that on December 15, 1890, “Sitting Bull and seven of his followers were killed as well as six Indian police.” It also calls him a medicine man; that term is not used in this record except as quoted. https://sittingbull.edu/about/history/who-was-sitting-bull/
5. West E. “Sitting Bull: Soothsayer.” *American History*, August 2011, reproduced by HistoryNet. Supports his role as “an extraordinarily gifted wichasha wakan, or holy man,” that his calling “had been confirmed in the Lakotas' most important ceremony, the sun dance,” that a wichasha wakan “was often equated, a bit misleadingly, with a medicine man,” and the 1876 Sun Dance on Rosebud Creek. https://historynet.com/sitting-bull-soothsayer/
6. Montana Cowboy Hall of Fame and Western Heritage Center. “Sitting Bull, Tatanka-Iyotanka (1831-1890),” 2008 inductee biography. Supports that he “was wounded in combat by a Crow warrior, who shot him in the foot,” and that the wound “caused him to limp for the rest of his life”; the 1864 wound “through the hip and back” in the attack on the Fisk wagon train; nearly two years at Fort Randall as prisoners of war; four months with the show in 1885; and his cabin “on the Grand River very near where he was born.” The page has no references. https://montanacowboyfame.org/inductees/2008/1/sitting-bull
7. Viall JJ. “Notes on Sitting Bull and the Sioux,” *A Teacher on Teaching* (March 2018): a reader's notes on Robert M. Utley's *The Lance and the Shield* (1993). Used only for the 1856 fight with a Crow chief, whose shot “struck SB's shield, then ranged downward, cutting a groove in his foot from heel to toe,” a wound that “bothered SB the rest of his life.” https://ateacheronteaching.blogspot.com/2018/03/notes-on-sitting-bull-and-sioux.html
8. Minnesota Public Radio archive. “On Being: Reimagining Sitting Bull - Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake” (December 5, 2010). Used only for the spelling of his name in the modern Lakota orthography. https://archive.mpr.org/profiles/ernie-lapointe
9. HISTMED Master Roster v3. Internal project source; not treated as independent corroboration.

## Editorial resolutions

- **Sources.** The case relies on the National Park Service, the Encyclopedia of the Great Plains, North Dakota Studies (the State Historical Society of North Dakota), Sitting Bull College (a tribal college at Standing Rock), a historian's article in *American History*, the Montana Cowboy Hall of Fame, and, for the 1856 date only, a reader's notes on Robert Utley's biography; the MPR archive is used only for the Lakota spelling. Utley's book and Nathaniel Philbrick's *The Last Stand* were not read. Britannica and the Smithsonian's National Anthropological Archives (which hold his pictographic autobiography) could not be fetched. Popular and fan sites found in searches were not used. No Lakota-authored biography was read; a Lakota-authored source was used only for the spelling of his name.
- **Recent-death rule.** He died in 1890, so the rule does not apply. A sick visit is included.
- **Birth date.** No record survives. The National Park Service and North Dakota Studies give 1831 to 1837, the Encyclopedia of the Great Plains the early 1830s, Sitting Bull College 1834, and the roster and the Montana Cowboy Hall of Fame 1831. The preferred birth is about 1831 (precision approximate), with 1834 kept as an unresolved alternative. Because the month and day are unknown, the synthetic date of birth uses July 1 as a placeholder: 1971-07-01, a shift of 140 years. The true-date variant therefore places his birth on July 1, 1831, which is a modeling artifact, not a claim. With a birth in 1834 he would have been about 52 in 1886, not 55.
- **Index age and dates.** The index visit on July 6, 2026 maps to July 6, 1886, when he was about 55 and living at his cabin on the Grand River at Standing Rock, the year after his 1885 tour and before the 1889 land loss. The visits on July 13, August 10, August 24, September 14, September 21 and October 26, 2026 map to the same days of 1886. His day-to-day whereabouts in 1886 are not documented in the sources read; nothing places him away from Standing Rock then. Past medical history includes only events up to 1883.
- **Name.** He is known as Sitting Bull; his Lakota name is Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake in the modern orthography (MPR archive), written Tatanka Iyotake by Sitting Bull College and Tatanka-Iyotanka by the National Park Service. He had no family name in the Western sense. identity.name_parts is used so that the C-CDA and FHIR names are not split into a given name “Sitting” and a family name “Bull”: his single name is placed in the family-name field (the usual practice for a person with one name) with no given name (the C-CDA carries the required given-name element as a null flavor, and the FHIR name has no given part); the FHIR name text and the display name are “Sitting Bull.” The notes call him Sitting Bull, as he asked in the modern episode, not “Mr. Bull.” The aliases are Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake and Tatanka Iyotake, which appear in no other record. His boyhood names (Jumping Badger, Slow) are in a claim, not aliases.
- **Holy man.** He is described as a holy man, a wichasha wakan (CLM-0004), in the historian's spelling, with the modern orthography wičháša wakȟáŋ added; the orthography was not checked against a Lakota language source. “Medicine man” is not used, following the historian's note that the equation is misleading; Sitting Bull College's use of the term is recorded only in this list of sources.
- **Ceremony.** The 1876 Sun Dance is documented (CLM-0007) and recorded only as religious history. It is not self-injury and not a medical event, and it is not coded or examined. No Lakota ceremony is described in the modern episode: traditional healing is offered through the clinic's traditional medicine program, at his choice, and the clinic asks nothing about his practices, following IHS policy. Ceremonial tobacco is not asked about; only commercial tobacco is.
- **Foot wound.** That a Crow warrior shot him in the foot and that he limped for the rest of his life comes from the Montana Cowboy Hall of Fame (no references), and the 1856 date and the shot glancing off his shield and cutting along the foot from heel to toe come from a reader's notes on Utley's biography (CLM-0005, D, probable). The left side was not stated in any source read; the roster brief and popular accounts give the left foot, and the modern episode uses the left foot (S). Other wounds: a wound through the hip and back in 1864 (CLM-0006, D, probable). The roster's “multiple combat wounds” is limited to these two.
- **Retained fragments and lead.** No source documents retained metal. The synthetic CT finds four small fragments in the soft tissue of the heel, outside any joint or bone, consistent with a wound that ran along the sole; a single blood lead (2.4 ug/dL, below the 3.5 ug/dL reference value used here) is checked because fragments were found, and the fragments are left in place, with repeat testing only for symptoms or migration into a joint, in line with the systematic review (higher lead with fractures and multiple fragments). The point-of-care test gives a same-day result.
- **Diagnosis and presenting problem.** Chronic left ankle and hindfoot pain with a limp (M25.572) is the presenting problem, resolved at the consultation when post-traumatic osteoarthritis of the subtalar and talonavicular joints (M19.172) and the retained fragments (Z18.12) were recorded. The raised blood pressure reading at intake (R03.0) resolved at the second visit, when home readings averaged 124/78 mmHg: no hypertension.
- **Displacement and historical trauma.** The pursuit of the bands, exile and hunger in Canada, the starving surrender, imprisonment at Fort Randall, the loss of the buffalo and reservation farming are documented (CLM-0008 to CLM-0010) and recorded as history and social history, not as symptoms. No trauma diagnosis is made; the PHQ-9 (6, then 4 and 3) is the only screening instrument, and his grief and anger are recorded as understandable responses to real losses. No safety assessment is required (no F-code, PHQ-9 below 10, PHQ-2 below 3).
- **Food.** The roster's commodity-ration diet is anchored to the documented loss of the buffalo and farming at Standing Rock (CLM-0010); the modern food insecurity, commodity diet and prediabetes (hemoglobin A1c 6.0%, then 5.8%) are synthetic. The Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations, which offers bison and other traditional foods, the Special Diabetes Program for Indians and the Community Health Representative program are real programs.
