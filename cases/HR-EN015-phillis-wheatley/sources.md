## Historical evidence

1. Massachusetts Historical Society. Letter from Phillis Wheatley to Obour Tanner, 30 October 1773 (Collections Online, with the society's transcription). In her own words: “I am at present indispos’d by a cold. & Since my arrival have been visited by the Asthma,” and “I can’t say but my voyage to England has conduced to the recovery (in a great measure) of my Health.” https://www.masshist.org/database/774
2. Massachusetts Historical Society. “Precious Cargo: Phillis Wheatley’s *Poems* and the Boston Tea Party” (Object of the Month, October 2023), presenting her letter to David Wooster of 18 October 1773. Supports the Boston newspaper notice of her sailing in May 1773 with Nathaniel Wheatley; that the trip was “partly to regain her fragile health, but more importantly to shepherd her book of poetry through to publication”; the failed Boston proposal of the year before; her reception in London; her return in September; her words “Since my return to America my Master, has at the desire of my friends in England given me my freedom”; her marriage to John Peters in 1778; that, “having lost two children already,” she and her young daughter died in a Boston boardinghouse in December 1784, her husband probably in prison for debt (citing Vincent Carretta’s biography); and the *Independent Chronicle* notice of the death of “Phillis Peters formerly Phillis Wheatley aged 31.” https://www.masshist.org/object-of-the-month/objects/october-2023
3. Poetry Foundation. “Phillis Wheatley” (biography). Supports her seizure from Senegal/Gambia at about seven; her purchase in August 1761 as “a slender, frail female child” whom the captain believed to be dying, “evidently suffering from a change of climate,” judged about seven from “shedding her front teeth”; her education and first poem (Newport *Mercury*, 21 December 1767); the Whitefield elegy; the failed Boston subscription of 1772; that she left for London “suffering from a chronic asthma condition” (misdated there to 1771); the publication of *Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral* (1773), “the first volume of poetry by an African American published in modern times”; manumission “some three months before Mrs. Wheatley died on March 3, 1774”; her marriage to John Peters on April 1, 1778; poverty and work as a charwoman; disputed evidence of up to three children, the last buried with her; and her death on December 5, 1784. https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poets/phillis-wheatley
4. Michals D. “Phillis Wheatley.” National Women’s History Museum, 2015. Supports her birth “around 1753 in the nations of Senegal and the Gambia”; that her early life before being kidnapped and trafficked in 1761 is unknown; her naming after the ship and the family; her education (“Within sixteen months … she could read the Bible, Greek and Latin classics, and British literature”); the Countess of Huntingdon’s support; her marriage in 1778 to John Peters, “a free Black man from Boston”; three children, “though none survived”; work as a maid in a boardinghouse; and her death on December 5, 1784. https://www.womenshistory.org/education-resources/biographies/phillis-wheatley
5. Bynum TA. “Phillis Wheatley Peters, A Chronology.” Society of Early Americanists, 2020. Supports her birth in the Senegambia region in 1753/54; her arrival in Boston on 11 July 1761 aboard the slave ship *Phillis*; her first published poem; the May 1773 departure with Nathaniel Wheatley; the London publication in September 1773; the Boston report of her return on 16 September; her freedom on her return; and her death on 5 December 1784. https://www.societyofearlyamericanists.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/Wheatley-Chronology.pdf
6. Baytop A. “Wheatley, Phillis.” *American Women Writers* (Gale), via Encyclopedia.com. Supports “Susannah Wheatley … bought the frail, asthmatic child”; three children, two dead by 1783 and the third dying “a few hours after her own death”; and her poverty after marriage. https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/wheatley-phillis
7. HISTMED Master Roster v3. Internal project source. Its entries were provisional and were not treated as independent corroboration.

## Editorial resolutions

- **Recent-death rule.** She died in 1784, so the rule does not apply. The claims are still limited to what the fetched sources state.
- **Sources not read.** Vincent Carretta’s biography was not read directly; it is cited only as quoted by the Massachusetts Historical Society. Britannica, the Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture and the Library of Congress pages were not reached (blocked or not found) and are not cited. An Encyclopedia.com entry said, in a search snippet, that she was troubled by “asthma and tuberculosis” in the winter of 1772; it was not fetched, and tuberculosis is not claimed. A search snippet that a family doctor advised a sea voyage was not verified and is left out.
- **Corrections to the brief.** The brief said she died “reportedly soon after childbirth.” No fetched source says so: the sources say only that she became ill and died, and that her last surviving child died at about the same time and was buried with her. Childbirth is left out of the claims and the cause of death is recorded as not stated. The sources also disagree about her children (three, none surviving; a young daughter; a son buried with her; the third dying a few hours after her), and the record states the disagreement. The brief’s “freed in 1773 or 1774” is resolved by her own letter of 18 October 1773, which says she had been freed since her return; the Poetry Foundation’s later dating is recorded as disputing it. The Poetry Foundation dates the London departure to 1771; the Boston newspaper notice and the chronology give May 1773, which is used.
- **Other figures.** The brief allowed naming one American statesman with a record in the dataset (HR-EN004), whom she met in London, only if a documented claim needed him. No claim does, so he is not named and `historical_mentions` is not used. Other prominent people she met or corresponded with, some of whom have records in the dataset, are not named.
- **Her enslavers.** John and Susanna Wheatley and their son Nathaniel are named only in the documented history, as the family who bought her and as her companion on the voyage. Nothing is said of their health; the sources’ account of why she cut her London stay short is left out because no claim needs it. Susanna Wheatley’s date of death appears once, only to date the manumission. The modern episode gives no decision, statement or health fact to any member of the household.
- **Birth date and synthetic date of birth.** No birth record exists. She was born about 1753 (the chronology gives 1753/54). The preferred birth is 1753 with precision “approximate,” and 1754 is an unresolved alternative. Because the month and day are unknown, the synthetic date of birth uses July 1, a placeholder, and is 2006-07-01; the shift is 253 years, so the true-date variant places her birth on July 1, 1753, a modeling artifact, not a claim.
- **Index age and dates.** The index visit on February 9, 2026 maps to February 9, 1773, when she was about 19, in Boston, before she sailed in May 1773, as the brief asked. The visits map to February 9, February 23, April 6 and April 27, 1773 (all in Boston before the voyage), September 21, 1773 (eight days after the newspapers reported her return), and October 26, 1773 (after her letter of 18 October saying she was free). None falls during her documented absence. The visits are not a claim about her real care.
- **Her legal status at the index date.** At the true-frame index date she was enslaved. A 2026 record cannot show slavery as a legal status, so the modern episode renders it truthfully as domestic servitude: a young woman brought to Boston as a child by traffickers, living and working without wages in the household that acquired her, without her own papers, with the household deciding her travel. No abuse beyond the documented is invented; asked privately, she reports no physical harm. Following the ACOG opinion on human trafficking, the clinic sees her alone, gives her information only to her, takes no instructions about her care from the household, offers social work and written resources, and respects her adult decisions; she declines further help and says she hopes to win her freedom in her own way. At the last visit she reports her freedom, as her letter of October 1773 does. This is a judgment call for the owner’s review; no diagnosis code is used for it.
- **Asthma (D, probable, and R).** Her description as “the frail, asthmatic child” rests on one reference work (CLM-0003, D, probable); the Massachusetts Historical Society and the Poetry Foundation document her fragile health and chronic asthma when she sailed (CLM-0006), and her own letter documents asthma with a cold after her return and her improvement abroad (CLM-0008, D, confirmed). Whether this was asthma as now defined is retrospective (CLM-0013, R, possible). The modern asthma (J45.30) is linked both_with_link to these claims; its severity, lung function, FeNO, IgE and triggers are synthetic.
- **Thin chart (rule 26).** Her documented health history is thin: frailty and the label of asthma in childhood, fragile health and asthma around the voyage, a cold and asthma after her return, and death in poverty at about 31 of a cause not recorded. Nothing else is padded in. The modern episode is built around asthma in a young woman, which fits what is documented, with preventive care around it.
- **Presenting problem.** Wheeze, cough and breathlessness (R06.2) are resolved at the diagnosis visit on February 23, 2026, when asthma is recorded. The tuberculosis test, pregnancy test and chest X-ray at intake exclude other causes; the intake documents state only what was known then.
- **Treatment choices.** GINA track 1, step 2 (as-needed low-dose budesonide-formoterol, no albuterol-only reliever) at diagnosis, for symptoms on several days a week with waking twice a month; a standby five-day prednisone course for the long sea journey, with written start rules, never used and stopped at the last visit; meclizine for seasickness; a step to maintenance and reliever (step 3) after the exacerbation, through the winter. These are clinical judgment calls for review.
- **Sick visit.** A mild-to-moderate asthma exacerbation with a cold eight days after her return (J45.31), anchored to her own letter (“indispos’d by a cold. & Since my arrival have been visited by the Asthma”). It was managed without an oral corticosteroid because she responded in clinic and her peak flow stayed above 60 percent of her best. The clinical details are synthetic.
- **Preventive visit.** A periodic preventive visit at 19 before the journey (CPT 99395, 99497 and vaccine codes in plain text): PHQ-9 4 and GAD-7 3 (below thresholds; no safety assessment needed); hepatitis B screening because she was born in a region where it is common, with HIV and hepatitis C; latent tuberculosis already excluded; HPV (three-dose catch-up) and hepatitis B vaccination started; contraception and pregnancy planning on her terms (no method wanted; all methods suitable with asthma; budesonide preferred and the controller continued in pregnancy; folic acid by her choice); cervical screening not yet due; chlamydia screening not indicated; voluntary advance care planning with an unnamed agent she chose from outside the household. Her school immunization record is synthetic.
- **Focused visits.** The three chronic-care follow-ups (diagnosis and consultation, six weeks with travel planning, and five weeks after the exacerbation) are documented as focused visits on GINA 2025, the 2020 NAEPP update, the CMID asthma monograph and the ATS race-neutral statement. One protocol deviation is recorded instead of an invented result: the review within a week of the exacerbation was missed because she did not attend (the nurse telephoned on day two); no harm, since she recovered without an oral corticosteroid and her lung function was normal five weeks later; corrective action: a telephone or video review on day five to seven when an in-person visit is not kept. The repeat spirometry “three to six months after starting” was due during her absence and was done on her return.
- **Consultation.** The pulmonology referral and consultation by Gideon Fairbanks-Mensah, MD (fictional), on the diagnosis day, are **S**. The consultant answers six questions consistently with what followed: asthma confirmed and tuberculosis excluded; race-neutral interpretation; GINA track 1 step 2; type 2 phenotype with cold air, smoke, colds and dust mite as triggers; the travel plan with a standby course; and pregnancy counselling.
- **Synthetic clinical picture.** All visits, results, lung function, FeNO, IgE, peak flows, her height (152 cm) and weights, the clinic social worker’s number (555-0142), and every medicine and vaccine are synthetic. Qualitative results (interferon-gamma release assay, pregnancy test, hepatitis B, HIV and hepatitis C screening, specific IgE, rapid viral tests) are in note text.
- **Real people.** Her friend and correspondent is not named in the modern episode; her husband and children appear only in the claims about her later life. No relative, enslaver or other real person is given a decision, a statement or a health fact in the modern episode.
