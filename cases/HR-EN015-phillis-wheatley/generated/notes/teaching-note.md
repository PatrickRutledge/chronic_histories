# Teaching Note — Phillis Wheatley pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Phillis Wheatley was born in West Africa, probably in the Senegambia region (present-day Senegal and the Gambia), about 1753 or 1754. Nothing is known of her early life before she was kidnapped and trafficked as a child. In 1761 the child arrived in Boston aboard the slave ship Phillis (on 11 July, by one scholarly chronology) and was bought by the Boston merchant John Wheatley and his wife Susanna (or Susannah), in whose household she was enslaved as a domestic servant and companion. She was named Phillis after the ship and Wheatley after the family. The family judged her to be about seven because she was shedding her front teeth. When she was bought in 1761 she was described as a slender, frail child, "evidently suffering from a change of climate"; the slave ship's captain is said to have sold her cheaply because he believed she was dying. One reference work describes her as "the frail, asthmatic child" and says she remained small and delicate. In the Wheatley household she was taught to read and write and within about sixteen months could read the Bible; she went on to read Greek and Latin classics and British literature. Her first known poem was published in the Newport Mercury in December 1767, and her elegy on the evangelist George Whitefield, published in 1770, brought her wide renown. Proposals to publish a volume of her poems in Boston in 1772 found too few subscribers, and the volume was instead prepared for publication in London with the support of Selina Hastings, Countess of Huntingdon. In May 1773 she sailed for London with Nathaniel Wheatley, the family's son. The Massachusetts Historical Society says the voyage was "partly to regain her fragile health, but more importantly to shepherd her book of poetry through to publication"; another biography says she left "suffering from a chronic asthma condition." In London she was received by many prominent people. Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral was published there in September 1773, the first volume of poetry by an African American published in modern times. She returned to Boston in September 1773; Boston newspapers reported her return on 16 September. In a letter from Boston dated 30 October 1773 to her friend Obour Tanner she wrote that she was "at present indispos'd by a cold. & Since my arrival have been visited by the Asthma," and that "my voyage to England has conduced to the recovery (in a great measure) of my Health." She was freed soon after her return. On 18 October 1773 she wrote to David Wooster: "Since my return to America my Master, has at the desire of my friends in England given me my freedom," adding that whatever she earned from her book was now hers. One biography dates the manumission to some three months before Susanna Wheatley's death on 3 March 1774. In 1778 (on 1 April, by one biography) she married John Peters, a free Black man of Boston. The couple lived in poverty in Wilmington and Boston during and after the Revolutionary War; she worked as a maid or charwoman while continuing to write, and a proposal in 1779 for a second volume of her poems found too few subscribers. She bore children who did not survive. Sources differ on the details: one says three children, none of whom survived; one that she had lost two children and died with her young daughter; one that her last surviving child, a son, was buried with her; and one that the third child died a few hours after she did. She died in poverty in a Boston boardinghouse on 5 December 1784, aged about 31; her husband was probably in prison for debt at the time. The Independent Chronicle noted the death of "Phillis Peters formerly Phillis Wheatley aged 31." The cause of her death is not recorded in the cited sources.
- **R:** Retrospective interpretation: the asthma her contemporaries named and she herself described, with a cold bringing it on and a sea voyage and a summer away relieving it, is consistent with asthma in the modern sense, but no source records her symptoms in enough detail to confirm it, and other chest disease cannot be excluded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
