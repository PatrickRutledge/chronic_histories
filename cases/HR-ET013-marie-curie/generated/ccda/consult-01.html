  • Marie Curie, born Maria Sklodowska, was born in Warsaw on November 7, 1867, the daughter of a secondary-school teacher. [D]
  • In 1920 Curie learned that she had a double cataract; her vision became so impaired that she wrote lecture notes in huge letters, and only after four operations, between 1923 and 1930, could she again do exacting laboratory work and drive a car. [D]
  • In a November 1920 letter to her sister Bronya, Curie wrote that radium perhaps had something to do with her troubles, but that this could not be affirmed with certainty. [D]
  • Her cataracts are regarded as an early documented example of radiation cataract. [R]
  • Curie's fingers were severely burned from handling radioactive materials with her bare hands while preparing radium standards. [D]
  • During the First World War Curie directed the Red Cross Radiology Service, set up France's first military radiology centers, and equipped 20 mobile X-ray vehicles, the first ready by late October 1914. [D]
  • In 1925 Curie took part in a French Academy of Medicine commission that recommended lead screens and periodic blood tests for workers handling radioactive materials, and she required Radium Institute staff to have regular blood counts. [D]
  • In May 1934 Curie went home sick from her laboratory and never returned; specialists who suspected tuberculosis advised a sanatorium stay, and a medical expert from Geneva diagnosed an incurable blood disorder. [D]
  • Curie's mother, Bronislawa Sklodowska, died in May 1878 of tuberculosis at age 42, after a five-year illness, when Marie was ten; her eldest sister, Zosia, died of typhus caught from a boarder when Marie was eight. [D]
  • With Pierre Curie, whom she married in 1895, she discovered polonium and radium; she shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics, received the 1911 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and directed the Curie Laboratory of the Radium Institute. Pierre Curie died in 1906. [D]
