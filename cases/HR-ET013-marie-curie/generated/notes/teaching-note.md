# Teaching Note — Marie Curie pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Marie Curie, born Maria Sklodowska, was born in Warsaw on November 7, 1867, the daughter of a secondary-school teacher. Curie died on July 4, 1934, after a short illness, in Savoy, France; the sanatorium director reported the disease as an aplastic pernicious anemia of rapid, feverish development. In 1920 Curie learned that she had a double cataract; her vision became so impaired that she wrote lecture notes in huge letters, and only after four operations, between 1923 and 1930, could she again do exacting laboratory work and drive a car. In a November 1920 letter to her sister Bronya, Curie wrote that radium perhaps had something to do with her troubles, but that this could not be affirmed with certainty. Curie's fingers were severely burned from handling radioactive materials with her bare hands while preparing radium standards. During the First World War Curie directed the Red Cross Radiology Service, set up France's first military radiology centers, and equipped 20 mobile X-ray vehicles, the first ready by late October 1914. In 1925 Curie took part in a French Academy of Medicine commission that recommended lead screens and periodic blood tests for workers handling radioactive materials, and she required Radium Institute staff to have regular blood counts. In May 1934 Curie went home sick from her laboratory and never returned; specialists who suspected tuberculosis advised a sanatorium stay, and a medical expert from Geneva diagnosed an incurable blood disorder. Curie's mother, Bronislawa Sklodowska, died in May 1878 of tuberculosis at age 42, after a five-year illness, when Marie was ten; her eldest sister, Zosia, died of typhus caught from a boarder when Marie was eight. With Pierre Curie, whom she married in 1895, she discovered polonium and radium; she shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics, received the 1911 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and directed the Curie Laboratory of the Radium Institute. Pierre Curie died in 1906.
- **R:** Her bone marrow failure has been attributed to long exposure to radiation: the sanatorium director wrote that the marrow had probably been injured by a long accumulation of radiations, and a historian concludes that radiation exposure likely shortened her life. Her cataracts are regarded as an early documented example of radiation cataract.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
