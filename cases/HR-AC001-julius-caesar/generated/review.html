Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Name. The display name is “Julius Caesar”. His full name, “Gaius Julius Caesar”, appears in no other record and is the alias. “Caesar” alone is not used as an alias, because the word appears in other records. The name splits simply, so `identity.name_parts` is not used.
  2. Dates and calendars. Historical dates use signed astronomical years: -0099 is 100 BC, -0045 is 46 BC, -0043 is 44 BC. His birth on 12 or 13 July 100 BC is a date in the pre-Julian Roman republican calendar, which by the 40s BC had drifted months away from the seasons (Suetonius 40), so it cannot be converted exactly; it is recorded with precision “approximate” and calendar “unknown”, and 12 July is used for the synthetic birthday, with 13 July as an unresolved alternative. Thapsus is 6 April 46 BC in the old calendar and about 7 February 46 BC in Julian terms. The Julian calendar took effect on 1 January 45 BC, after 46 BC was lengthened to 445 days; his death on 15 March 44 BC is therefore recorded as a Julian date, although leap days were at first inserted every third year, so its exact equivalent in the proleptic Julian calendar is uncertain by a day or so. Because he was born before 1 CE, the record has no true-date variant (docs/date-shifting.md).
  3. Index age and dates. The episode presents him at 54. The synthetic date of birth is July 12, 1972, so the whole-year shift is 2071 years and 2026 maps to 46 BC. The documented sources place him in Rome from July to early November 46 BC (after the African campaign and before he left for Spain), so all six visits, August 4 to October 27, 2026, fall in Rome in the true frame (August 4 to October 27, 46 BC, read in the old Roman calendar). 45 BC was rejected: he was in Spain until about June and returned to Rome only in October, too late for an episode that must end by November 1. The assignment's “index at about 54 to 55” is met at 54; he is 54 throughout. The true-frame dates fall in the year of the long calendar, so they are approximate in the true frame; no documented event in those months is contradicted.
  4. Thin chart. Little is knowable. Two ancient biographers, writing about 150 years later, report fainting fits, nightmares, headache (“distemper in the head”), and attacks of the “falling sickness”, the first at an undated time at Corduba; one alternative account places an attack at Thapsus; one anecdote late in his life has him blame his illness for not rising to the senators, an excuse Plutarch doubts. These reports are D (the sources plainly say so) with their uncertainty in the certainty field: Thapsus is “possible”, because Plutarch gives it only as what others say. The Latin comitialis morbus covered any falling attack and is not taken to mean epilepsy in the modern sense. Every modern diagnosis is R: epilepsy with complex partial seizures (Hughes 2004), late-onset epilepsy from neurocysticercosis (McLachlan 2010; Bruschi 2011), cerebrovascular disease with transient ischemic attacks (Galassi and Ashrafian 2015), an arteriovenous malformation (Montemurro 2015), a brain tumor (Gomez 1995; Retief 2010), celiac disease (Imparato 2016), and malaria. The dispute stays unresolved in the record. The quartan fever in his youth is D as a reported illness; the modern reading as malaria is noted but not coded.
  5. Roster corrections. The roster lists epilepsy “throughout career”, “head trauma; stress illness; TBI probable”, and an age at death of 56. The sources read place the attacks late in life (Suetonius, “towards the end”) or give no date (Plutarch's Corduba attack), record no head injury, and give his age as “in the fifty-sixth year” (55; Plutarch's “fully fifty-six” is noted). No claim about head trauma or a stress illness is made, and the record's age at death is 55.
  6. Modern diagnosis (judgment call). The presenting problem is recurrent transient loss of consciousness, kept as its own resolved problem (R56.9) until the cause is found at the second visit. The episode decides for late-onset focal epilepsy with a structural vascular cause (G40.209), from a chronic cortical infarct not recognized before (I69.398), because the semiology (a rising epigastric aura, staring with oral automatisms, a bitten tongue, and prolonged confusion), the left temporal EEG sharp waves, and the matching MRI lesion point to seizures; syncope is excluded by the ECG, orthostatic readings, echocardiogram, and more than 40 days of rhythm monitoring, and transient ischemic attack because the spells cause loss of awareness rather than a loss of function and the arteries are not narrowed. This lets the modern episode teach both sides of the historical argument (late-onset epilepsy is often vascular) without endorsing either reading of his real illness. The MRI's lack of a tumor, malformation, cyst, or calcification is a teaching nod to the other hypotheses and makes no claim about his real brain.
  7. Secondary rather than primary prevention (judgment call). The AHA/ASA 2017 statement advises primary prevention for silent infarcts. The consultant judges that an infarct that has become a seizure focus is no longer silent and treats it as an ischemic stroke of undetermined cause under the AHA/ASA 2021 guideline: aspirin 81 mg, atorvastatin 80 mg to an LDL below 70 mg/dL, blood pressure below 130/80 mmHg, and at least 30 days of rhythm monitoring. A reviewer may prefer primary prevention; the record's statin and blood pressure treatment would be indicated either way.
  8. Echoes, not claims. The modern episode echoes documented details without asserting them: his height (Suetonius, “tall”) and lean build (Plutarch, “spare habit”); very little alcohol (Suetonius 53; AUDIT-C 1); headaches and vivid dreams that wake him; a spell abroad six months earlier during which he was helped into a nearby building (Thapsus); a public career with long outdoor ceremonies (the triumphs of 46 BC); and the sudden death of his father, recorded in the family history with the cause “not recorded in the cited source”. Pliny's second sudden death in the family is not put in the family history, because the relationship is uncertain.
