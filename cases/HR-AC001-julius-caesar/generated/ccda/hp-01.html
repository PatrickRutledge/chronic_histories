Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Gaius Julius Caesar was born on 12 or 13 July 100 BC in the Subura district of Rome, into the patrician family of the Julii. [D]
  • Suetonius writes that he lost his father 'in the course of his sixteenth year'. [D]
  • Pliny the Elder records that two men of the family, one of them his father, a former praetor, 'died without any apparent cause, in the morning, while putting on their shoes'. [D]
  • As a young man, in hiding from Sulla's agents, he was 'suffering from a severe attack of quartan ague' and changed his hiding place almost every night. [D]
  • Plutarch describes him as 'of a spare habit', with 'a soft and white skin', and says he 'suffered from distemper in the head, and was subject to epileptic fits', the first of which attacked him at Corduba; he made 'his military service a cure for his feeble health'. [D]
  • Suetonius says he was tall, of sound health, 'except that towards the end he was subject to sudden fainting fits and to nightmare as well', and that he was 'twice attacked by the falling sickness' (comitialis morbus) during his campaigns. [D]
  • Plutarch gives a second account of the battle of Thapsus in Africa in 46 BC, in which 'his usual sickness laid hold of him' as he was drawing up his army, and, sensing it coming, he was carried to a nearby tower and stayed there during the battle. [D]
  • He drank very little wine: 'not even his enemies denied' it. [D]
  • His only daughter, Julia, died in childbirth in 54 BC. [D]
  • After the African campaign he returned to Rome in July 46 BC, celebrated four triumphs within a single month, and left Rome for Further Spain early in November 46 BC. [D]
  • He reformed the Roman calendar, which had drifted so far that the harvest festivals no longer came in summer, to a year of 365 days with a day added every fourth year; to realign it he lengthened 46 BC to 445 days, and the new calendar took effect on 1 January 45 BC. [D]
  • Retrospective readings take the attacks as epilepsy: one review counts four attacks that were probably complex partial (focal impaired-awareness) seizures and suggests an inherited cause; another treats them as late-onset epilepsy beginning at about 54 and proposes neurocysticercosis. [R]
  • Galassi and Ashrafian (2015) argued that his symptoms (headache, vertigo, falls, limb weakness, the collapse at Thapsus, and the giddiness at the Senate) fit cerebrovascular disease with transient ischemic attacks better than epilepsy, and that the sudden deaths in his family suggest cardiovascular disease. [R]
  • Other proposed causes include a brain arteriovenous malformation, a brain tumor such as a meningioma, celiac disease, and malaria. [R]
