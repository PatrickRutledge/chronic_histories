## Editorial resolutions

- **Recent-death rule.** Griffith Joyner died in 1998, so the rule applies. The case uses what was reported during her life (the Associated Press report of the 1996 seizure), the coroner’s findings as reported by news organizations and World Athletics, and reference works for her life and career. The earlier treated seizures come from the coroner’s investigation records as reported by *Salon* after her death; they are graded probable (CLM-0006) and are used only as history. No sick visit is added. Her death is recorded once (CLM-0007), in plain words. No claim concerns mental health or substance use; the modern mood screens are synthetic and below the threshold for a safety assessment.
- **What is left out.** The family asked in 1996 that no details of that seizure be released, and the modern episode respects that: it adds no account of the real seizure beyond what was published, and the description of the seizure in the modern notes (witnessed stiffening and jerking, confusion afterwards, no memory of it) is synthetic and generic. Details from the coroner’s scene and investigation notes and the statements of relatives are not used. Rumors about performance-enhancing drugs, which were never proven, are not part of the clinical record; the coroner’s toxicology showed only over-the-counter medicines in usual amounts, and an Associated Press report said the autopsy found no indication of drug use (CBS News).
- **Real people.** No relative is named, and no statement, decision or health fact is attributed to any relative. The household is described only as married with a young child, without names or roles; the nocturnal detection watch alerts “a contact she chose.” Officials quoted in the reports appear only as their sources. The family history is left empty.
- **Conflicts.** (1) Her given names appear as Delorez Florence (Olympedia) and Florence Delorez (Encyclopedia.com); CLM-0001 records both. (2) The coroner’s investigator was reported as saying cavernous angiomas are found in about 25 percent of the population (CBS News) and in 0.25 percent (World Athletics); the second matches the medical literature, and neither figure is used. (3) The *Irish Times* spells the chief forensic pathologist’s name differently from the other reports; the name is not used. (4) Her stay after the 1996 seizure is described as release the next day (Associated Press, 1996) and as a day under observation (Encyclopedia.com), which agree. (5) The *Los Angeles Times* and *New York Times* reports could not be fetched and are not cited; the *Los Angeles Times* is quoted only through *Salon*, and that quotation is not used.
- **Alias.** “Flo-Jo” appears in no other record and is used as an alias, with her full name. “Griffith” is part of her family name, not an alias; `identity.name_parts` gives the family name as “Griffith Joyner.”
- **Index age and dates.** The index visit on April 22, 2026 maps to April 22, 1996, when she was 36: about two and a half weeks after the seizure on a flight, the first seizure reported publicly in her life.
