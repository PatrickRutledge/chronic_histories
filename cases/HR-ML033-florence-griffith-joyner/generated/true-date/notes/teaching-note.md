# Teaching Note — Florence Griffith Joyner pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Florence Griffith was born in Los Angeles on December 21, 1959, the seventh of eleven children, and grew up from the age of four in a public housing project in the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles. Reference works give her given names in both orders, Delorez Florence and Florence Delorez. She ran for California State University, Northridge, and then for UCLA, graduating in 1983, and won the silver medal in the 200 meters at the 1984 Olympic Games in Los Angeles, competing as Florence Griffith. In 1988 she set a world record of 10.49 seconds for 100 meters at the U.S. Olympic Trials and, at the Seoul Olympic Games, won gold medals in the 100 meters, the 200 meters (in a world record of 21.34 seconds) and the 4 x 100 meters relay, and a silver medal in the 4 x 400 meters relay. She retired from competition in 1989. In 1993 she was appointed co-chair of the President's Council on Physical Fitness and Sports, and in 1995 she was inducted into the U.S. Track and Field Hall of Fame. She also worked as a designer of clothing and athletic wear. In April 1996 she had a seizure on an airplane on the way to St. Louis. She was taken to hospital and discharged the next day, and her family asked that no other details be released. Coroner's investigation records, reported after her death, state that she had a generalized (grand mal) seizure in 1990 and had been treated for seizures in 1990, 1993 and 1994. She died in her sleep at her home in Mission Viejo, California, on September 21, 1998, at 38. The Orange County Sheriff-Coroner reported that she died of positional asphyxia during an epileptic seizure, and that the seizure was caused by a congenital cavernous angioma (cavernous malformation) of the left orbital frontal cerebrum.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
