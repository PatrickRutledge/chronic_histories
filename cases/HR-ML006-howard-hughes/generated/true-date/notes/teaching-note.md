# Teaching Note — Howard Hughes pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Howard Robard Hughes Jr. was born in Houston, Texas, on December 24, 1905, the only child of an oilman whose drill bit became the foundation of the family fortune. He was an aviator who set speed records and founded the Hughes Aircraft Company, a film producer, and the owner of the Hughes Tool Company; during the Second World War he was contracted to build a very large wooden flying boat. After an unexplained paralysis in 1919, as a boy, he developed what a state historical handbook calls a lifetime phobic regard for his health; a psychologist who studied his life after his death concluded that his fear for his health began in childhood. He suffered a nervous breakdown in 1944. On July 7, 1946, on the first flight of the XF-11 reconnaissance plane, he crashed into houses in Beverly Hills and was critically injured, with a crushed chest, a collapsed lung, multiple broken bones and second- and third-degree burns; he nearly died. Drugs for pain were first given to him after the 1946 crash; the crash left him with lingering pain and an addiction to codeine, the beginning of a lifelong dependence on painkillers. His fear of germs grew throughout his life, with efforts to protect himself from contamination from outside; a news magazine linked his celebrated fear of germs in part to the 1946 crash, which injured his lungs and left him susceptible to bronchial infection. Business pressures drove him to an emotional breakdown in 1958, and he was not seen in public after 1958. He became increasingly deaf; in his later years he could understand speech face to face when people talked loudly, seldom used his hearing devices, and played films with the sound turned up for his impaired hearing. From the 1950s he lived in growing seclusion, conducting business through memos; he lived on the top floor of the Desert Inn in Las Vegas from 1966 to 1970, then moved between hotels in the Bahamas, Nicaragua, Canada, England and Mexico, arriving unannounced and taking extreme precautions for privacy. In his later years his staff followed elaborate procedures to protect him from germs, such as wrapping objects in tissue before handing them to him; he lived in darkened rooms, ate little, injected drugs with a syringe, and became emaciated from too little food and too many drugs. He died on April 5, 1976, at 70, aboard a plane taking him from Acapulco, Mexico, to Houston for medical treatment. He flew the flying boat on its only flight, at Long Beach, California, on November 2, 1947. The autopsy in Houston attributed his death to kidney failure; he weighed only about 90 pounds, his kidneys had shrunk to less than half their normal size, and codeine and diazepam (Valium) were found in his blood.
- **R:** Psychiatrists and OCD clinicians have described his behavior, retrospectively, as a severe contamination-type obsessive-compulsive disorder; one journal report calls his case an unusual end state of obsessive-compulsive neurosis in which a germ phobia coexists with neglect of personal hygiene.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
