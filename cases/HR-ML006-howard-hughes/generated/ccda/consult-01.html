History of Present Illness

Mr. Hughes, a 41-year-old aviator and aircraft builder, is seen at the request of Dr. Garrity-Moyo one week after intake. He describes a fear for his health since boyhood and, for about a year, fears of contamination that became much worse after the crash ten months ago: unwanted thoughts that he will be contaminated from outside, by people, door handles, money and food, with washing, cleaning and avoidance that take about four hours a day. He knows the fears are out of proportion but cannot resist the rituals. He does not feel that things must be done in a perfect order for their own sake, he has no fixed belief that he has a serious illness, and he has no nightmares or flashbacks of the crash, though he avoids talking about it. He has no hallucinations and no beliefs that others are harming him. His staff wash before handing him anything, pass him objects wrapped in tissue and screen visitors for illness. He wants treatment and wants it kept private. [S]

Assessment

  • Obsessive-compulsive disorder, contamination type, with good or fair insight (F42.2). The DSM-5-TR criteria are met: intrusive, unwanted contamination obsessions and washing, cleaning and avoidance compulsions aimed at preventing harm, taking more than an hour a day and limiting his work. He recognizes the fears are probably excessive, so the specifier is with good or fair insight; there is no tic history. The rituals are distressing to him, which separates this from obsessive-compulsive personality disorder; his fear is of contamination rather than of having an illness, which separates it from illness anxiety disorder; the PCL-5 of 18 and the absence of re-experiencing argue against post-traumatic stress disorder; and there are no psychotic features. Y-BOCS 28 is in the severe range. [S]
  • Medication. An SSRI at OCD doses with exposure and response prevention is first-line under the APA guideline and NICE CG31. Sertraline is preferred to fluvoxamine, which inhibits the enzymes that clear buprenorphine. Start at 50 mg and raise every four to six weeks as tolerated toward 200 mg; OCD often needs higher doses and longer trials than depression, and a fair trial is 8 to 12 weeks with at least four to six weeks at the highest tolerated dose. Check tolerability two weeks after each change and the sodium at follow-up. [S]
  • Exposure and response prevention. Exposure and response prevention is the psychological treatment with the best evidence. A hierarchy built with him, from door handles and shared objects to food made by others, restaurants, handshakes and finally the clinic bathroom, with weekly 90-minute sessions and daily home practice, moving to twice weekly once he is engaged. Sessions can be held at the clinic early in the day to protect his privacy. [S]
  • Household staff. His staff are doing what family members often do: carrying out rituals and giving reassurance, which keeps the fears going. Money and staff remove every natural limit on avoidance. With his written consent, his assistant should join two sessions to learn to step back in planned stages that follow his hierarchy and to offer support instead of reassurance. Staff should not be asked to monitor or report on him. [S]
  • If the response is poor. If the Y-BOCS has fallen by less than about 25 to 35 percent after 8 to 12 weeks at the highest tolerated sertraline dose with good exposure therapy, add a low-dose antipsychotic (aripiprazole or risperidone) or switch to clomipramine, with an ECG first. Neither is needed now. [S]
  • Opioid use disorder and safety (F11.20). Buprenorphine treats the opioid use disorder and helps his pain, and it has no important interaction with sertraline. He has no suicidal ideation; the safety plan, naloxone and weekly dispensing are appropriate. Review suicidal thoughts at each visit, as SSRIs can briefly increase restlessness early on. [S]
