Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Calendar and dates. England used the Julian (Old Style) calendar throughout her life, and the legal year began on 25 March, so her death was written at the time as 24 March 1602. The sources give Julian dates with the year counted from 1 January. The record converts them to the Gregorian calendar for the true frame, as other records with Old Style dates do: her birth on 7 September 1533 (Julian) is 17 September 1533 in the proleptic Gregorian calendar (the reform came in 1582), and her death on 24 March 1603 (Julian) is 3 April 1603 (Gregorian). The difference was ten days throughout her lifetime; the conversions were checked by computing Julian day numbers. The preferred birth and death dates are the Gregorian ones, with the Julian dates recorded as superseded alternatives; timeline and past-history dates carry Gregorian ISO values, with the Julian date in the display text. The synthetic date of birth keeps 17 September.
  2. Index age and dates. The episode presents her at 69, in the winter before her death, when her chaplain later said she had been troubled with melancholy by fits for three or four months and her godson found her low and eating little. The synthetic date of birth is September 17, 1956, so the whole-year shift is 423 years and 2026 maps to 1603. Because she died on April 3, 1603 (Gregorian), every visit had to fall between January 1 and April 2, 2026. The index visit is January 12, 2026 (true frame January 12, 1603, Gregorian, which is January 2, 1603, Julian); the follow-ups are January 26 (consultation), February 23, and March 16; the sick visit is February 2 and the annual wellness visit March 2, 2026 (true frame the same days of 1603). She is 69 throughout. The last visit, March 16 (true frame March 6, 1603, Julian), falls just before the worst of her documented final illness. The modern episode is a counterfactual in which treatment helps; it makes no claim about what happened to her, and in the true frame the later visits overlap her documented decline.
  3. Name. The display name is “Elizabeth I”. Split at the last space it would give the family name “I”, so `identity.name_parts` is given ["Elizabeth"], family "Tudor", the name of her royal house, following the dataset's precedent for royal names; the C-CDA and FHIR names are therefore “Elizabeth Tudor”, and the notes address her as Ms. Tudor. “Elizabeth Tudor” appears in no other record and is the alias. The roster's “Queen Elizabeth I” is a style with a title and is not used.
  4. What was verified and what was not. Smallpox in October 1562 at 29, survived, is D (Folger chronology quoting her letter and the Spanish ambassador). The roster's “facial scarring from smallpox” is contradicted by the same source, which says her nurse was scarred “unlike her” and that the recovery medal shows her face unblemished, so it is recorded only that way. The last months: deep melancholy, sleeplessness, refusal of food, rest, and physic, sighing, and grief after the death of the Countess of Nottingham are D in several contemporary letters, Carey's memoir, and Manningham's diary. A “sore throat” could not be found in any contemporary source read: Camden wrote of “an inflammation from the breast upward” and Father Rivers of an “imposthumation in her head”; the museum page lists infected tonsils as one later theory. These are recorded as written, with no throat diagnosis asserted. Her black teeth (Hentzner, 1598) are D; the museum's statement that she lost most of her teeth is D, probable. The report of failing judgement and memory (Rivers) conflicts with the chaplain's statement that she kept her senses and memory; both are recorded.
  5. Lead and adrenal leads. The roster's lead poisoning from cosmetics is recorded only as one of several proposed causes of death in a national museum's account (R, disputed), and is not used in the modern episode: no lead level is ordered and no cosmetic exposure is invented. No reputable source read suggested adrenal insufficiency, so it is left out.
  6. Depression as a modern reading. The record never states that she had major depression. Her melancholy, sleeplessness, and poor intake are D; the modern major depressive disorder (F32.1) is synthetic, placed both_with_link to those claims. The museum's link between her melancholy and the deaths of Katherine Howard and Essex is R, possible. The chaplain's account dates the onset of low spirits to late 1602, before the index date; the Countess of Nottingham's death and the March 1603 accounts come after it, so they are linked only to later timeline events and appear in no intake document.
  7. Presenting problem. Low mood, sleeplessness, poor appetite, and weight loss (R45.89) is the intake problem, resolved on January 26, 2026, when major depressive disorder and comorbid insomnia are recorded after the tests for medical causes come back normal. The treatment choice (sertraline and interpersonal psychotherapy) carries `recorded_date` January 26; intake documents show only the workup, the safety plan, the sleep and nutrition measures, and the referral.
  8. Safety assessment. The synthetic intake records a passive wish not to wake on some mornings, without intent, plan, or behavior (PHQ-9 item 9 scored 1), with a safety plan, the 988 Lifeline, medicines held by her maid, and review at every later visit. This echoes, without asserting, the French ambassador's report that she said she wished to die (D, sensitive), recorded as his report and not interpreted. All claims about her mood, sleep, appetite, and death are marked sensitive.
  9. Bereavement. The modern social history says only that several people closest to her have died, including a former protégé and confidant after a bitter breach, and two friends of her generation. It echoes the documented execution of the Earl of Essex in 1601 and her distress at his memory in Harington's letter; no real person is named in the modern episode or given a statement, decision, or health fact. The Countess of Nottingham's death, after the index date, is not used in the modern episode.
  10. Family history. Only documented facts are recorded: her mother's execution in 1536 (not a medical cause), her father's death in 1547, and her half-sister's death in November 1558, causes not recorded in the cited source.
  11. Past medical history. Smallpox (1562), black teeth (1598), the ache in her arm, side, and hip (1602), and the strained foot and fall boarding her barge (December 1602, before the index date) are D. The fall anchors the synthetic fall-risk problem (Z91.81).
  12. Dental problem. The synthetic partial tooth loss with an ill-fitting denture (K08.409) echoes the documented descriptions of her teeth without asserting a cause; the roster's “dental disease severe” is not otherwise used.
  13. Drug choices. Sertraline starts at 25 mg for a week, then 50 mg, as an older-adult start, following the APA guideline's preference for a second-generation antidepressant combined with interpersonal psychotherapy and the Beers Criteria (no paroxetine, tricyclics, or strongly anticholinergic drugs). An over-the-counter diphenhydramine sleep aid found on medication review is stopped at intake. Insomnia is treated with CBT-I and no hypnotic. Acetaminophen, not an NSAID, is used for the sore throat because of the bleeding risk with an SSRI. Sodium is checked four weeks after the start, normal.
  14. Psychotherapy and privacy. Individual interpersonal psychotherapy is chosen over the APA guideline's group options because of her need for privacy as a public figure; the record keeps her care private (end-of-day appointments, restricted chart access, results in person).
  15. Instruments. PHQ-9 (44261-6), GDS-15 (48545-8), and MoCA (72172-0) are coded surveys. No LOINC code for the Insomnia Severity Index was found on the public terminology server (searches for “insomnia severity” and “ISI”), so its scores (19, 14, 11) are kept in the note text. The C-SSRS screen and Timed Up and Go results are in the text.
  16. Sick and wellness visits. The sick visit is a viral pharyngitis with cough, runny nose, and hoarseness after colds among her staff; it echoes the documented cold of January 1603 and teaches when not to test for streptococcus, acetaminophen rather than an NSAID on an SSRI, avoiding anticholinergic cold remedies, and the warning signs of a peritonsillar abscess. It asserts no cause for the inflammation reported in March 1603. Sertraline is raised to 50 mg at that visit, as planned. The wellness visit is `annual_wellness_subsequent` because she is 65 or older; it orders a DXA, gives the seasonal COVID-19 vaccine, and records advance care planning that names no real person; no DXA result falls within the episode.
  17. Focused visits. The three chronic-care follow-ups (two, six, and nine weeks after intake) are documented as focused visits on the CMID depression monograph with the APA 2019 and VA/DoD 2022 guidelines, the VA/DoD suicide-risk guideline, the AGS Beers Criteria, and the ACP insomnia guideline: PHQ-9 at every visit, GDS-15 monthly, suicide-risk reassessment at every visit, the workup and bipolar interview at the diagnosis visit, sodium after starting the SSRI, adherence and adverse effects, weight, orthostatic blood pressure and gait, CBT-I, and psychotherapy attendance. Two deviations are recorded as `deferred`, each with its outcome and corrective action, and no result was invented: the SSRI tolerability check two weeks after the increase to 50 mg was not booked (first review three weeks after the change, with no harm), and the first interpersonal psychotherapy session took place two weeks after referral rather than within the first week because private sessions had to be found. The episode ends at a response (PHQ-9 8, 53 percent below baseline), not remission; the planned twelve-week review falls after her true-frame death and is not part of the record.
  18. Consultation. The geriatric psychiatry referral and consultation (Gideon Rasmussen-Hale, MD, a fictional consultant, on the day of the two-week visit) are S. The consultant confirms a major depressive episode rather than grief alone, with no bipolar, psychotic, or cognitive features, and recommends what the record then shows: sertraline 25 mg then 50 mg with an increase only if the response is inadequate after six to eight weeks (no increase was needed), a sodium check at two to four weeks (done at four), gait and orthostatic checks, individual interpersonal psychotherapy, CBT-I without hypnotics, safety review at every visit, and continuation after remission. Mirtazapine is named as a second choice only; it was never needed.
  19. Primary clinician. Philippa Ashcombe-Nwosu, MD, is cast as her primary care physician.
  20. Elizabeth I died in 1603, so the recent-death review rule does not apply. Ordinary clinical, provenance, and safe-messaging reviews remain required.
