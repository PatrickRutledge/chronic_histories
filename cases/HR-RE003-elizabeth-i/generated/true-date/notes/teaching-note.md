# Teaching Note — Elizabeth I pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Elizabeth was born at Greenwich on 7 September 1533 (Julian calendar; 17 September 1533 in the proleptic Gregorian calendar), the daughter of Henry VIII and his second wife, Anne Boleyn. Her mother, Anne Boleyn, was executed in 1536, when Elizabeth was two; her father, Henry VIII, reigned until 1547. She succeeded her half-sister Mary I on Mary's death in November 1558 and never married. In October 1562, aged 29, she fell ill at Hampton Court with a fever that turned to smallpox. On 15 October she wrote that a hot fever prevented her writing more; the Spanish ambassador reported that she lost her speech and was thought lost, that the eruption appeared in the night, and that she then improved. The court feared for the succession, and she recovered. Lady Sidney, who nursed her through the smallpox, caught it and was scarred for life; the court chronology states that, unlike the Queen, she was scarred. A medal struck for the Queen's recovery shows her face unblemished. A German visitor who saw her at Greenwich in August 1598, in her 65th year, described her face as fair but wrinkled and her teeth as black, a defect he attributed to the English use of sugar. A museum account of her death states that she had lost most of her teeth and her hair by the end of her life. On 25 February 1601 (Julian) the Earl of Essex, once her favourite, was executed at the Tower after his rising. Through 1602 court letters describe an ache in her arm that later spread to her side and hip; in September observers wrote that her health was very good and that she hunted and rode often. In December 1602 (Julian) she strained her foot leaving a dinner at her secretary's new house and, refusing help, stumbled and fell entering her barge, bruising her shins, which soon healed. On 27 December 1602 (Julian), her godson Sir John Harington wrote that she now bore show of human infirmity: he had found her in a most pitiable state, she wept at the memory of the Irish war in which he had served with the Earl of Essex, she said she was past her relish for his verses, and she told him that her bodily meat did not suit her and that she had eaten only one ill-tasted cake since the night before. In January 1603 (Julian) she caught a cold shortly before the court moved from Whitehall to Richmond on 21 January, and was reported well amended of it at Richmond. On 6 February she received the Venetian envoy in state and spoke at length. The Countess of Nottingham, her cousin and a lady of her Privy Chamber since 1559, died on 25 February 1603 (Julian). The French ambassador wrote that the Queen asked to be excused audiences while she got over her grief and had shed many tears for her. In March 1603 (Julian) her secretary wrote that for eight or nine days she had been much deprived of sleep, which decayed her appetite; another observer wrote that she rested ill at night and abstained more than usual from her meat; and a third that for some twenty days she had slept very little and her sleep and stomach had left her. Her cousin Robert Carey, who reached Richmond in March 1603, wrote that she told him her heart had been sad and heavy for ten or twelve days, sighed forty or fifty times as they spoke, and that her melancholy was too deep-rooted to be removed; she then sat on cushions for at least four days and nights and could not be persuaded to eat or to go to bed, and refused all remedies. The French ambassador reported in March 1603 that she had said she wished not to live any longer but desired to die, that she took no medicine, and that everyone agreed melancholy was the true cause of her illness. On 23 March 1603 (Julian) a royal chaplain told the diarist John Manningham that she had been troubled with melancholy by fits for some three or four months and for the last fortnight had been extremely oppressed by it, refusing to eat, to take physic, or to rest in bed; that she had always kept her senses and memory; and that her physicians thought she had a firm constitution likely to have lived many years. Another letter of March 1603 reported that she complained of an imposthumation in her head, aches in her bones, and continual cold in her legs, with a notable decay of judgement and memory; William Camden wrote of her sleepless indisposition joined with an inflammation from the breast upward. She died at Richmond Palace between one and two in the morning of 24 March 1603 (Julian; 3 April 1603 Gregorian), aged 69, after growing speechless the day before; by English legal reckoning, in which the year began on 25 March, the date was written as 1602.
- **R:** A museum account links her late bouts of melancholy to the deaths of her long-serving lady-in-waiting and of the Earl of Essex. Proposed causes of her death include blood poisoning from a lead-based white face paint (Venetian ceruse), pneumonia, a streptococcal infection of the tonsils, and cancer; no post-mortem was allowed.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
