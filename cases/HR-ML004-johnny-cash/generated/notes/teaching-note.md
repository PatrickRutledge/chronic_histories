# Teaching Note — Johnny Cash pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** J. R. (Johnny) Cash was born on February 26, 1932, in Kingsland, Cleveland County, Arkansas, to Ray and Carrie Cash. Cash died at Baptist Hospital in Nashville, Tennessee, on September 12, 2003; his manager, Lou Robin, said in a statement issued by the hospital that he died of complications from diabetes, which resulted in respiratory failure. Cash's older brother Jack died in a sawmill accident in 1944. In 1957 Cash began taking amphetamines to stay awake on a long road trip, the start of an addiction that lasted about a decade; in a 2002 interview he named amphetamines and barbiturates in the 1960s. Cash was hospitalized in 1983 with internal bleeding; after recovering he entered the Betty Ford Clinic for treatment. In October 1997 Cash was diagnosed with Shy-Drager syndrome, an illness similar to Parkinson's disease; in a 2000 interview he said the diagnosis was a misdiagnosis. Cash later learned he had autonomic neuropathy; in November 2002 he described it as a deadening of the nerve endings in his lower extremities that had taken the strength from his legs, so that he could walk, but not very well. Cash was repeatedly hospitalized for pneumonia in his last years; he was treated for pneumonia at Baptist Hospital in Nashville in February 2001, and in November 2002 he said he had had pneumonia four times in three years. In November 2002 Cash said about 60 percent of his vision was gone, which he attributed to the neuropathy and the diabetes, and he said he hoped the glaucoma could also be reversed.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
