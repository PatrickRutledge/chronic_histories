## Historical evidence

1. Childress B. “Arthur Ashe (1943–1993).” *Encyclopedia Virginia* (Virginia Humanities), December 7, 2020. Supports his birth as Arthur Robert Ashe Jr. in Richmond on July 10, 1943, to Mattie Cunningham Ashe and Arthur Robert Ashe Sr.; his mother’s death before he was seven; his heart attack on July 31, 1979, while teaching tennis to inner-city youth, and the quadruple bypass that followed; a second bypass operation and a blood transfusion in 1983; his HIV diagnosis in 1988, which he at first kept private; his announcement on April 8, 1992, made to preempt a planned *USA Today* story; his death from AIDS on February 6, 1993; and the posthumous memoir *Days of Grace*. https://encyclopediavirginia.org/entries/ashe-arthur-1943-1993/
2. Lerner BH. “Arthur Ashe and AIDS: Did the public have the right to know his diagnosis?” *STAT*, June 26, 2022. Supports his atherosclerotic heart disease, which both parents also had at early ages; the 1979 heart attack and bypass; the second bypass in 1983 when chest pain recurred; the 1988 difficulty moving his right hand, the mass in the left side of his brain, its removal, and the diagnosis of toxoplasmosis, followed by testing that showed HIV and AIDS; his doctors’ conclusion that he was almost certainly infected during the 1983 operation, before blood was tested for the virus; that he told only a few family members and friends; and the press conference after *USA Today*’s inquiry. https://www.statnews.com/2022/06/26/arthur-ashe-and-aids-did-the-public-have-the-right-to-know-his-diagnosis/
3. Markel H. “Arthur Ashe’s other great serve: as activist for health.” PBS NewsHour, July 10, 2021. Supports the quadruple bypass in 1979 and the double bypass in 1983; that one of the transfusions during or after surgery carried HIV; right-arm paralysis in 1988 attributed to toxoplasmosis and a blood test showing HIV; his announcement on April 8, 1992; his mother’s death at 27 of complications from surgery and his father’s heart attacks at 55 and 59; and his death on February 6, 1993, of AIDS-related pneumonia. It dates his first heart attack to July 1976 (see the editorial resolutions). https://www.pbs.org/newshour/health/arthur-ashes-other-great-serve-as-activist-for-health
4. Biography.com Editors. “Arthur Ashe.” Biography.com (A&E Television Networks), April 2, 2014, updated November 16, 2021. Supports the quadruple bypass in 1979 and the second bypass in 1983, emergency brain surgery in 1988 after paralysis of his right arm, HIV acquired from a blood transfusion, and his death in New York City on February 6, 1993, from AIDS-related pneumonia. https://www.biography.com/athletes/arthur-ashe
5. HISTMED Master Roster v3. Internal project source. Its clinical claims were provisional and were not treated as independent corroboration.

## Editorial resolutions

- **Recent-death rule.** Ashe died in 1993. The case uses only what Ashe made public himself, at his April 1992 press conference and in his memoir, as reported in the four sources above, and what was reported publicly at his death. No medical records, family accounts beyond what the sources report, or speculation about undisclosed conditions are used. Claims about HIV are marked sensitive. No sick visit is added, and no illness other than the documented heart disease, toxoplasmosis, and HIV is introduced.
- **Index age and consistency with what he knew.** The index visit on January 12, 2026 maps to January 12, 1989, when he was 45: after his HIV diagnosis in 1988, so the diagnosis was known to him, and long before his public announcement in 1992. The synthetic date of birth is July 10, 1980 (shift 37 years). The chronic-care visits on February 9, April 13, July 13, and October 19, 2026 and the preventive visit on August 17, 2026 (age 46) map to the same days of 1989. The diagnostic admission (September 2025 in the modern frame) maps to September 1988; the sources date the operation only to 1988, and one search listing names September, which was not confirmed in a fetched source. Past medical history and family history include only events before the index date. His announcement and death are in the timeline only.
- **Heart-attack date.** Encyclopedia Virginia dates the heart attack to July 31, 1979, and STAT and Biography.com place the first bypass in 1979; the PBS essay dates a first heart attack to July 1976 at 33. The 1979 date is preferred and the PBS date is linked as a dispute on CLM-0002. The days of the two bypass operations in the modern frame (December 2016 and June 2020, mapping to December 1979 and June 1983) are synthetic; the sources give only the years.
- **Transfusion in the modern frame.** The documented route, a transfusion during the 1983 operation before blood was screened, is kept as history (**D**). In the modern frame the second operation falls in 2020, when transfusion-transmitted HIV is extremely rare. The episode does not explain this away: the notes say only that he understands he acquired HIV from the transfusion, and the consultation adds one synthetic line that the hospital reported the suspected transfusion-transmitted infection to the blood supplier at diagnosis, which is what a modern hospital would do.
- **Toxoplasmosis.** Sources agree that a left-sided brain mass caused right hand or arm weakness in 1988 and that the diagnosis was toxoplasmosis (PBS calls it fungal; it is a parasite, and the record treats it as one). In the modern frame the lesion is diagnosed by stereotactic biopsy rather than removal, before the HIV result returned. The recovery of the right hand, all counts and viral loads, the regimens, and their dates are synthetic. No source says what treatment he received in 1988, and none is described.
- **Problems.** HIV is coded B20, which applies after any AIDS-defining illness; toxoplasmosis B58.2 (Toxoplasma meningoencephalitis, the ICD-10-CM code for cerebral toxoplasmosis); coronary artery disease I25.10 and old myocardial infarction I25.2, linked to the documented history; hyperlipidemia E78.5 is synthetic. No behavioral-health diagnosis is coded, and the PHQ scores stay below the thresholds, so no structured safety assessment is required.
- **Person-first language.** The record says “person with HIV,” “acquired,” and “HIV infection,” not “victim,” “infected patient,” or “AIDS patient.” Disclosure is always his decision; the consultation and wellness notes keep results and the health care agent in the confidential part of the chart. His spouse and child are mentioned only as supports, and no relative’s health or decision is recorded in any visit note.
- **Synthetic content.** All visits, laboratory values, vital signs, vaccines, medicines, the ECG, the MRI and biopsy, the PHQ and AUDIT-C scores, his never-smoker status, his work routine, and his height (185 cm) are synthetic. The viral load is recorded as 0 when HIV-1 RNA is not detected (below 20 copies/mL), because the schema stores numbers only; the reference range text says so.
- **Preventive visit.** A periodic adult preventive visit (CPT 99396 with 99497 in plain text) was added at age 46. Judgment calls: (1) colorectal cancer screening by colonoscopy is ordered and anal cancer screening deferred to the next visit at his choice, with no results recorded; (2) the syphilis screen is ordered and no result value is recorded; (3) advance care planning names an unnamed health care agent and records only general priorities, including control over his own medical information, and no treatment preference.
- **Focused visits.** The four chronic-care follow-ups (four weeks, three months, six months, and nine months) are documented as focused visits on the DHHS and IAS-USA antiretroviral guidance, the DHHS opportunistic infection guidelines, the HIVMA/IDSA primary care guidance, and the CMID ischemic heart disease and hyperlipidemia monographs. Protocol deviations recorded instead of invented results: (1) the urine albumin test recommended at the start of tenofovir alafenamide were not done in the hospital or at intake (deferred at four weeks), and (2) the ordered urine sample was not collected at three months (deferred again); it was collected at six months and was normal, with no harm evident; (3) the lipid recheck after adding ezetimibe came at 13 weeks, one week outside the 4 to 12 week window, with no effect on care. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is stopped at nine months, when the CD4 count has been above 200 for more than six months with an undetectable viral load.
- **Consultation.** The infectious diseases referral and the consultation by Priyanka Rao, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the four-week visit, are **S**. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: the regimen continues unchanged; abacavir, boosted regimens, and efavirenz are avoided; maintenance therapy stops at nine months by the stated criteria; the three-monthly monitoring, the urine albumin test, the hepatitis B antibody check, the meningococcal, zoster, influenza, and COVID-19 vaccines, and the cancer screening at the preventive visit all follow.
- **Guideline URLs.** clinicalinfo.hiv.gov refuses automated requests (HTTP 403), so its pages could not be fetched. The antiretroviral guideline is cited through its Healthy People 2030 listing, which resolved; the opportunistic infection section URL (node 9358, the *Toxoplasma gondii* section) was confirmed through search-engine listings of the page and its Spanish version. The IAS-USA and HIVMA/IDSA documents were confirmed through PubMed.
