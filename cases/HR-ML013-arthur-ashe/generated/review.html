Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Recent-death rule. Ashe died in 1993. The case uses only what Ashe made public himself, at his April 1992 press conference and in his memoir, as reported in the four sources above, and what was reported publicly at his death. No medical records, family accounts beyond what the sources report, or speculation about undisclosed conditions are used. Claims about HIV are marked sensitive. No sick visit is added, and no illness other than the documented heart disease, toxoplasmosis, and HIV is introduced.
  2. Index age and consistency with what he knew. The index visit on January 12, 2026 maps to January 12, 1989, when he was 45: after his HIV diagnosis in 1988, so the diagnosis was known to him, and long before his public announcement in 1992. The synthetic date of birth is July 10, 1980 (shift 37 years). The chronic-care visits on February 9, April 13, July 13, and October 19, 2026 and the preventive visit on August 17, 2026 (age 46) map to the same days of 1989. The diagnostic admission (September 2025 in the modern frame) maps to September 1988; the sources date the operation only to 1988, and one search listing names September, which was not confirmed in a fetched source. Past medical history and family history include only events before the index date. His announcement and death are in the timeline only.
  3. Heart-attack date. Encyclopedia Virginia dates the heart attack to July 31, 1979, and STAT and Biography.com place the first bypass in 1979; the PBS essay dates a first heart attack to July 1976 at 33. The 1979 date is preferred and the PBS date is linked as a dispute on CLM-0002. The days of the two bypass operations in the modern frame (December 2016 and June 2020, mapping to December 1979 and June 1983) are synthetic; the sources give only the years.
  4. Transfusion in the modern frame. The documented route, a transfusion during the 1983 operation before blood was screened, is kept as history (D). In the modern frame the second operation falls in 2020, when transfusion-transmitted HIV is extremely rare. The episode does not explain this away: the notes say only that he understands he acquired HIV from the transfusion, and the consultation adds one synthetic line that the hospital reported the suspected transfusion-transmitted infection to the blood supplier at diagnosis, which is what a modern hospital would do.
  5. Toxoplasmosis. Sources agree that a left-sided brain mass caused right hand or arm weakness in 1988 and that the diagnosis was toxoplasmosis (PBS calls it fungal; it is a parasite, and the record treats it as one). In the modern frame the lesion is diagnosed by stereotactic biopsy rather than removal, before the HIV result returned. The recovery of the right hand, all counts and viral loads, the regimens, and their dates are synthetic. No source says what treatment he received in 1988, and none is described.
