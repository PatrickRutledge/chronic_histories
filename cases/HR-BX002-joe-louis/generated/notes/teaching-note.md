# Teaching Note — Joe Louis pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Joe Louis was born Joseph Louis Barrow on May 13, 1914, near LaFayette in Chambers County, Alabama, to Munroe and Lillie Barrow, Alabama sharecroppers; he was one of eleven children. His family moved to Detroit in the summer of 1926, when he was about twelve, and he took up boxing at a local gym there. He won the world heavyweight championship on June 22, 1937, by knocking out James J. Braddock, defended it about two dozen times (24 or 25 defenses in different sources), and retired as champion in 1949. He enlisted in the U.S. Army in January 1942 and served through the Second World War, staging 96 exhibition bouts for the troops and donating the purses of two 1942 fights, nearly $100,000, to the Army and Navy relief societies. Despite large career earnings, he faced a federal tax debt that by 1956 exceeded $1 million in back income tax, penalties and interest; the debt shaped his finances for many years, and the sources describe its resolution differently. In 1969 he collapsed on a Manhattan street, shortly after a television interview in New York in late June, and was hospitalized for what was described as a physical breakdown; he later said that the collapse resulted from cocaine use. In the same period he was troubled by fears of a plot against his life, which a reference biography calls paranoid delusions. In 1970 he was hospitalized for about five months in Denver, Colorado, for paranoid delusions, described in his wire-service obituary as treatment of an emotional disorder at the Veterans Administration Hospital in Denver. In his later years he worked as an official greeter at Caesars Palace in Las Vegas. In 1977 he had surgery with an arterial graft to correct an aortic aneurysm and afterwards used a wheelchair; he had strokes and heart problems in his last years and had a pacemaker implanted in Houston about five months before his death. He died of cardiac arrest on April 12, 1981, at 66, after collapsing at his home in the Las Vegas area, and was buried with full military honors at Arlington National Cemetery. He won 68 of his 71 professional bouts, 54 by knockout.
- **R:** Whether his symptoms in 1969 and 1970 amounted to a stimulant-induced psychosis, another psychotic illness, or a neurocognitive disorder after a long boxing career cannot be determined from the historical record.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
