Assessment

  • Cocaine use disorder, severe, with cocaine-induced psychotic disorder (F14.250). Seven DSM-5-TR criteria make a severe cocaine use disorder. The delusions began during heavy use, he has never had psychotic symptoms without cocaine, they are already fading after ten days, and the examination, laboratory tests and MRI show no other cause: this is a cocaine-induced psychotic disorder. A primary psychotic disorder becomes likelier only if symptoms persist beyond about a month without cocaine, and the diagnosis of schizophrenia, which is made too often in Black men, should not be made on a first presentation like this. [S]
  • Cognition after a long boxing career. A MoCA of 25 after three hours of sleep and during active psychosis does not show a neurocognitive disorder; the MRI is normal. Traumatic encephalopathy syndrome is a research diagnosis under the NINDS consensus criteria, not a clinical one, and chronic traumatic encephalopathy can be confirmed only after death, so neither label belongs in his record. The MoCA should be repeated when he has slept well and been well for some weeks. [S]
  • Antipsychotic. Symptoms that persist after ten days without cocaine and keep him from sleeping justify an antipsychotic, as the ASAM/AAAP guideline advises. Aripiprazole at a low dose has little effect on weight, glucose, prolactin and the QT interval, which suits a man with raised blood pressure and a borderline lipid profile. Watch for akathisia. After a period of remission, a gradual taper is advised for a stimulant-induced psychosis. [S]
  • Treatment of cocaine use disorder. No medicine is approved by the FDA for cocaine use disorder. Contingency management is the treatment with the strongest evidence and should be the core of his plan, with cognitive behavioral therapy alongside it. The off-label options in the guideline, such as topiramate or bupropion, can wait while he does well, and the stimulant-based ones (modafinil, or topiramate with extended-release amphetamine) are unsafe after a psychosis and with high blood pressure. [S]
  • Raised blood pressure and cardiovascular risk (R03.0). Cocaine raises blood pressure and the risk of heart attack, stroke, arrhythmia and aortic dissection. His blood pressure remains raised ten days after his last use, and the ECG shows left ventricular hypertrophy by voltage, so hypertension is likely; it should be confirmed with home readings once he has been without cocaine for some weeks. Beta-blockers are best avoided while he may still use cocaine. [S]
  • Harm reduction, safety and family. Fentanyl contaminates much of the illicit stimulant supply, and people who do not use opioids have died of it; naloxone and test strips are essential. He has no suicidal or homicidal ideation and no access to firearms, and he is safe to treat as an outpatient with weekly visits. Family can help, but only with his consent and on his terms. [S]

Plan of Treatment

  • Start aripiprazole 5 mg each morning; check weight, blood pressure, akathisia and abnormal movements at each visit, glucose and lipids at four months if still on it, and taper gradually after about six weeks of remission. [S]
  • Contingency management with twice-weekly urine tests and prize draws for twelve weeks, and weekly cognitive behavioral therapy. [S]
  • No medicine for cocaine use disorder for now; avoid stimulant-based options. [S]
  • Home blood pressure readings before the four-week visit; if hypertension is confirmed, a calcium channel blocker or thiazide first, an echocardiogram for left ventricular hypertrophy that also measures the aortic root, and no beta-blocker while he may use cocaine. [S]
