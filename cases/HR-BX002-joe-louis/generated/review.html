Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Sources. The New York Times obituary and the International Boxing Hall of Fame page could not be fetched, and the Smithsonian NMAAHC pages returned an error, so none is cited. Randy Roberts's Joe Louis: Hard Times Man was not read, and the reviews found do not discuss his cocaine use or paranoia, so nothing is drawn from it. The case relies on the 1981 UPI obituary, the Encyclopedia of Alabama, two Gale reference biographies reproduced on Encyclopedia.com, a U.S. Army article, and a Detroit Historical Society photo record.
  2. Recent-death rule. He died in 1981, so the rule (deaths after 1990) does not apply. Substance use and psychiatric claims are still marked sensitive (CLM-0006 to CLM-0009), and a sick visit is included.
  3. Birth and death. Born May 13, 1914, near LaFayette, Alabama (Encyclopedia of Alabama; UPI gives the date). Contemporary Black Biography gives Lexington, Alabama; it is not used. Death on April 12, 1981: UPI and Contemporary Black Biography give cardiac arrest, the Encyclopedia of Alabama a heart attack; the place is given as Las Vegas or Paradise, Nevada (CLM-0012). One search summary gave April 13; no fetched source does.
  4. Name. He was born Joseph Louis Barrow and was known throughout his public life as Joe Louis. The display name splits simply into given name Joe and family name Louis, so identity.name_parts is not used; the C-CDA and FHIR names are Joe Louis, the name he used, and the birth name is the alias “Joseph Louis Barrow,” which appears in no other record. “Louis” alone and “Barrow” alone are not aliases (“Barrow” appears in another record as part of an institute's name, and “Louis” in other records as other people's names). The press nickname is not used. The notes call him Mr. Louis.
  5. Index age and dates. The index visit on July 13, 2026 maps to July 13, 1969, when he was 55: about two to three weeks after his collapse in New York, dated to late June by the AP caption on a June 26 photograph. The synthetic date of birth is May 13, 1971 (shift 57). The visits on July 20, August 3, August 10, September 14, September 21 and October 26, 2026 map to the same days of 1969, before his documented hospitalization in Denver in 1970 (whose month is not given in the fetched sources). His whereabouts in those months are not documented in the sources read; the facility's town is not specified. Past medical history includes only events up to June 1969; the 1970 hospitalization, the 1977 surgery, the strokes and his death are linked only to later timeline events.
  6. Cocaine and paranoia. That he collapsed in 1969 and later said the collapse resulted from cocaine use is documented (CLM-0006, D, sensitive), as are his fears of a plot against his life, called paranoid delusions (CLM-0007, D, sensitive), and the five-month hospitalization in Denver in 1970 (CLM-0008, D, sensitive; UPI names the Veterans Administration Hospital, other sources a mental institution in Colorado). Whether this was a stimulant-induced psychosis or something else cannot be determined (CLM-0009, R, possible). The roster's specific persecutory content (the CIA, gas through vents) was not found in any fetched source and is not used. Search summaries said that family members arranged the 1970 admission; this is not used, and nothing is attributed to any relative. Claims that particular friends paid his medical bills were not verified and are not used.
  7. Cognition. The roster's “probable boxing-related cognitive decline” was not supported by a fetched source and is not used. His 71 professional bouts (CLM-0013) are the only anchor for weighing head impacts in the modern differential. The modern episode finds no neurocognitive disorder (MoCA 25 during symptoms, 27 after ten weeks; normal brain MRI) and says plainly that traumatic encephalopathy syndrome is a research diagnosis and chronic traumatic encephalopathy a post-mortem one; neither is coded.
  8. Aortic aneurysm. His 1977 surgery for an aortic aneurysm is documented (CLM-0011) but is after the index date. The modern echocardiogram measures the aortic root and ascending aorta because of stage 2 hypertension, left ventricular hypertrophy on the ECG and cocaine use, and finds them normal (3.6 and 3.5 cm); abdominal aortic ultrasound is recorded as not indicated under the USPSTF recommendation (men 65 to 75 who have ever smoked). The normal synthetic findings are not a claim about his real aorta in 1969.
  9. Tax debt. The debt is documented (CLM-0005); the sources differ on how it ended (paid down, cleared by the mid-1960s, or forgiven). The modern episode treats it as settled years ago and anchors only his money worries and lack of savings to it; a free Low Income Taxpayer Clinic review finds nothing owed. No current debt is claimed.
  10. Equity and dignity. He is a Black man and an Army veteran. The modern episode describes him with dignity and without stereotype, uses person-first language, records his strengths (service, generosity, discipline), and names the documented bias toward diagnosing schizophrenia in Black men as a reason for a careful, structured differential (GL-DISPARITY-2014). Amlodipine is chosen as a first-line drug that suits his hypertension without relying on race alone: he has no albuminuria or other indication for an ACE inhibitor, and beta-blockers are avoided because of cocaine.
  11. Relatives and others. His parents appear only in CLM-0001. His modern wife is unnamed and has no role; the family member he authorizes is not identified, and no conversation with any relative is recorded. His friends from boxing and the Army, the acquaintance at the lapse, the counselor and the social worker are synthetic and unnamed. No living person is named. Other boxers and public figures are not named in the modern episode.
  12. Synthetic clinical picture. Everything in the modern episode is synthetic: the cocaine history and DSM-5-TR count, every score (DAST-10, AUDIT-C, PHQ-9, PHQ-2, GAD-7, MoCA; the AIMS and Barnes scale are narrative), all laboratory values, the ECG, MRI and echocardiogram, the urine drug test, HIV, hepatitis, syphilis, influenza and COVID-19 test, stool test and PSA results (narrative except PSA), the medicines, the lapse and the outcomes. Height (187 cm) and weights are synthetic.
  13. Presenting problems. Persecutory thoughts and poor sleep (R41.89; the closest symptom code, since ICD-10-CM has no code for paranoid ideation alone) are the presenting problem, resolved at the consultation when the cocaine-induced psychotic disorder (F14.250) was diagnosed. Cocaine use disorder coded at intake (F14.20) is superseded at the consultation by the combined code, since ICD-10-CM codes the use disorder and the induced disorder together. The raised blood pressure at intake (R03.0) resolves into hypertension (I10) at four weeks, after home readings without cocaine.
  14. Clinical judgment calls. (1) Outpatient care for persecutory delusions with no suicidal or homicidal ideation, no firearms, intact self-care and weekly visits, with written criteria for urgent care. (2) No antipsychotic at intake, three days after cocaine, and aripiprazole 5 mg at ten days when the delusions persisted and kept him from sleeping; chosen for its low metabolic and QT risk. (3) Brain MRI for new psychosis after 50 with years of head impacts. (4) A taper after six weeks of remission (2 mg for two weeks), as the ASAM/AAAP guideline advises, with no return of symptoms. (5) No medicine for cocaine use disorder: contingency management and cognitive behavioral therapy; stimulant-based off-label options avoided after psychosis and with hypertension. (6) Naloxone and fentanyl test strips for a man who does not use opioids, because of fentanyl in the stimulant supply. (7) Amlodipine for stage 2 hypertension confirmed after four weeks without cocaine; no beta-blocker. (8) The one-day lapse is recorded honestly, confirmed by a urine test, and met without blame. (9) Family involvement only with his signed consent; in the end he chose to tell his family himself.
  15. Preventive visit. A periodic adult preventive visit at 55 (CPT 99396 and 99497 in plain text). Judgment calls: HIV, hepatitis B and C and syphilis tests at intake; a stool immunochemical test (negative); PSA after shared decision-making that includes the higher risk in Black men (1.3 ng/mL); lung cancer and abdominal aortic aneurysm screening not indicated; PREVENT 10-year risk about 6 percent, no statin; influenza, Tdap, PCV20 and the first hepatitis A and B doses, with zoster and the second hepatitis B dose at the fifteen-week visit; COVID-19 vaccine declined for now; advance care planning with an unnamed agent.
  16. Sick visit. A common cold two weeks into aripiprazole, before his blood pressure was treated, chosen because it is common, insinuates no undocumented diagnosis, and tests the plan: decongestants are avoided because they raise blood pressure, act as stimulants that can stir cravings, and (pseudoephedrine) can make a urine screen read falsely positive for amphetamine; no NSAID while his blood pressure is untreated; no antibiotic. The nasal septum is examined because of intranasal cocaine. It is not a historical claim.
  17. Focused visits. The four chronic-care follow-ups (one week with the consultation, four weeks, ten weeks and fifteen weeks) are documented as focused visits on the ASAM/AAAP 2023 guideline, SAMHSA TIP 33, the APA 2020 guideline (antipsychotic monitoring), the 2025 AHA/ACC blood pressure guideline, the CMID hypertension and depression monographs, the NINDS traumatic encephalopathy syndrome criteria and the VA/DoD suicide-risk guideline. One deviation is recorded instead of invented results: two contingency management urine tests were missed during a four-day trip in late August, and he used cocaine on one day after returning; he reported it, had no psychotic symptoms and stopped at once, and the corrective action was a travel plan with testing at a partner clinic and telephone therapy. The four-month glucose and lipid check on aripiprazole was not needed because the drug was stopped before then; this is recorded as not indicated. Synthetic findings introduced: mild akathisia in the first week of aripiprazole, the one-day lapse and the echocardiogram.
  18. Consultation. The addiction psychiatry referral and the consultation by Naomi Featherstone-Ibe, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the one-week visit, are S. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: a severe cocaine use disorder with a cocaine-induced psychotic disorder, not schizophrenia and not a neurocognitive disorder; aripiprazole with metabolic and movement monitoring, tapered after about six weeks of remission; contingency management and cognitive behavioral therapy with no medicine; home blood pressure readings, a calcium channel blocker if hypertension is confirmed, an echocardiogram measuring the aortic root and no beta-blocker; and naloxone, test strips, safety review and family involvement only with his consent.
