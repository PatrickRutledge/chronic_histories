## Editorial resolutions

- **Sources.** The International Boxing Hall of Fame page for Marciano could not be reached (connection refused), the New York Times archive could not be fetched, and Russell Sullivan's biography *Rocky Marciano: The Rock of His Times* was not read, directly or through a fetched quotation; no Brockton Historical Society page on his health was found. The record therefore rests on reference encyclopedias (Encyclopedia.com), HISTORY.com, Biography.com, two sports history features (VICE, ESPN Classic), Boxing News and the Iowa History Journal. Claims supported by a single feature article are graded D with certainty “probable” (CLM-0002, CLM-0006, CLM-0010).
- **Thin documented health history.** The documented health facts are few: pneumonia as a small child, broken hands, back injuries and cuts during his career, by one account a ruptured disc from 1949 and an arthritic right elbow from 1951, and a hospital diagnosis of a ruptured disc in July 1956. Nothing else about his health is documented in the sources read, and nothing is added to his history. The modern episode is built around common, defensible problems for a retired boxer: chronic low back pain with a recent disc herniation, thumb-base osteoarthritis, the metabolic change after heavy training stops, and a brain health baseline.
- **Name.** The display name is “Rocky Marciano”; the alias “Rocco Francis Marchegiano,” his birth name, appears in no other record. “Newton” appears only as the place of the crash. No other figure with a record in the dataset is named; his opponents other than Jersey Joe Walcott are not named.
- **Index age and dates.** The index visit on August 4, 2026 maps to August 4, 1956, when he was 32 and living in Brockton, three months after he announced his retirement and a few weeks after the July 1956 hospital diagnosis of a ruptured disc. The synthetic date of birth is September 1, 1993 (shift 70 years). The visits on August 18, September 8, September 22, September 29 and October 27, 2026 map to the same days of 1956, at ages 32 and 33, thirteen years before his death. The index setting is Brockton rather than Fort Lauderdale: the sources read place him in Brockton in 1956 and do not date a move to Florida.
- **Corrections to the brief.** (1) The brief gives his retirement at 32; HISTORY.com and Boxing News agree, while VICE and the UXL and World Encyclopedia entries say 31. Born September 1, 1923, he was 32 on April 27, 1956, and the record says 32. (2) The brief names “back problems and hand injuries” as documented; the sources support broken hands and back injuries in general terms only, not which hand or which bones, so the left thumb in the modern episode is a synthetic choice. A broken left thumb in his amateur days is reported in secondary summaries citing Sullivan, but no source that states it was read, so it is not a claim.
- **Disc dates (CLM-0006, CLM-0010).** ESPN Classic says he knew from August 1949 that he was fighting with a ruptured disc; VICE says a hospital diagnosed a ruptured disc in July 1956. Both are recorded, each with certainty “probable,” and the record does not try to reconcile them. The modern L4-L5 level, left side and MRI findings are synthetic.
- **Roster leads not used.** The roster's anxiety, fear of flying, and hoarding of cash are not documented in any source read; the only related facts found are that he was frugal, secretive about business, and often took rides on friends' private planes (CLM-0012). No anxiety, phobia or hoarding claim is made. As the brief asks, anxiety is screened in the modern episode: GAD-7 4 at intake and 2 at twelve weeks, minimal, with no diagnosis.
- **The crash (CLM-0013).** The crash is recorded on the timeline only, from the Iowa History Journal account of the investigation, HISTORY.com and Biography.com. Sources disagree on the time of the crash and whether it was near Newton or outside Des Moines; the record follows the Iowa History Journal (near Newton airport) and gives no time. The modern episode contains nothing that foreshadows it: no flying, travel or accident advice beyond routine seat-belt questions.
- **Family.** His father's work, his marriage and his two children are recorded as documented history (CLM-0001, CLM-0011). His daughter appears only in the documented July 1956 event about his own back; the modern episode says he hurt his back “bending to lift a child at home” and names no relative. No relative has a role, statement, decision or health fact in the modern episode; the health care agent is unnamed. The family history is empty because no health fact about any relative is documented.
- **Brain health (PRB-006).** No concussion, loss of consciousness or cognitive change is documented. The modern episode records a normal baseline after a long career with heavy sparring (MoCA 29, normal neurological screen, normal mood, anxiety and sleep screens), states that traumatic encephalopathy syndrome is a research diagnosis under the 2021 NINDS criteria and is not made on exposure alone, and orders no brain imaging. ICD-10-CM Z87.828 (personal history of other healed physical injury and trauma) is used as the closest code for the exposure history; no brain injury code is used.
- **Clinical judgment calls.** (1) Disc herniation with an improving L5 radiculopathy and no deficit: no repeat MRI and no surgical opinion, with clear triggers for both. (2) Naproxen as the drug, first-line under ACP 2017, for two weeks then as needed; no opioids or muscle relaxants. (3) Post-traumatic thumb-base osteoarthritis treated with a hand-based orthosis, hand exercises and topical diclofenac; no injection was needed. (4) Elevated blood pressure at intake confirmed as elevated, not hypertension, by a week of home readings, and managed with lifestyle change. (5) LDL 137 mg/dL at 32 with low 10-year risk: lifestyle therapy, no statin, repeat lipids in about five years. (6) Weight loss target of 5% to 7% over six months with an exercise prescription from the Physical Activity Guidelines. (7) No HPV vaccine at 33 by shared decision. (8) Dyspepsia under 60 without alarm features: no endoscopy; test and treat for Helicobacter pylori, with the stool sample collected before the first omeprazole dose.
- **Synthetic clinical picture.** Everything in the modern episode is synthetic: every result and score (laboratory tests, PHQ-9, PHQ-2, GAD-7, AUDIT-C, MoCA, PROMIS sleep, pain scores, Oswestry, FIHOA, grip strength), the outside MRI report, the hand X-ray, the examinations, the medicines, the weight change and the dyspepsia. Height (179 cm) and weights are synthetic.
- **Preventive visit.** A periodic adult preventive visit at 33 (CPT 99395 in plain text): HIV, hepatitis C and hepatitis B screening (negative), the first hepatitis B vaccine dose (the second at twelve weeks), influenza vaccine at twelve weeks, COVID-19 vaccine due, advance care planning; colorectal screening not yet due.
- **Sick visit.** Epigastric pain after about seven weeks of near-daily naproxen, chosen because it follows from the plan: the as-needed naproxen drifted into daily use. Hemoglobin was normal and there were no alarm features; naproxen was stopped, omeprazole given for four weeks, and the Helicobacter pylori stool antigen was negative. It is not a historical claim.
- **Focused visits.** The three chronic-care follow-ups (two weeks, with the consultation; eight weeks; twelve weeks) are documented as focused visits on the 2017 ACP low back pain guideline, the CMID osteoarthritis monograph with the 2019 ACR/AF guideline, the 2025 AHA/ACC blood pressure guideline with the CMID hypertension and hyperlipidemia monographs, the 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline, the Physical Activity Guidelines, the 2021 NINDS traumatic encephalopathy syndrome criteria, the ACG dyspepsia guideline and the USPSTF anxiety and depression recommendations. Two deviations are recorded instead of invented results: (1) the hand function questionnaire (FIHOA) was not given at intake; it was first given at the two-week visit, before the splint, so the pretreatment baseline was kept; (2) at the preventive visit he said he was taking naproxen most days and this was not acted on; dyspepsia followed without bleeding; the corrective action was to stop naproxen, give omeprazole, and check the dose actually taken at every visit for any oral NSAID. Synthetic findings introduced: the dyspepsia and the improving neurological signs.
- **Consultation.** The sports medicine referral and the consultation by Tobias Achterberg-Lund, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the two-week visit, are **S**. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: no imaging or surgical opinion, with triggers; extension-based exercise therapy and a graded return to jogging; a thumb orthosis, hand exercises and topical diclofenac, with injection held in reserve; a yearly brain health baseline without imaging, with traumatic encephalopathy syndrome explained as a research diagnosis; and an exercise and weight plan. His advice to report daily naproxen use was not followed up at the preventive visit, which the eight-week visit records as a deviation.
