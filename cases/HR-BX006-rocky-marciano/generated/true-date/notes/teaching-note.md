# Teaching Note — Rocky Marciano pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Rocco Francis Marchegiano was born on September 1, 1923, in Brockton, Massachusetts, where his father, Pierino, worked in a shoe factory; he later boxed as Rocky Marciano. As a small child he had pneumonia and was not expected to survive. He was drafted into the Army in 1943, served in Britain, and completed his service in March 1946 at Fort Lewis, Washington; he took up boxing in the Army. He turned professional in March 1947 and won the world heavyweight championship on September 23, 1952, in Philadelphia, knocking out Jersey Joe Walcott in the thirteenth round. Broken hands, back injuries and many facial cuts were among the injuries he had during his career. By one account he knew from August 1949 that he was fighting with a ruptured disc in his back, and from 1951 he had an arthritic right elbow that left his right arm slightly shorter than the left. He was unbeaten as a professional, retiring with 49 wins in 49 bouts, 43 by knockout; he is the only heavyweight champion to retire with a perfect record. He trained exceptionally hard: more than once he sparred about 250 rounds to prepare for a fight, about 100 more than usual, and he limited his diet. On April 27, 1956, at 32, he announced his retirement as undefeated heavyweight champion, saying he wanted to spend more time with his family. Accounts also mention that he was weary of training, at odds with his manager over money, did not want to fight past his prime, and had an ailing back. In July 1956, after he felt pain in his back while playing with his three-year-old daughter, he checked into a hospital and was diagnosed with a ruptured disc in his back. He married in 1950; he and his wife had a daughter and later adopted a son. After boxing he earned a living from personal appearances and business interests, hosted a weekly boxing program on television in 1961, and worked for many years as a referee and boxing commentator. He was frugal and often accepted rides from friends with private planes. On August 31, 1969, the eve of his 46th birthday, he was killed when the small private plane in which he was a passenger, a Cessna 172 flying at night in bad weather, struck a tree in a pasture about two miles short of the airport at Newton, Iowa; the pilot and another passenger also died. Investigators found that the pilot, who had limited night experience and no instrument rating, attempted a flight beyond his experience and became spatially disoriented.
