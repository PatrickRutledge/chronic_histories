# Teaching Note — Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was born in Salzburg on January 27, 1756, the son of Leopold Mozart, a musician in the service of the Archbishop of Salzburg. Of the seven children of his parents' marriage only two survived: his sister Maria Anna, born July 30, 1751, and Wolfgang. In Vienna in late 1762, on the family's second journey, an attack of scarlet fever kept him in bed for a fortnight. At The Hague in the autumn of 1765, after his sister's dangerous illness, he was seized with a violent attack of fever that reduced him to extreme weakness for several weeks; he kept writing on a board laid across his bed. In 1767 the family fled a smallpox outbreak in Vienna to Olmütz, but he and then his sister caught smallpox there; the family was taken in by Count Leopold Anton von Podstatzky, Dean of Olmütz, and his illness was so severe that he lay blind for nine days and had to protect his eyes for several weeks afterwards. In the spring of 1783 he fell ill with an illness his biographer calls cholera, during an epidemic in Vienna. In a letter to his daughter dated September 14, 1784, Leopold Mozart wrote that his son had been very ill in Vienna: he had become overheated at Paisiello's new opera and had gone out into the open air to find the servant who had his overcoat, and this "brought on rheumatic fever, which without careful attention might have turned to typhus." He quoted his son's letter: "I have had raging colic every day for a fortnight at the same hour, accompanied by violent vomiting," and that his doctor, Sigmund Barisani, visited him almost daily. In 1784, up to the time of the episode, he lived at Graben 29 in Vienna. From October 1784 to April 1787 he lived at Domgasse 5 in Vienna. His mother died in Paris on July 3, 1778, while traveling with him, after an illness of some weeks during which she was bled; the cause is not recorded in the cited source. He was appointed imperial chamber musician on December 7, 1787, with a salary of 800 gulden a year, but despite constant work he was in financial difficulty; from 1788 he repeatedly asked his fellow Freemason Michael Puchberg, a merchant, for loans, and at his death he owed Puchberg about a thousand florins. In April 1789 he also borrowed a hundred florins from Franz Hofdemel. He was in Prague in September 1791 for the coronation opera La clemenza di Tito, first performed there on September 6, 1791, and returned to Vienna. Around November 20, 1791, he became seriously ill and took to his bed in his apartment in the Rauhensteingasse in Vienna. According to the accounts of his widow and his sister-in-law Sophie Haibel, his hands and feet began to swell, he had partial paralysis and violent vomiting, and he remained conscious through a fortnight in bed; his physician Dr. Closset called Dr. Sallaba, chief physician at the hospital, into consultation on November 28. He died at about one o'clock in the morning on December 5, 1791, aged 35. A letter from Prague of December 12 reported that "dropsy set in" and that the swelling of his body after death led to suspicions of poisoning. The official record of his death gives the cause as "hitzigen Friesel Fieber" (hitziges Frieselfieber, an acute fever with a miliary eruption). His body was blessed at St. Stephen's Cathedral and buried in St. Marx cemetery outside the city, not in a mass grave but in a general, simple grave that was open to all social classes regardless of rank; no stone marked it, and its exact place is unknown.
- **R:** A 2009 study of the official Vienna death register for November 1791 to January 1792 and the same months of the years before and after found that deaths from edema were markedly increased among younger men in the weeks around his death, a minor epidemic that may have begun in the military hospital; the authors concluded that their analysis is consistent with his last illness being a streptococcal infection leading to an acute nephritic syndrome from post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, with scarlet fever less likely. One author argues that he had frequent attacks of tonsillitis throughout his life and that in 1784 he developed a post-streptococcal Schönlein-Henoch syndrome. The same author proposes that the 1784 illness caused chronic glomerulonephritis and chronic kidney failure, that the fatal illness was Schönlein-Henoch purpura with death from cerebral hemorrhage and bronchopneumonia, and that bloodletting may have contributed to his death. Many other causes of his final illness have been proposed, including kidney failure and trichinosis (argued in a 2001 review titled "What killed Mozart?"), and none has been established. In his last weeks he told his wife that he believed he had been poisoned, and rumors of poisoning circulated after his death, including a later accusation against Antonio Salieri. His nineteenth-century biographer calls the accusation a calumny, rebutted at the time by Carpani and Neukomm; no evidence supports poisoning.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
