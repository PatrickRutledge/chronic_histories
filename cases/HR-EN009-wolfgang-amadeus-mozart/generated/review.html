Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Index age and whereabouts. The modern episode presents him at 28. The synthetic date of birth is 1998-01-27 (the true month and day), so the shift is 242 years. The index visit is September 15, 2026 (true frame September 15, 1784), the day after the date of his father's letter describing his illness in Vienna, and the last chronic-care visit is October 27, 2026 (true frame October 27, 1784). The Wien Geschichte Wiki places him at Graben 29 until October 1784 and at Domgasse 5 from October 1784, so he was in Vienna throughout; the synthetic move "to a larger apartment at the end of the month" echoes the move to the Domgasse. Index age 35 in 1791 was considered and not chosen, because he was in Prague for part of September 1791 and no illness before the final one is documented that year.
  2. Anchor and modern diagnosis. The 1784 illness is documented (D) only through his father's letter, which calls it rheumatic fever and quotes his account of daily colic and violent vomiting; his own letter is lost. The synthetic episode makes the modern diagnosis acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, chosen because the strongest modern analysis of his final illness (Zegers 2009) points to it, and because it lets the record teach the distinction from acute rheumatic fever, the term his father used. Acute rheumatic fever was considered as the modern diagnosis and not chosen; it is instead assessed and excluded with the Jones criteria and an echocardiogram. Neither choice is a claim about what he had in 1784 or what killed him.
  3. Presenting problem. The acute nephritic picture at intake is its own problem (PRB-001, coded R60.1, generalized edema), resolved at the consultation two days later, when the diagnosis (PRB-003) is recorded. The intake results (low C3, raised ASO and anti-DNase B) are shown as same-day urgent results; the intake documents call the cause probable, not confirmed.
  4. Cause of death. Not documented. The death record's "hitzigen Friesel Fieber" is an eighteenth-century description of fever with a rash, not a modern diagnosis. Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis in an epidemic (R, possible), Schönlein-Henoch purpura with chronic glomerulonephritis (R, disputed), and kidney failure and trichinosis (R, disputed) are recorded as claims only. Rheumatic fever as the cause of death was not found in a fetched reputable source and is not recorded as a claim. No rash in the final illness is recorded, because the fetched accounts describe swelling and vomiting and do not describe a rash; the death-record wording is quoted as it stands.
  5. Poisoning. His belief that he had been poisoned and the later rumors, including the accusation against Salieri, are recorded once (CLM-0018, R, disputed) and marked unsupported: his biographer calls the accusation a calumny rebutted at the time, and no evidence supports it.
  6. Burial. The roster's "unmarked pauper's grave" is not used. The City of Vienna's historical encyclopedia states that he was buried not in a mass grave but in a general simple grave open to all classes regardless of rank. The 1882 biography's framing of the grave as an economy is not repeated.
  7. Money. His debts and requests for loans from 1788 are documented (D) and come after the episode; they appear in the timeline and claims only. The roster's statement that he died of an infection he could not afford to treat was not found in any fetched source and is not used. The synthetic episode gives him health insurance and records only the worry about lost earnings during illness.
  8. Real people. His wife, sister-in-law, father, and physicians appear only in documented claims. The modern episode names no relatives, records no health facts about his wife, and names his health care agent only as "a trusted friend".
  9. Alias. No alias is recorded; the baptismal name in tourism sources was not confirmed in a source of the required standard.
  10. Not used. The full text of Davies 1983 and Zegers 2009 could not be fetched; only the abstracts were used. Childhood illnesses with joint pain that some authors call rheumatic fever were not found in the fetched sources; only the illnesses named by his biographer are recorded. The date of Paisiello's opera was not confirmed and is not given.
  11. Sick and preventive visits. A periodic adult preventive visit (age 28) on October 13, 2026 (true frame October 13, 1784) and a same-day sick visit on October 22, 2026 (true frame October 22, 1784) were added. The sick visit is a viral cold, chosen because it interacts with the recovering nephritis: it teaches when not to test for group A streptococcus, avoiding NSAIDs, and the difference between synpharyngitic hematuria (IgA nephropathy) and a post-streptococcal relapse. It does not echo a documented illness.
  12. Focused visits. The three chronic-care follow-ups (two days, two weeks, and six weeks) are documented as focused visits on the KDIGO 2021 glomerular diseases guideline, the AHA 2015 Jones criteria statement, the IDSA 2012 pharyngitis guideline, and the CMID chronic kidney disease and hypertension monographs. All results, the ECG, and the normal echocardiogram are synthetic. The rapid antigen test, red cell casts, and Jones criteria tally are recorded in the notes only, because they are not numeric results. One protocol deviation is recorded: the creatinine and potassium check planned for about a week after the consultation was missed; the next results showed no harm, and blood tests were then booked for the morning of each visit.
  13. Consultation. The nephrology consultation by the fictional Dr. Okonkwo-Brandt on the second visit is synthetic. Its recommendations (outpatient supportive care, furosemide until euvolemic, salt and fluid limits, completing penicillin, no NSAIDs, no biopsy unless the course becomes atypical, no rheumatic fever prophylaxis, and C3, kidney, and urine monitoring) are those the later visits carry out; the missed one-week laboratory check is recorded as a deviation.
  14. Mozart died in 1791, so the recent-death review rule does not apply. Ordinary clinical and provenance reviews remain required.

CLM-0014A 2009 study of the official Vienna death register for November 1791 to January 1792 and the same months of the years before and after found that deaths from edema were markedly increased among younger men in the weeks around his death, a minor epidemic that may have begun in the military hospital; the authors concluded that their analysis is consistent with his last illness being a streptococcal infection leading to an acute nephritic syndrome from post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, with scarlet fever less likely.possible

CLM-0016The same author proposes that the 1784 illness caused chronic glomerulonephritis and chronic kidney failure, that the fatal illness was Schönlein-Henoch purpura with death from cerebral hemorrhage and bronchopneumonia, and that bloodletting may have contributed to his death.disputed
CLM-0017Many other causes of his final illness have been proposed, including kidney failure and trichinosis (argued in a 2001 review titled "What killed Mozart?"), and none has been established.disputed
