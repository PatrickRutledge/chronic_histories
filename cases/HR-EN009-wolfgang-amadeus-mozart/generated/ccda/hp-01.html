Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was born in Salzburg on January 27, 1756, the son of Leopold Mozart, a musician in the service of the Archbishop of Salzburg. Of the seven children of his parents' marriage only two survived: his sister Maria Anna, born July 30, 1751, and Wolfgang. [D]
  • In Vienna in late 1762, on the family's second journey, an attack of scarlet fever kept him in bed for a fortnight. [D]
  • At The Hague in the autumn of 1765, after his sister's dangerous illness, he was seized with a violent attack of fever that reduced him to extreme weakness for several weeks; he kept writing on a board laid across his bed. [D]
  • In 1767 the family fled a smallpox outbreak in Vienna to Olmütz, but he and then his sister caught smallpox there; the family was taken in by Count Leopold Anton von Podstatzky, Dean of Olmütz, and his illness was so severe that he lay blind for nine days and had to protect his eyes for several weeks afterwards. [D]
  • In the spring of 1783 he fell ill with an illness his biographer calls cholera, during an epidemic in Vienna. [D]
  • In a letter to his daughter dated September 14, 1784, Leopold Mozart wrote that his son had been very ill in Vienna: he had become overheated at Paisiello's new opera and had gone out into the open air to find the servant who had his overcoat, and this "brought on rheumatic fever, which without careful attention might have turned to typhus." He quoted his son's letter: "I have had raging colic every day for a fortnight at the same hour, accompanied by violent vomiting," and that his doctor, Sigmund Barisani, visited him almost daily. [D]
  • In 1784, up to the time of the episode, he lived at Graben 29 in Vienna. [D]
  • His mother died in Paris on July 3, 1778, while traveling with him, after an illness of some weeks during which she was bled; the cause is not recorded in the cited source. [D]
  • One author argues that he had frequent attacks of tonsillitis throughout his life and that in 1784 he developed a post-streptococcal Schönlein-Henoch syndrome. [R]
