# Teaching Note — Joe Frazier pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Joe Frazier was born on January 12, 1944, in Beaufort County, South Carolina, the eleventh child of a sharecropping family. As a teenager he moved to Philadelphia, where he had relatives, and worked in a slaughterhouse, where he punched sides of beef in the cold room. He won the heavyweight gold medal at the 1964 Olympic Games in Tokyo, fighting the final with a broken left thumb. Later in 1964 he learned that he had a cataract in his left eye; visually impaired, he turned professional anyway. The cataract grew worse, and by 1975, according to his eye doctor, his vision was 20/50 in the right eye and 20/400 in the left. He became undisputed world heavyweight champion in February 1970 and held the title until January 1973, defending it four times; he retired in 1976 after a professional record of 32 wins, 4 losses and 1 draw, with 27 knockouts, and returned for a single bout in 1981. His three-fight rivalry in the 1970s included a 1971 victory that handed his opponent his first professional defeat; in the third fight, in Manila in 1975, he could no longer see, and his trainer would not let him come out for the fifteenth round. In later life he lived in Philadelphia, where he owned and ran a boxing gym, and he had financial trouble. During his life it was reported publicly that he had diabetes and high blood pressure. In October 2011 he was diagnosed with liver cancer; he was in and out of the hospital from early October and received hospice care at home in his last week. He died on November 7, 2011, in Philadelphia, at 67, after a brief illness with liver cancer. He published an autobiography, Smokin' Joe, in 1996.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
