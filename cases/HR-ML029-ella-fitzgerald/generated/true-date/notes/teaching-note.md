# Teaching Note — Ella Fitzgerald pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Ella Jane Fitzgerald was born on April 25, 1917, in Newport News, Virginia, to Temperance and William Fitzgerald. Most biographies gave 1918, but her birth certificate and school records show 1917. In 1932 her mother died suddenly of a heart attack; orphaned at 15, she was placed in the Colored Orphan Asylum in Riverdale, New York. In 1934 she won an amateur contest at the Apollo Theater in Harlem, which brought her to the attention of the drummer and bandleader Chick Webb; she made her first recording with his band in 1935, and her 1938 swing version of the nursery rhyme "A-Tisket, A-Tasket" became her first hit. She won the Grammy Award for Best Jazz Individual Performance in 1958 for Ella Fitzgerald Sings the Duke Ellington Song Book, and received the Kennedy Center award for lifetime achievement in 1979. She was known as America's first lady of song. She was awarded the National Medal of Freedom in 1992. She began to have health problems during the 1970s; she had diabetes, which caused problems with her eyes. In the 1970s, after cataract surgery, she returned to touring more than 40 weeks a year. In 1986 she was admitted to intensive care for heart trouble and underwent quintuple-bypass heart surgery; the following year she was back onstage. She had been suffering from diabetes and its circulatory system complications for many years before her death. In 1993 both of her legs were amputated below the knees because of complications of diabetes. In 1993 she created and funded the Ella Fitzgerald Charitable Foundation, whose areas of interest include medical care and research with an emphasis on diabetes, vision problems, and heart disease. In 1989, asked how she spent her convalescence, she said: "Staying home and being bored. I miss the road. I miss going overseas." She died at home in Beverly Hills, California, on June 15, 1996, at 79. The exact cause of death was not released; one public timeline attributes it to complications of diabetes.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
