# Teaching Note — Gilda Radner pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Gilda Radner was born on June 28, 1946, into a prosperous Detroit Jewish family; her paternal grandfather had emigrated from Lithuania. She was the first person cast in Saturday Night Live, performed with its original troupe from 1975 to 1980, won an Emmy Award in 1978, and made several films, including three with Gene Wilder, whom she married in 1984. Her father died of brain cancer when she was fourteen. Her grandmother, a cousin, and an aunt had all had ovarian cancer. Her illness began with severe fatigue, which sources date to the filming of Haunted Honeymoon in England in 1985 or to early 1986; her internist attributed it to Epstein-Barr virus and told her not to worry. Over the following months she had bloating so marked that she could barely button her trousers, cramps and pelvic pain, fevers, and shaking legs; one doctor thought it was depression and another constipation, and gynecologists said the cramps were nothing to worry about. In her memoir she wrote that she now had 'Epstein-Barr virus and mittelschmerz.' It was ten months from her first symptoms before ovarian cancer was found, and the CA-125 blood test was never done. She was diagnosed with stage IV ovarian cancer in October 1986; surgery removed a tumor the size of a grapefruit, and chemotherapy and radiation therapy followed. Before her illness she and her husband tried to have a child; she had two miscarriages, one during the filming of Haunted Honeymoon in London, and an unsuccessful round of in vitro fertilization, after which doctors told her she was infertile. During her Saturday Night Live years her lifelong insecurity about her appearance resurfaced in bouts of bulimia, which she later wrote about. During treatment she found emotional support at The Wellness Community, through the psychotherapist Joanna Bull, and wrote her autobiography, It's Always Something, between chemotherapy treatments; it was published in 1989. After a remission, her cancer returned in December 1988. In May 1989 she was admitted to Cedars-Sinai Medical Center in Los Angeles for a CT scan, did not regain consciousness after sedation, and died there three days later, on May 20, 1989, at 42. After her death, Gilda's Club was founded in her honor by Gene Wilder, Joanna Bull and Joel Siegel, with the first clubhouse opening in 1995; it later merged with The Wellness Community to form the Cancer Support Community. Wilder testified to a House subcommittee about ovarian cancer, a detection center at Cedars-Sinai was named for her, and in 1990 the familial ovarian cancer registry at Roswell Park was named for her.
- **R:** After her death her husband said that no doctor had asked about her family history of ovarian cancer, and wrote that her cancer might have been caught in time if her doctors had given her the CA-125 blood test or asked about that history, and that she 'didn't have to die.'
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
