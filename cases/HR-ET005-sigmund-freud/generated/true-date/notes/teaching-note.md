# Teaching Note — Sigmund Freud pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Sigmund Freud was born Sigismund Schlomo Freud on May 6, 1856, in Freiberg, Moravia, now in the Czech Republic; in 1860 his family settled in Vienna. In the 1880s he experimented with cocaine himself, praised it in his paper On Coca (1884), and urged others to use it before he realized it could be addictive. According to his personal physician's account, as summarized in a 2019 review, he still used cocaine regularly in the 1890s. He began smoking in his twenties, first cigarettes and then cigars, following the example of his father, and often smoked about twenty cigars a day; his biographer described it as an addiction rather than a habit. In 1894, at 38, his friend the physician Wilhelm Fliess attributed his irregular heartbeat to smoking and told him to stop. He tried repeatedly and failed: one attempt ended within seven weeks, and a later one lasted fourteen months before he started again, describing the abstinence as torture. In 1923, at 67, he was diagnosed with cancer of the right upper jaw and palate, a squamous cell carcinoma; a first excision in April 1923 was followed by radiation treatment. Later in 1923 the oral surgeon Hans Pichler performed a radical operation that removed part of the upper jaw and soft palate, and made the obturator prosthesis that closed the defect; Freud called it "the monster." Pichler provided most of his later prosthetic care. Over the sixteen years after the diagnosis he had more than thirty operations and procedures (33 or 34 are reported), radiation treatment, and constant pain from his mouth. He kept smoking cigars after the cancer was diagnosed and refused to give them up. At 73, after a heart illness, he stopped smoking for 23 days and then gradually started again. After the Nazi annexation of Austria in 1938 he was forced to flee Vienna; he arrived in London as a refugee on June 6, 1938, and spent the last year of his life there. He died in London on September 23, 1939, at 83, after his physician Max Schur, at his request, gave him morphine.
- **R:** One retrospective analysis has proposed that his destructive palatal disease might have been a cocaine-induced midline lesion rather than cancer; most accounts and his medical records describe a squamous cell carcinoma caused mainly by smoking.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
