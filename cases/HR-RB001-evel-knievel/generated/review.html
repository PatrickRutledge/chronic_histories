Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Recent-death rule. Knievel died in 2007, so the rule applies. The case uses only what was reported in obituaries and record-keeper accounts (the Associated Press, ESPN, Reuters, the Deseret News and Guinness World Records) and his own words in a newspaper interview quoted by Reuters. No sick visit is added. His death is recorded once (CLM-0009), in plain words. Two claims are marked sensitive: the Reuters report that he did not stop drinking until his 1999 transplant (CLM-0006) and the implanted drug pump for pain in his last year (CLM-0008).
  2. Alcohol. The roster lists “alcohol use disorder.” No source fetched gives that diagnosis, so none is made: there is no F10 code, and alcohol appears only in the social history, as AUDIT-C scores and as counseling, all synthetic and anchored to the obituary report. The modern episode has him cut down after counseling and stop when treatment starts; this is a synthetic course and implies nothing about his real drinking. No safety assessment is required (no behavioral-health code; PHQ-9 at most 6; PHQ-2 at most 1).
  3. What is not public. When his hepatitis C was diagnosed, the stage of his liver disease before the transplant, and any treatment he had were not made public; one secondary page dates the diagnosis to 1993, but it is not a reliable source and is not used. Every clinical detail in the modern episode (the presenting tests, genotype 1a, the viral load, liver stiffness, Child-Pugh and MELD-Na, carvedilol, sofosbuvir-velpatasvir and cure, osteoporosis, all values) is synthetic. The modern course ends in cure with compensated cirrhosis, as current treatment allows; it does not describe his real course, in which he needed a liver transplant three years after the true-frame index date.
  4. Fractures. CLM-0004 records the conflict: Guinness World Records lists 433 bone fractures by the end of 1975 under “most broken bones in a lifetime,” while the Associated Press obituary says nearly 40 broken bones before he retired in 1980. The claim is graded D with certainty “disputed,” and the modern episode says “dozens of fractures.” Other counts reported on Wikipedia, including his own lower estimate, are not cited.
  5. Conflicts and gaps. (1) The Caesars Palace crash is dated 1968 in the wire obituaries; the jump is usually dated to New Year’s Eve 1967, and later accounts dispute the month-long coma; CLM-0002 is graded probable and uses the obituaries’ year. (2) Cause of death: the roster says pulmonary fibrosis caused his death; the obituaries fetched report natural causes after years of diabetes and pulmonary fibrosis, so CLM-0009 states that. (3) The year of the pulmonary fibrosis diagnosis (2005 on Wikipedia, without a fetched source) and the dates of the two strokes are not stated; both conditions came after the index age and are not used in the modern episode. (4) The liver transplant hospital and month are not stated (secondary sources not used). (5) The New York Times obituary, Britannica and Smithsonian pages returned access errors and are not cited. (6) Hepatitis C is described as “likely contracted through a blood transfusion”; the modern episode treats transfusion before 1992 as the likely source, as the obituaries do.
  6. Left out. His 1977 assault conviction is not medical and is left out. The lawsuit, finances and family history reported in the obituaries are left out. No relative is named; the sons and granddaughter quoted in the obituaries have no role, and no health fact, statement or decision is attributed to any relative. The modern family history is left empty. The roster’s “PTSD from career injuries” has no source and is not used.
  7. Alias. “Robert Craig Knievel” is used as the alias; it appears in no other record. “Bob” and “Robert” alone are not used.
  8. Index age and dates. The index visit on January 12, 2026 maps to January 12, 1996, when he was 57, three years before the 1999 transplant. The synthetic date of birth is October 17, 1968 (shift 30 years). The chronic-care visits on January 26, February 23, May 18 and August 17, 2026 and the preventive visit on September 21, 2026 (age 57, so a periodic adult preventive visit) map to the same days of 1996, within his lifetime and before the transplant. The presenting-problem pattern is used: raised transaminases with low platelets (R74.01) are a resolved problem when hepatitis C with cirrhosis is diagnosed at the consultation. The claims about the transplant, drinking until the transplant, his last years and his death are linked to timeline events after the index date, so the intake documents do not show them.
  9. Diabetes and pain at the index age. His diabetes and chronic pain are documented only for his last years. Placing type 2 diabetes on metformin and chronic pain from old fractures at 57 is a synthetic choice that serves the teaching objective; both problems are linked to the documented claims and marked synthetic.
  10. Synthetic clinical picture and teaching points. Cirrhosis is diagnosed without biopsy (FIB-4 4.94, liver stiffness 26.8 kPa, nodular liver, splenomegaly, platelets 118). Sofosbuvir-velpatasvir was chosen over glecaprevir-pibrentasvir because the latter should not be given with atorvastatin; the proton pump inhibitor is stopped. Liver stiffness of 25 kPa or more establishes clinically significant portal hypertension under Baveno VII, so carvedilol is started and screening endoscopy is not done; this differs from the endoscopic surveillance named on the AASLD-IDSA page and follows the newer Baveno VII and AASLD 2024 portal hypertension guidance. Transplant evaluation is not indicated (compensated; MELD-Na 8, then 6). The A1c reads below the glucose management indicator, as it can in cirrhosis, so a continuous glucose monitor guides diabetes care. Osteoporosis is treated with zoledronic acid rather than an oral bisphosphonate because of portal hypertension. Pain care avoids oral NSAIDs and opioids.
