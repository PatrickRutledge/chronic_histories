Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Sources. The Mark Twain Project's Autobiography and the Library of Congress pages were not read; one Mark Twain Project letter was read. The New York Times is not fetched directly (the site refuses automated access); its 1905, 1906 and 1910 articles are used through TwainQuotes.com transcriptions, and the 1910 obituary, which its transcriber flags as inaccurate, only for points other sources support or for contemporary opinion. No peer-reviewed article on his own health could be fetched (a medical-humanities essay in Hektoen International refused access; the PubMed records found concern his writing or his wife's health), so none is cited. Mark Twain Day by Day (David Fears, digital edition via the Center for Mark Twain Studies) is used for dates and for quotations from his letters and notebooks that it attributes to the Mark Twain Project.
  2. Recent-death rule. He died in 1910, so the rule does not apply. Claims about his bereavements and mood are marked sensitive (CLM-0004, CLM-0006 to CLM-0008, CLM-0015), and a sick visit is included.
  3. Birth and death. Born November 30, 1835, in Florida, Missouri (Mark Twain House; New York Times). Died April 21, 1910, at Stormfield, Redding, Connecticut, of angina pectoris (Associated Press via the University of Virginia; New York Times; Mark Twain House). The Times gives 6:22 p.m. and the AP 6:30 p.m.; the time is not used.
  4. Name. The display name is Mark Twain, his pen name and the name by which he is known. His legal name was Samuel Langhorne Clemens, so identity.name_parts codes given names “Samuel Langhorne” and family name “Clemens”: the C-CDA and FHIR patient names carry the legal name, and the FHIR name text keeps “Mark Twain,” as in other records whose patients are known by a pen name or chosen name. The notes call him Mr. Clemens. The aliases “Samuel Langhorne Clemens” and “Samuel Clemens” appear in no other record; “Clemens” and “Twain” alone are not used as aliases.
  5. Index age and dates. The index visit on June 12, 2026 maps to June 12, 1905, when he was 69: a year and a week after his wife's death on June 5, 1904, which satisfies the DSM-5-TR requirement that at least twelve months have passed. The synthetic date of birth is November 30, 1956 (shift 121). The visits on June 19, July 17, July 31, September 11, September 25 and October 16, 2026 map to the same days of 1905, when he was summering at Dublin, New Hampshire (May 18 to October 21, 1905; Day by Day). No visit falls between August 10 and 29, when he was away in Boston, Hartford and Norfolk, Connecticut. The modern episode places a gout flare two days after he left, treated by an unnamed doctor where he was staying; this matches the documented severe attack of gout in Norfolk in August 1905 (CLM-0012), but the modern treatment is synthetic and is not attributed to any real physician.
  6. What is documented and what is interpretation. Documented (D): his bereavements (CLM-0004, CLM-0006, CLM-0007, later CLM-0015), bankruptcy and the lecture tour (CLM-0005), smoking from the age of eight (CLM-0009), gout from about 1894 (CLM-0010) and in August 1905 (CLM-0012), bronchitis in 1906 (CLM-0013), chest pain from 1909 that he called a “tobacco heart” (CLM-0014) and death from angina pectoris (CLM-0016). That the losses preyed upon his mind is a contemporary opinion; whether he had a depressive illness is graded R, possible (CLM-0008). The roster's “probable bipolar” lead is not supported by any source read and is not used: no bipolar disorder or depressive disorder is assigned, and the modern diagnosis is prolonged grief disorder only. His later chest pain, bronchitis and Jean's death are linked only to later timeline events.
  7. Grief scale. The brief asked for the PG-13-R or another validated grief scale as a coded survey. LOINC has no code for the PG-13-R or any other prolonged grief scale (searched on the terminology server), and the C-CDA generator codes every observation as LOINC, so the PG-13-R scores (41, 37, 32, 29 and 26) are recorded in the problem, visit and monitoring text instead. The PHQ-9 is coded.
  8. Relatives and others. His wife, children and the doctors of his time appear only in documented history claims. In the modern episode he lives with an unnamed family member, a secretary and household staff; no relative is named or given a role, and nothing is attributed to any relative. His friends, therapist, social worker and the doctor who treated the August flare are synthetic and unnamed. Other public figures named in the sources are not named in the record.
  9. Synthetic clinical picture. Everything in the modern episode is synthetic: the grief symptoms and their course, every score (PHQ-9, PHQ-2, AUDIT-C, Mini-Cog; the PG-13-R in narrative), all laboratory values, the ECG and the CT coronary angiogram, the coronary disease, the stool test, ultrasound and hepatitis C results (narrative), the medicines, the August flare treatment, the lapse and the outcomes. Height (173 cm) and weights are synthetic.
  10. Presenting problems. Persistent yearning, poor sleep, low mood and withdrawal (Z63.4) are the presenting problem, resolved at the consultation into prolonged grief disorder (F43.81). Exertional chest tightness (R07.89) resolves at five weeks into nonobstructive coronary artery disease (I25.10) after CT coronary angiography. This modern heart disease is not a claim about his arteries in 1905; his documented chest pain began four years later.
  11. Clinical judgment calls. (1) Major depression is judged absent despite a PHQ-9 of 11, because the symptoms are grief-focused and fewer than five, so no antidepressant is started, following the 2016 trial in which an antidepressant added little to grief therapy except for depressive symptoms. (2) Passive thoughts of death without intent or plan are recorded as current ideation present, managed as an outpatient with weekly contact, means safety (no firearms; alcohol limited) and the 988 Lifeline, given the high suicide risk of older bereaved men. (3) Prolonged grief disorder therapy for sixteen sessions, two by telephone while he traveled. (4) No sleeping pill, by the Beers criteria. (5) CT coronary angiography rather than stress testing for stable exertional tightness at 69; nonobstructive disease treated with atorvastatin 40 mg and aspirin 81 mg without functional testing. (6) Varenicline for a cigar smoker with coronary disease, with an extra twelve weeks after he stopped. (7) Allopurinol started at the five-week visit, not at intake, because he wanted one change at a time; started at 100 mg with colchicine prophylaxis and continued through the August flare; HLA-B*58:01 testing not indicated. (8) Lung cancer screening recorded as not indicated, because the USPSTF criteria count cigarette pack-years; abdominal aortic aneurysm screening offered because he has smoked.
  12. Annual wellness visit. A subsequent annual wellness visit at 69 (HCPCS G0439 with CPT 99497 in plain text): Mini-Cog 5, PHQ-2 1, Timed Up and Go 10 seconds, whispered-voice test passed; high-dose influenza and PCV20 vaccines, with RSV and the first zoster dose at the eighteen-week visit at his request and Tdap and COVID-19 vaccines still due; a stool immunochemical test, hepatitis C antibody and abdominal aortic ultrasound (all reported normal at eighteen weeks in narrative); prostate screening declined after shared decision-making; advance care planning with an unnamed agent.
  13. Sick visit. Acute viral bronchitis on the day before his quit date, two weeks into varenicline. It was chosen because it is common, insinuates no undocumented diagnosis (his documented bronchitis was in 1906 and later), and tests the plan: no antibiotic or chest X-ray by ACP and CDC criteria; no first-generation antihistamine (Beers) or decongestant (coronary disease); and keeping the quit date. It is not a historical claim.
  14. Focused visits. The four chronic-care follow-ups (one week with the consultation, five weeks, thirteen weeks and eighteen weeks) are documented as focused visits on the DSM-5-TR criteria with the PG-13-R and prolonged grief disorder therapy, the VA/DoD suicide-risk guideline with the Erlangsen study, the USPSTF tobacco recommendation, the 2021 chest pain and 2023 chronic coronary disease guidelines, ACR 2020, and the CMID ischemic heart disease, hyperlipidemia and depression monographs. One deviation is recorded instead of an invented result: the urate check due three to four weeks after allopurinol started was missed while he was away, delaying the first dose increase by about four weeks; a flare occurred in that time but caused no lasting harm, and the corrective action was a urate test four to five weeks after each change at a laboratory near wherever he is staying. One grief therapy session was missed while he was away and two were held by telephone. Synthetic findings introduced: the CT coronary angiogram, the August flare treated with prednisone, a two-cigar lapse, and triglycerides slightly higher after the prednisone course.
  15. Consultation. The geriatric psychiatry referral and the consultation by Desmond Ifeanyi-Strand, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the one-week visit, are S. The consultant answers the six referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: prolonged grief disorder without major depression; no antidepressant; sixteen weekly sessions of prolonged grief disorder therapy; outpatient safety planning with means safety; a sleep plan without sedative-hypnotics; and varenicline as acceptable with mood checks, which he started at five weeks.
