History of Present Illness

Mr. Clemens, a 69-year-old writer and lecturer, is seen at the request of Dr. Achterberg-Lowe one week after intake. His wife died a year and two weeks ago after a long illness. He has lost a son and his eldest daughter in earlier years and says each loss "took a piece" but that this one took the rest. He thinks of her many times every day, feels that part of himself died with her, cannot believe at times that she is gone, avoids her photographs and the places they shared, feels intensely lonely, and finds his work meaningless. He sleeps four to five hours. He has passing wishes to join her but no intent or plan and has never tried to harm himself. Away from reminders his humor and curiosity return, and he does not regard himself as worthless. He has no history of mania or of psychiatric treatment. He drinks a glass of whisky most evenings and smokes about ten cigars a day. [S]

Assessment

  • Prolonged grief disorder (F43.81). More than twelve months after the death of someone close, he has daily yearning for and preoccupation with his wife, and at least three of the accessory symptoms (a sense that part of himself has died, disbelief, avoidance of reminders, intense emotional pain, difficulty taking up his life and work, meaninglessness and loneliness), lasting most days for at least the past month, beyond the norms of his community and culture, and impairing his work and friendships. This meets the DSM-5-TR criteria for prolonged grief disorder. The PG-13-R score of 41 agrees. [S]
  • Major depressive disorder. Not present. The PHQ-9 of 11 reflects symptoms shared with grief; on interview his low mood is focused on the loss, his self-worth is intact, interest and humor return away from reminders, and he has fewer than five depressive symptoms. No antidepressant is advised now: in trials, prolonged grief disorder therapy works and an antidepressant added little to it except for depressive symptoms. Reconsider if major depression develops or the PHQ-9 rises. [S]
  • Treatment. Prolonged grief disorder therapy (Shear), sixteen weekly sessions with a trained therapist: understanding grief, telling the story of the death, imaginal conversations with the deceased, memories, gradually revisiting avoided reminders, and rebuilding personal goals and relationships. His writing can be part of the goals work. [S]
  • Suicide risk. Older widowed men carry a high risk of suicide after a loss, and he has passive thoughts of death, insomnia, nightly alcohol and social withdrawal. Protective factors are strong: no intent, plan or past attempt, no firearms, his writing, his humor and the people around him. Outpatient care is appropriate with the safety plan, means safety, a telephone check and review at every visit. [S]
  • Sleep. Sleep should improve with grief treatment. Avoid benzodiazepines, zolpidem and sedating antihistamines, which the Beers criteria advise against at his age; use a fixed rising time, daylight and walking, and no cigar or whisky late in the evening. [S]
  • Varenicline. Varenicline is the most effective single medicine for stopping smoking, and large trials found no rise in serious neuropsychiatric events, including in people with psychiatric illness. It is reasonable for him; mood, sleep and any thoughts of death should be checked at each visit. [S]
