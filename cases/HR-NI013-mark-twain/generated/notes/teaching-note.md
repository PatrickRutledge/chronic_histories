# Teaching Note — Mark Twain pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Samuel Langhorne Clemens was born on November 30, 1835, in Florida, Missouri, the sixth of seven children; at four he moved with his family to Hannibal, Missouri, on the Mississippi River. Writing for a Virginia City, Nevada, newspaper, he first used the pen name Mark Twain; he became a celebrated writer, humorist and lecturer, author of The Adventures of Tom Sawyer and Adventures of Huckleberry Finn. He married Olivia (Livy) Langdon in 1870; they had a son and three daughters. Their first child, Langdon, died of diphtheria in 1872, at about two years old. Bad investments brought him to bankruptcy: his publishing company failed in 1894, and from July 1895 to July 1896 he made a worldwide lecture tour to earn money; he repaid his creditors. His eldest daughter, Susy, died of meningitis in August 1896, at 24, while staying at the family's former home in Hartford; the family never returned to live in Hartford. His wife, Olivia, died of heart failure at the Villa di Quarto near Florence, Italy, on the evening of June 5, 1904, after 22 months of illness; in his notebook that night he wrote that "she that was the life of my life" had died. He smoked heavily from boyhood: in 1882 he wrote that he had begun to smoke immoderately at eight, starting with one hundred cigars a month and rising to three hundred a month by his thirties, and that cigar smoking did no harm to his health. He had his first attack of gout in Paris in about 1894 and was laid up with gout again in Vienna in October 1897. On May 18, 1905, he arrived for the summer at a rented house at Dublin, New Hampshire, in the hills below Mount Monadnock. In August 1905 he was confined to bed with a severe attack of gout while visiting Norfolk, Connecticut. He was confined to his New York home with bronchitis from late April to early May 1906. In June 1909, in a Baltimore hotel, he had his first attack of chest pain; in July 1909 he wrote that it had been "decided that I have what is technically termed a 'tobacco' heart," that he must smoke four times a day instead of forty, and that the pains came about twice a day. His youngest daughter, Jean, who had epilepsy, drowned in her bath at Stormfield on December 24, 1909, during an apparent seizure. He died at Stormfield, his house in Redding, Connecticut, on April 21, 1910, at 74, of angina pectoris; his doctors had cut his twenty cigars a day to four, and his physician said he could not show that the angina followed from tobacco.
- **R:** His obituary in The New York Times said that the loss of his son, two daughters and his wife had preyed upon his mind; whether he had a depressive illness at any point cannot be determined from the sources read, and the roster's suggestion of bipolar disorder is not supported by them.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
