# Teaching Note — Mahatma Gandhi pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Mahatma Gandhi was born on October 2, 1869. According to his health file for 1924 to 1947, kept at the National Gandhi Museum, his blood pressure readings were as high as 194/130 on October 26, 1937, and 220/110 on February 19, 1940, and other readings in between reached about 170/110 and 180/105. In March 1927 his blood pressure was high throughout the day, and physicians' statements published in the Bombay Chronicle of March 29, 1927, warned of a danger of apoplexy from high blood pressure due to continuous overwork and advised absolute rest in a cool climate; on May 2, 1927, his blood pressure was found to be normal. To lower his blood pressure he placed a mud poultice on his abdomen, applied a mud bandage to his head in summer, and took sarpagandha, an Ayurvedic preparation of Rauwolfia serpentina; a physician's account says he took garlic daily after it was suggested as a remedy. His electrocardiogram and oscillograph reports of October 27 and 28, 1937, found diminished arterial elasticity, a fairly good myocardium, and slight enlargement of the heart; the report of February 19, 1940, found slight myocarditis, normal for his age, with cardiovascular degeneration arrested. A basal metabolic test on April 30, 1939, recorded his height as 5 feet 5 inches and his weight as 46.7 kg. During a fast at Rajkot in March 1939, health bulletins reported his blood pressure as 170/110 on March 4 with weakness of the heart and swelling of the feet, and 180/110 on March 5, with a loss of two pounds; a urine test on March 5 showed acetone in large amounts; he broke the fast on the afternoon of March 7 with fruit juices, honey, and jaggery. In a letter of October 28, 1941, to one of his physicians, he wrote that his blood pressure continued to remain high, that he had taken three drops of sarpagandha morning and evening, and that his pressure was 166/98 that morning, 196/112 after talking while walking, and 150/90 in the afternoon, and that his food intake was good. He kept a vegetarian diet; he took a vow not to drink cow's or buffalo milk and later, when ill, accepted goat's milk on medical advice; in Key to Health he wrote that experience had taught him that a vegetarian diet must include milk and milk products such as curd, butter, and ghee. Earlier illnesses in the cited summary include pleurisy in London in 1914, acute dysentery in 1918 and 1929, an operation for piles in 1919, an operation for acute appendicitis on January 12, 1924, and malaria in 1925 and 1936. He walked about 18 km a day for nearly 40 years, about 79,000 km during his campaigns from 1913 to 1948; his daily routine at Sevagram included a morning walk of about 5 km, kitchen and cleaning work, and spinning. Blood sugar measurements of 41 in January 1936, 71.4 in December 1937, and 115 in April 1938 are recorded in his health file; his cholesterol readings were within the normal range and his hemoglobin was 14.96 g/dL. He preached against tea and coffee, along with drugs, tobacco, and alcohol. He began his last fast on January 13, 1948, at 78, his eighteenth fast; on its third day he was carried in an armchair, a bulletin on January 16 said his kidneys were not functioning properly, and he broke the fast on the sixth day. He was assassinated on January 30, 1948.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
