Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Legend and sources. Much of what is written about her is legend, and her 1896 pamphlet is unreliable (CLM-0008). The case relies on a Montana Historical Society journal article that draws on James McLaird's biography, McLaird's own 1994 South Dakota History article and encyclopedia entry, the City of Deadwood's historic preservation brochure, a local history article quoting 1901 newspapers, and, for 1901 only, a HistoryNet article. McLaird's 2005 book was not read and is cited only as these sources report it. No National Park Service page about her was found beyond a photograph record, so none is cited. Popular sites found in searches were not used.
  2. Birth date. The 1860 census lists her as born in 1856, and her principal biographer and the Deadwood brochure give 1856; the roster and many popular accounts give May 1, 1852, and her gravestone implied 1850. The preferred date is about 1856 (precision approximate). The Deadwood brochure's May 1, 1856 is kept as an unresolved alternative, since her biographer writes only “perhaps May 1.” The synthetic date of birth, May 1, 1981, uses the traditional month and day; the shift is 125 years.
  3. Index age and dates. The index visit on March 2, 2026 maps to March 2, 1901, when she was about 44: two days after a Butte newspaper reported her in Livingston and a few weeks after her stay at the Gallatin County poor farm. The visits on March 9, March 23, March 30, April 27, May 11 and June 22, 2026 map to the same days of 1901, when newspapers placed her in southern and central Montana (Bozeman, Livingston, and reports in Lewistown, Billings and Butte papers), before she left for Buffalo in July. The facility's town is not specified. The suggested touring years (1893 to 1896) were not used because her whereabouts then do not allow months of visits in one region: she toured the eastern states in the first months of 1896. Past medical history includes only events up to February 1901.
  4. Name. She is known as Calamity Jane, which has no family name in the usual sense; her name at birth was Martha Canary. The record uses identity.name_parts (given Martha, family Canary) so that the C-CDA and FHIR patient names carry her legal name, while the display name and the FHIR name text are Calamity Jane. The notes call her Ms. Canary after noting that she goes by Calamity Jane. Aliases are Martha Canary and Martha Jane Canary; no other record contains either. “Jane” as a middle name is common in popular accounts but is not documented in the sources read. “Jane” alone is not an alias.
  5. Clothing and work. She often wore men's clothing and did work then done mostly by men (CLM-0003). This is recorded neutrally as documented social history, not as a medical matter. No gender identity is assigned: she never stated one in any source read. The roster's “gender nonconformity stress” lead is not used.
  6. Alcohol. Her heavy drinking is documented (CLM-0009, D, sensitive); the label alcoholic is a later judgment (CLM-0010, R). The modern alcohol use disorder is synthetic. Her death is recorded as inflammation of the bowels, with pneumonia also reported (CLM-0018); the role of alcohol is a biographers' judgment (CLM-0019, R).
  7. Hickok. James Butler (Wild Bill) Hickok appears only in documented history (CLM-0005, D): the 1876 trip and his murder. The romance is legend (CLM-0006, R, disputed). Nothing is attributed to him, and he does not appear in the modern episode.
  8. Smallpox nursing. Graded D with certainty probable (CLM-0007): the Deadwood brochure and McLaird date it to 1878, and Etulain cites several memoirists, but the accounts are recollections.
  9. Tuberculosis. The roster lists TB exposure. No source read documents tuberculosis; one documents a stay in a pest house for people with communicable diseases (CLM-0013). The modern episode screens for latent tuberculosis because of shelter stays and records a negative result; no infection is claimed.
  10. Relatives and companions. Her parents, siblings, children and partners are not named in the modern episode and are given no decisions, statements or health facts. The parents' deaths, the son's death and the daughter's birth appear only as dated historical facts, and the reported violence by a partner is recorded once without detail. The modern social history says only that she has a grown daughter she has not seen for some time. No family history is recorded. The friend, the peer recovery specialist, the community health worker and the man who offered pills are synthetic and unnamed.
  11. Synthetic clinical picture. Everything in the modern episode is synthetic: the drinking history, the DSM-5-TR count, every score (AUDIT, PHQ-9, GAD-7, PC-PTSD-5, and the PAWSS and CIWA-Ar, which have no LOINC total code here and are recorded as narrative), all laboratory values, PEth, the elastography readings, the FIB-4 values (calculated from the synthetic results and recorded as narrative), the urine drug, pregnancy, HIV, hepatitis, HPV and interferon-gamma release assay results (narrative), the medicines, the lapse and the outcomes. Height (165 cm) and weights are synthetic.
  12. Clinical judgment calls. (1) Outpatient withdrawal management at a PAWSS of 3 with a support person and daily nurse checks, although her housing is unstable; hospital criteria were written down. (2) Gabapentin rather than a benzodiazepine, since she is likely to be alone at times and could return to drinking during withdrawal, and her liver tests are raised. (3) Three days of intramuscular thiamine, then oral. (4) Naltrexone with an AST under three times the upper limit, changed to the monthly extended-release injection after missed doses. (5) No antidepressant: the PHQ-9 fell from 14 to 5 after ten weeks without alcohol, and the depressive symptoms were judged alcohol-induced. (6) The first elastography reading (9.6 kPa) was treated as indeterminate because of recent inflammation and repeated at sixteen weeks (6.8 kPa). (7) The two-day return to drinking is recorded honestly, corroborated by PEth, and met without blame. (8) Naloxone was not prescribed: she uses no opioids, and the urine test showed none.
  13. Sex-specific risks. The NIAAA Core Resource is the basis for counseling that women reach higher blood alcohol levels than men from the same amount, develop liver damage sooner, and have a higher breast cancer risk with drinking; the plan includes a pregnancy test, breast and cervical screening, and contraception if needed.
  14. Social needs. Montana Medicaid, SNAP, a food bank, the Human Resource Development Councils (Montana's community action agencies, which run housing programs), Montana 211 and the federal Lifeline phone program are real public programs. No phone number other than 988 is given.
  15. Preventive visit. A periodic adult preventive visit at 44 (CPT 99396 with 99497 in plain text). Judgment calls: HIV, hepatitis C and hepatitis B screening done at intake; self-collected HPV testing offered as a trauma-informed choice (negative); mammography at a mobile unit with transport (normal); an interferon-gamma release assay for latent tuberculosis because of shelter stays (negative); a lipid panel with low risk; Tdap, the first hepatitis A and B doses and PCV20; the HPV vaccine declined; advance care planning names an unnamed friend as agent.
  16. Sick visit. A dental abscess two weeks into oral naltrexone, chosen because it fits years without dental care and tests the plan: opioids are avoided because naltrexone blocks them and attempts to overcome the block can be fatal, ibuprofen with acetaminophen (no more than 2 g a day) treats the pain, amoxicillin is given because of fever and facial swelling, and definitive dental treatment is arranged the next day. It is not a historical claim.
  17. Focused visits. The four chronic-care follow-ups (one week with the consultation, four weeks, ten weeks and sixteen weeks) are documented as focused visits on the APA 2018 guideline, the ASAM 2020 withdrawal guideline, the NIAAA Core Resource, the AASLD 2019 guidance, the CMID depression monograph and the VA/DoD suicide-risk guideline. Two deviations are recorded instead of invented results: (1) weekly peer recovery contact lapsed for about two weeks in early April when she had no phone; no harm resulted (no drinking; she kept going to her meeting), and the corrective action was a Lifeline phone and text confirmation of each call; (2) the third extended-release naltrexone injection was given four days late while she was away for work, and her two days of drinking came just before it; she stopped within two days with no withdrawal and her liver results improved, and the corrective action was scheduling injections on days she chooses, with a reminder call and a ride. Synthetic findings introduced: a CIWA-Ar of 10 on the second day of withdrawal, the missed oral doses, the indeterminate first elastography, and the two-day lapse.
  18. Consultation. The addiction medicine referral and the consultation by Dolores Two Bulls-Harmon, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the one-week visit, are S. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: severe alcohol use disorder with withdrawal safely managed as an outpatient; naltrexone, changed to the monthly injection when tablets were missed; liver enzymes rechecked at four weeks; elastography at four weeks and again after months; the PHQ-9 at four weeks before any antidepressant; PEth at four and sixteen weeks; peer support, mutual-help choices and a plan for a return to drinking; and help with benefits and housing.
