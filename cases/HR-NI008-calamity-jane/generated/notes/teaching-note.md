# Teaching Note — Calamity Jane pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Calamity Jane was born Martha Canary about 1856, perhaps on May 1, near Princeton in Mercer County, Missouri, the eldest of six children of Robert and Charlotte Canary; the 1860 census lists her as born in 1856. Many popular accounts give 1852. Her family moved to the Montana gold camps about 1864; in December 1864 a Virginia City newspaper reported the Canary girls destitute. Her mother died at Blackfoot City, Montana Territory, in 1866 and her father in Utah in 1867, leaving her an orphan of about eleven with younger brothers and sisters. After her parents' deaths she made her way alone through the railroad construction camps and military posts of Wyoming, working as a dance-hall girl, waitress and laundress, and later at work usually done by men, such as driving ox teams as a bullwhacker and hauling wood. She often wore men's clothing, including buckskins. In 1875 she joined the Newton-Jenney expedition to the Black Hills without authorization, and by 1876 she was known as Calamity Jane; how she came by the name is uncertain, and the story that an army captain named her after she saved his life is unconfirmed. In June 1876 she traveled to Deadwood in a wagon train that included James Butler (Wild Bill) Hickok, who was murdered in Deadwood on August 2, 1876. During a smallpox epidemic in the Black Hills in 1878 she nursed sick people, and several contemporaries and memoirists remembered her as a nurse to the sick and a helper of people in need. Her 1896 autobiographical pamphlet, Life and Adventures of Calamity Jane, By Herself, written with a ghostwriter, describes exploits as an army scout and rider that historians regard as largely imaginary. Newspapers and contemporaries repeatedly reported her heavy drinking from the 1870s on; she was arrested for intoxication and disorderly conduct, and her principal biographer writes that every effort to end her drinking failed. Her drinking, wandering and lack of money left her poor even in settled periods. She gave birth to a son in 1882 who died soon after birth, and to a daughter in 1887. In the mid-1880s, newspapers in Rawlins, Wyoming, reported that a man she lived with, and later married, beat her and stole from her. In the 1880s and 1890s she moved from town to town in Montana, Wyoming and the Dakotas, working in hotels and saloons and hauling wood, selling copies of her pamphlet and photographs, living at times on the help of acquaintances, and for a time staying in a pest house set aside for people with communicable diseases. In 1896 she toured the northern United States with the Kohl and Middleton dime museum show. In February 1901 she fell ill on a train journey from Livingston, Montana, and, having no money, was taken to the Gallatin County poor farm at Bozeman; she was discharged within days, and afterwards she angrily denied newspaper reports that she had been a county charge. In July 1901 a New York writer brought her to the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo, where binge drinking got her into trouble, and she returned west late that year. She died at the Calloway Hotel in Terry, South Dakota, on August 1, 1903, of what was recorded as inflammation of the bowels, with pneumonia also reported, and was buried at Mount Moriah Cemetery in Deadwood near Hickok's grave, reportedly at her wish.
- **R:** Stories of a romance or marriage between her and Hickok are legend; her biographers find that they were acquaintances for a few weeks and that no evidence supports a love affair. Historians describe her as an alcoholic; a diagnosis in present-day terms cannot be made from the historical record. By 1901 a writer who met her in Livingston thought she had aged far beyond her years from heavy drinking and poor nutrition. Her biographers judge that years of heavy drinking contributed to her death.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
