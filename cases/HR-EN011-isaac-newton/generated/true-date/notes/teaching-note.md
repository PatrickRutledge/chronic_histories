# Teaching Note — Isaac Newton pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Isaac Newton was born at Woolsthorpe, in the parish of Colsterworth, Lincolnshire, on Christmas Day 1642 (25 December 1642 in the Julian calendar then used in England; 4 January 1643 in the Gregorian calendar), the only son of Isaac, a farmer, and his wife Hannah Ayscough. His father died before he was born, and his mother later remarried and had three more children. He was educated at Grantham and at Trinity College, Cambridge, became a Fellow of Trinity in 1667, held the Lucasian Professorship of Mathematics from 1669 to 1702, and was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1672. His biographer David Brewster, working from Newton's manuscripts, wrote that he was deeply engaged in chemical experiments in December 1692 and January 1693. Among his papers is a one-page manuscript in his hand titled 'Notes on mercury and its purification'. Brewster wrote that from the autumn of 1692 Newton complained of loss of appetite and want of sleep, which continued for nearly a twelvemonth. On 13 September 1693 (Julian; 23 September, Gregorian) Newton wrote to Samuel Pepys that he was 'extremely troubled at the embroilment I am in, and have neither ate nor slept well this twelvemonth, nor have my former consistency of mind', that he had never designed to get anything by Pepys's interest or the King's favour, and that he must withdraw from Pepys's acquaintance and see neither him nor the rest of his friends any more. On 16 September 1693 (Julian; 26 September, Gregorian), writing from the Bull in Shoreditch, London, he told John Locke that, being of opinion that Locke had endeavoured to embroil him with women and by other means, he had answered, when told Locke was sickly and would not live, that 'twere better if you were dead'. He begged Locke's pardon for this, for representing that Locke's book struck at the root of morality and taking him for a Hobbist, and for saying or thinking that there was a design to sell him an office or to embroil him; he signed himself Locke's 'most humble and unfortunate servant'. John Millington of Magdalene College wrote to Pepys on 30 September 1693 (Julian) that he had met Newton at Huntingdon on the 28th, when Newton, of his own accord, said he had written Pepys 'a very odd letter', in 'a distemper that much seized his head, and that kept him awake for above five nights together', and asked Millington to beg Pepys's pardon. Millington judged him now very well, though 'under some small degree of melancholy', with no reason to suspect that it had touched his understanding. Locke replied from Oates on 5 October 1693 (Julian) that he forgave Newton freely and fully. Newton answered from Cambridge on 15 October 1693 (Julian; 25 October, Gregorian) that the previous winter, by sleeping too often by his fire, he had got an ill habit of sleeping, that a distemper which that summer had been epidemical had put him further out of order, and that when he wrote he had not slept an hour a night for a fortnight together, and for five days together not a wink; he did not remember what he had said of Locke's book. The scientist Christiaan Huygens noted on 29 May 1694 that a Scotsman had told him that some eighteen months earlier Newton had become insane, from too intense application to his studies or from grief at losing his laboratory and manuscripts to fire, and that his friends had cared for him at home until he recovered. Brewster rejected the story, pointing to the letters and scholarly work Newton produced during that time. Locke remarked in 1703 that he had several reasons to think Newton truly his friend, but that he was 'a nice man to deal with, and a little too apt to raise in himself suspicions where there is no ground'. Newton became Master of the Mint in 1699 and was President of the Royal Society from 1705 to 1727. In 1722, in his eightieth year, he was seized with incontinence of urine, first ascribed to the stone and thought incurable, then attributed to a weakness of the bladder sphincter; his physician advised him to give up his carriage and dining out and to eat simply. In August 1724 he passed, without pain, a stone about the size of a pea, in two pieces some days apart; John Conduitt noted that he had voided two stones as big as a pea. He fell ill on 3 March 1727 (Julian) after a visit to London. When John Conduitt learned of it on 11 March he sent for Dr Richard Mead and the surgeon William Cheselden, who pronounced the disease to be stone in the bladder and held out no hope of recovery. He had severe fits of pain, seemed easier on 15 March, became insensible on the evening of the 18th, and died at about one in the morning of Monday 20 March 1726/7 (Julian; 31 March 1727, Gregorian) at Kensington, aged 84, unmarried. He was buried in Westminster Abbey on 28 March (Julian).
- **R:** Brewster, in his 1855 biography, argued that Newton's mind was never in a state of derangement during 1692 and 1693; a reviewer of the following year judged that his own letters placed beyond doubt an illness that lamentably shook his nerves and, for a time, interfered with the coherent exercise of his faculties. In 1979 two papers in Notes and Records of the Royal Society proposed that mercury poisoning from his chemical and alchemical experiments explained his illness of 1692 and 1693: Johnson and Wolbarsht called it a probable cause of his physical and mental ills, and Spargo and Pounds reported that hairs attributed to him contained several heavy metals, including mercury, at concentrations much higher than in modern hair, which they offered as circumstantial evidence for the hypothesis. Other writers proposed manic-depressive illness rather than mercury poisoning (Lieb and Hershman, 1983). In 2008 Milo Keynes argued that the madness of 1692 and 1693 was due to mercury poisoning and not to clinical depression, and that Newton's lifelong distrust of others followed from his separation from his mother in childhood and not, as had been claimed, from Asperger's syndrome.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
