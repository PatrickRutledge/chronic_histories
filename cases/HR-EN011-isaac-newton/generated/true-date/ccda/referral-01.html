History of Present Illness

Mr. Newton is a 50-year-old professor of mathematics who for many years has run a private chemistry laboratory beside his college rooms. He heats elemental mercury in open crucibles, makes amalgams, roasts antimony ore, and works with lead and occasionally arsenic sulfide, for six to ten hours several nights a week, with no fume hood, local exhaust, or respirator; spills are swept up with a brush and he eats at the bench. His last session was three days before today's visit. For a year he has slept and eaten poorly and lost 4 kg (now 64.0 kg, height 170 cm); for two weeks he has barely slept. He has had suspicious thoughts that friends were plotting against him, has written accusing letters, and now partly doubts these ideas. PHQ-9 20 (item 9 scored 0), GAD-7 11, MoCA 27. No suicidal thoughts; no thoughts or intent of harming others; a safety plan with the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline was made today. On examination he has no tremor, gingivitis, stomatitis, or neuropathy. He has stopped all laboratory work today, the laboratory is to be closed, and the university safety office has been asked to measure mercury vapor. Blood count, metabolic panel, TSH, vitamin B12, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, blood mercury, blood lead, and a 24-hour urine mercury with creatinine have been ordered [S]

Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Isaac Newton was born at Woolsthorpe, in the parish of Colsterworth, Lincolnshire, on Christmas Day 1642 (25 December 1642 in the Julian calendar then used in England; 4 January 1643 in the Gregorian calendar), the only son of Isaac, a farmer, and his wife Hannah Ayscough. His father died before he was born, and his mother later remarried and had three more children. [D]
  • He was educated at Grantham and at Trinity College, Cambridge, became a Fellow of Trinity in 1667, held the Lucasian Professorship of Mathematics from 1669 to 1702, and was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1672. [D]
  • His biographer David Brewster, working from Newton's manuscripts, wrote that he was deeply engaged in chemical experiments in December 1692 and January 1693. Among his papers is a one-page manuscript in his hand titled 'Notes on mercury and its purification'. [D]
  • Brewster wrote that from the autumn of 1692 Newton complained of loss of appetite and want of sleep, which continued for nearly a twelvemonth. [D]
  • On 13 September 1693 (Julian; 23 September, Gregorian) Newton wrote to Samuel Pepys that he was 'extremely troubled at the embroilment I am in, and have neither ate nor slept well this twelvemonth, nor have my former consistency of mind', that he had never designed to get anything by Pepys's interest or the King's favour, and that he must withdraw from Pepys's acquaintance and see neither him nor the rest of his friends any more. [D]
  • On 16 September 1693 (Julian; 26 September, Gregorian), writing from the Bull in Shoreditch, London, he told John Locke that, being of opinion that Locke had endeavoured to embroil him with women and by other means, he had answered, when told Locke was sickly and would not live, that 'twere better if you were dead'. He begged Locke's pardon for this, for representing that Locke's book struck at the root of morality and taking him for a Hobbist, and for saying or thinking that there was a design to sell him an office or to embroil him; he signed himself Locke's 'most humble and unfortunate servant'. [D]
