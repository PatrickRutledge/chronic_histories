Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Calendar and dates. England used the Julian (Old Style) calendar throughout his life, with the legal year beginning on 25 March. He was born on 25 December 1642 (Julian), which is 4 January 1643 in the Gregorian calendar, and died on 20 March 1726/7 (Julian), written 1727 by the Abbey and Brewster, which is 31 March 1727 (Gregorian). The difference was ten days until February 1700 and eleven days after. The record converts every documented date to the Gregorian calendar for the true frame, as other records with Old Style dates do; the conversions were checked by computing Julian day numbers in the build script. The preferred birth and death dates are Gregorian, with the Julian dates recorded as superseded alternatives, and timeline displays give both. Huygens wrote in the Netherlands, and the source does not state which calendar he used; his date of 29 May 1694 is recorded as Brewster gives it.
  2. Index age and dates. The episode presents him at 50, at Cambridge, in the autumn of 1693, just after the documented letters of September 1693 to Pepys and Locke. The synthetic date of birth is January 4, 1976, so the whole-year shift is 333 years and 2026 maps to 1693. The index visit is September 28, 2026 (true frame 28 September 1693, Gregorian, which is 18 September, Julian, two days after the letter to Locke); the follow-ups are October 8 (consultation; true frame 28 September, Julian, the day he met Millington at Huntingdon) and October 29 (true frame 19 October, Julian, four days after his letter to Locke about his sleeplessness). The sick visit is October 15 and the preventive visit October 22, 2026. He is 50 throughout. Only three chronic-care visits fit before the November 1, 2026 cutoff, which falls in early November 1693 in the true frame.
  3. Name. “Isaac Newton” splits simply into given and family names, so `identity.name_parts` is not used. “Sir Isaac Newton” is the alias; it appears in no other record. The bare word “Newton” is not used as an alias because another record mentions an unrelated expedition of that name.
  4. What was verified and what was not. His sleeplessness, poor appetite, withdrawal from friends, suspicions of Locke and their retraction, Millington's report of a distemper that kept him awake and of “some small degree of melancholy”, and his own account of near-total sleeplessness are D, from his letters as printed by Brewster and Baden Powell on the Newton Project. The roster's “18-month psychotic episode” rests on a single second-hand note by Huygens, recorded as D that Huygens wrote it, with Brewster's rebuttal; the record does not describe the episode as psychosis. His chemical experiments of December 1692 and January 1693 and a manuscript on the purification of mercury are D; how much mercury he breathed is unknown. Westfall's biography and the Oxford DNB could not be read and are not cited. The full texts of the two 1979 Notes and Records papers could not be read (the Royal Society site returned HTTP 403); their claims are recorded from the Trinity College archive record, the PubMed record, and the University of Cape Town obituary, and no hair concentration is quoted. The Royal Museums Greenwich blog on Pepys and Newton redirected to an index page and is not cited.
  5. Mercury, depression, and other explanations. Mercury poisoning as the cause of 1692 and 1693 is R, disputed (Johnson and Wolbarsht; Spargo and Pounds; Keynes, 2008). Manic-depressive illness (Lieb and Hershman, 1983) and clinical depression are competing readings, also R, disputed, and Brewster's denial of any derangement is R. The record diagnoses none of them historically.
  6. Autism and other roster leads. The roster's “probable autism spectrum” is a retrospective speculation. The only reputable source found that mentions it is Keynes (2008), who reports that Asperger's syndrome “has been claimed” and argues against it; it is recorded in CLM-0012 as R, disputed, and is not a problem or diagnosis. The roster's “OCD features” and “extreme social isolation” were not found in any source read and are left out. “Never married” is supported by the Abbey's “died unmarried” (CLM-0016).
  7. Presenting problem and diagnosis. The intake problem (R45.89) is a year of poor sleep and appetite with two weeks of near-sleeplessness and suspicious thoughts, resolved on October 8, 2026, when major depressive disorder, comorbid insomnia, and raised urine mercury are recorded. The DSM-5-TR differential is decided as major depressive disorder, single episode, severe, without psychotic features (F32.2): the suspicious ideas were never fixed and were retracted, with no hallucinations, so neither psychotic features nor delusional disorder is diagnosed; a mercury-induced depressive disorder is considered and not made, because the urine level (46 ug/L) is below the ATSDR band for depression and irritability, there are no signs of mercurialism, and the illness has followed the year-long course of a depressive episode. The record states that mercury may have contributed and that the diagnosis would be reconsidered if mood lifted quickly with removal alone. F32.2 is placed both_with_link to the documented letters.
  8. Mercury results. The synthetic 24-hour urine mercury of 46 ug/L (41 ug/g creatinine) with blood mercury 9.4 ug/L was chosen as a moderate, defensible elevation: real occupational absorption about twice the occupational biological exposure index, in ATSDR's 20 to 100 ug/L band, without signs of toxicity. It falls to 31 ug/L a month after removal, consistent with a urinary half-life of one to two months, and blood mercury normalizes. Blood lead is normal. Arsenic and antimony are recorded in the exposure history but not tested, because the exposure was small and intermittent and ACMT warns against broad metal screens without a specific exposure and signs. The workplace vapor measurement (0.08 mg/m3 at the furnace) is synthetic and is compared with the NIOSH REL (0.05 mg/m3 TWA) and the OSHA PEL (0.1 mg/m3) as the NIOSH Pocket Guide gives them.
  9. Chelation. Chelation is not given and no provoked urine test is done, following ACMT and the ACMT symposium papers (removal from exposure first; provoked testing not valid) and the ATSDR fact sheet (chelation for severe symptoms and highly elevated levels).
  10. Safety assessment. The intake records no suicidal thoughts (PHQ-9 item 9 scored 0; C-SSRS screen negative), but a severe depressive episode with severe insomnia, so a safety plan, the 988 Lifeline, and review at every visit are recorded. Because his documented letter records that he had said a friend would be better dead, the synthetic mental-status examination includes a regretted angry remark and a violence screen with no thoughts or intent of harm; the record states this echoes the letter without asserting anything about his real state of mind. Means safety includes the removal of mercury and arsenic compounds from the laboratory. Claims about his sleep, mood, and suspicions are marked sensitive.
  11. Real people. Pepys, Locke, Millington, Huygens, Conduitt, Mead, and Cheselden appear only in documented history claims. The modern episode names none of them and attributes no decision, statement, or health fact to them; the friends he apologizes to are unnamed and synthetic.
  12. Family and past history. Only his father's death before his birth is recorded as family history (cause not recorded in the cited source); his mother's later life is not in the sources read and is left out. Past medical history holds only the documented loss of sleep and appetite from the autumn of 1692, before the index date. His later incontinence, bladder stones, and death from a stone in the bladder (1722 to 1727) are D and appear only on the timeline, after the index date.
  13. Drug and treatment choices. Sertraline 50 mg with weekly CBT and collaborative-care review by the clinic psychiatrist, following the APA 2019 and VA/DoD 2022 guidelines and the CMID monograph; no antipsychotic, because there are no psychotic features; CBT for insomnia without hypnotics, following the ACP guideline. At the sick visit, combination cold medicines are avoided because dextromethorphan with sertraline risks serotonin toxicity and sedating antihistamines undo the sleep plan.
  14. Instruments. PHQ-9 (44261-6), GAD-7 (70274-6), and MoCA (72172-0) are coded surveys. The C-SSRS screen and the Mood Disorder Questionnaire have no score recorded; the bipolar screen is by interview, which the monitoring table states.
  15. Sick and preventive visits. The sick visit is a common cold a week after starting sertraline; it echoes, without asserting, his mention of a distemper that was epidemical that summer, and teaches no antibiotics or testing, acetaminophen, and avoiding dextromethorphan and sedating antihistamines on an SSRI. The preventive visit is `preventive_adult` because he is 50; it gives influenza vaccine, PCV20 (ACIP: all adults aged 50 and over), and the first recombinant zoster dose, orders a fecal immunochemical test, screens lipids (normal), and records advance care planning that names no real person.
  16. Focused visits. The two chronic-care follow-ups (ten days and a month after intake) are documented as focused visits on the CMID depression monograph with the APA 2019 and VA/DoD 2022 guidelines, the VA/DoD suicide-risk guideline, DSM-5-TR, the ACP insomnia guideline, and ATSDR, NIOSH, and ACMT guidance: PHQ-9 at every visit, GAD-7, suicide and violence risk at every visit, the workup and bipolar interview at the diagnosis visit, SSRI tolerability at two weeks (done at the preventive visit), urine and blood mercury before and a month after removal, kidney tests due at three months, examination for tremor, gingivitis, and neuropathy, the hazard assessment, and chelation recorded as not indicated. One deviation is recorded as `deferred`, with its outcome and corrective action, and no result was invented: the safety office's re-measurement of mercury vapor after decontamination was planned within two weeks and had not been done by the last visit; there was no harm because the laboratory stayed closed, and the clinic wrote to ask for it, with his return to laboratory work on hold. The consultant finds occupational mercury absorption without clinical mercury poisoning, advises against chelation and provoked testing, sets the hazard assessment and the return-to-work conditions, and agrees that the depression needs treatment in its own right; the record then shows the laboratory staying closed, urine mercury falling, and no chelation.

CLM-0007DJohn Millington of Magdalene College wrote to Pepys on 30 September 1693 (Julian) that he had met Newton at Huntingdon on the 28th, when Newton, of his own accord, said he had written Pepys 'a very odd letter', in 'a distemper that much seized his head, and that kept him awake for above five nights together', and asked Millington to beg Pepys's pardon. Millington judged him now very well, though 'under some small degree of melancholy', with no reason to suspect that it had touched his understanding.
