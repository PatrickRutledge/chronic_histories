History of Present Illness

Mr. Newton is seen at the request of Dr. Okonkwo-Fairbanks, ten days after his first visit. For many years he has heated mercury and other metals in a small brick outbuilding with a furnace and no ventilation beyond a door and one window, mostly at night, in sessions of six to ten hours several times a week over the past year. He makes amalgams with gold and other metals and distils mercury; he roasts antimony ore and works with lead; arsenic sulfide is used rarely and in closed vessels. He has swept up spilled mercury with a brush and has eaten and drunk at the bench. He has not worked in the laboratory since his first visit. He has had no metallic taste, excess salivation, bleeding gums, tremor, change in handwriting, numbness, rash, or change in urine. He describes a year of poor sleep and appetite and weeks of exhaustion, low mood, and suspicious ideas about friends that he now attributes to exhaustion. He has no thoughts of harming himself or others. The university safety office measured mercury vapor of 0.08 mg/m3 at the furnace and visible droplets in the floor joints, sealed the laboratory, and removed the mercury and arsenic compounds. [S]

  • Isaac Newton was born at Woolsthorpe, in the parish of Colsterworth, Lincolnshire, on Christmas Day 1642 (25 December 1642 in the Julian calendar then used in England; 4 January 1643 in the Gregorian calendar), the only son of Isaac, a farmer, and his wife Hannah Ayscough. His father died before he was born, and his mother later remarried and had three more children. [D]

  • His biographer David Brewster, working from Newton's manuscripts, wrote that he was deeply engaged in chemical experiments in December 1692 and January 1693. Among his papers is a one-page manuscript in his hand titled 'Notes on mercury and its purification'. [D]
  • Brewster wrote that from the autumn of 1692 Newton complained of loss of appetite and want of sleep, which continued for nearly a twelvemonth. [D]
  • On 13 September 1693 (Julian; 23 September, Gregorian) Newton wrote to Samuel Pepys that he was 'extremely troubled at the embroilment I am in, and have neither ate nor slept well this twelvemonth, nor have my former consistency of mind', that he had never designed to get anything by Pepys's interest or the King's favour, and that he must withdraw from Pepys's acquaintance and see neither him nor the rest of his friends any more. [D]

He begged Locke's pardon for this, for representing that Locke's book struck at the root of morality and taking him for a Hobbist, and for saying or thinking that there was a design to sell him an office or to embroil him; he signed himself Locke's 'most humble and unfortunate servant'. Assessment

  • Raised urine mercury from occupational exposure (R82.6). Real occupational absorption of elemental mercury vapor, confirmed by the vapor measurement and a urine level about twice the occupational biological exposure index. At 46 ug/L the urine level lies in the band that ATSDR associates with subtle changes on nerve-conduction and psychomotor testing, not with the irritability, depression, tremor, and kidney effects seen above 100 ug/L. There is no tremor, gingivitis, erethism beyond his mood disorder, neuropathy, or kidney injury, so this is exposure without clinical mercury poisoning. Mercury may have added to his insomnia and irritability but does not explain a year of depressive illness. Lead absorption is negligible. Arsenic and antimony exposure was small and intermittent, and a broad metal screen is not useful without a specific exposure and signs. [S]
  • Major depressive disorder, single episode, severe, without psychotic features (F32.2). I agree that his depressive and sleep symptoms need treatment now, in their own right, alongside removal from exposure. [S]

Data reviewed: Blood mercury 9.4 ug/L (95th percentile of US adults below 5 ug/L); 24-hour urine mercury 46 ug/L and 41 ug/g creatinine; blood lead 2.4 ug/dL; creatinine 0.92 mg/dL, eGFR 101; urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio 9 mg/g with negative dipstick protein; blood count, sodium, potassium, glucose, calcium, albumin, ALT, TSH, and vitamin B12 normal; PHQ-9 20 at intake and 19 today; MoCA 27; the safety office's vapor measurement; today's vital signs (weight 63.8 kg). Plan of Treatment

  • No chelation: it is not indicated for urine mercury below 100 ug/L without signs of toxicity, and removal from exposure is the treatment. No provoked (chelator-challenge) urine testing, which is not valid. [S]
  • Keep him out of the laboratory and its adjoining rooms until it is professionally decontaminated, mercury vapor is re-measured below the NIOSH limit of 0.05 mg/m3 averaged over a work shift (OSHA 0.1 mg/m3), and a ducted fume hood is fitted. [S]
  • The hazard assessment should cover vapor at the furnace and bench, the floor and drains, his clothing and his rooms, storage of chemicals, a mercury spill kit, and no food or drink in the laboratory. [S]
  • Repeat 24-hour urine mercury with creatinine and blood mercury four to six weeks after removal, and again at three months with creatinine and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio; nerve conduction studies only if numbness or weakness appear. [S]
  • Return to laboratory work when the conditions above are met and the urine mercury is below 20 ug/g creatinine, with urine mercury checked every six months while he works with mercury. [S]
  • Treat the depression and insomnia now as planned, with sertraline and cognitive behavioral therapy; please ask me to see him again if tremor, gum disease, numbness, or proteinuria appear, or if the urine mercury fails to fall. [S]
  • Discussed with Dr. Okonkwo-Fairbanks the same day; Mr. Newton agrees with the plan and was given a letter for the university safety office. [S]
  • Follow-up: Primary care continues with Dr. Okonkwo-Fairbanks. I will review the three-month urine mercury and the safety office's re-measurement before he returns to laboratory work. [S]
