# Teaching Note — Benny Leonard pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Benjamin Leiner was born on April 7, 1896, in New York City, to Gershon Leiner, a tailor, and Minnie Leiner, Orthodox Jewish immigrants from Russia, and grew up on the Lower East Side of Manhattan. He began fighting professionally at about fifteen and took the name Benny Leonard to keep his boxing secret from his parents, because boxing was not thought a respectable living in their community. He won the world lightweight championship from Freddie Welsh on May 28, 1917, and held it until he retired as undefeated champion in January 1925, at his mother's wish. The son of Orthodox Jews, he never fought on a Jewish holiday and became a symbol of the acceptance of Jews in American life at a time of open antisemitism; the newspaper editor Arthur Brisbane wrote that he had done more to conquer anti-Semitism than a thousand textbooks. He later supported the Maccabiah Games. Retired, he had become wealthy; in his own words he was practically a millionaire, without a scratch. He lost most of his savings in the 1929 stock market crash and in October 1931, at 35, came back as a welterweight; he won about nineteen fights in a row before Jimmy McLarnin knocked him out in the sixth round in October 1932, and he retired for good. During the Second World War he served in the U.S. Maritime Service of the Merchant Marine, rising to lieutenant commander. He returned to boxing as a referee of professional bouts in New York and also taught boxing at City College of New York. On April 18, 1947, at 51, after refereeing the whole card at St. Nicholas Arena in New York on a hot night in a smoke-filled hall, he slumped over the lower rope early in the first round of the last bout and sank to the canvas. The commission physician tried to revive him, and he was carried to the dressing room, where he was found to have died.
- **R:** His sudden death is described in most sources as a heart attack; one reference work gives a cerebral hemorrhage. No autopsy finding was found in the sources read, and no earlier heart disease is documented.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
