# Teaching Note — Swami Vivekananda pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** He was born Narendranath (Narendra Nath) Datta in an affluent family in Calcutta (Kolkata) on 12 January 1863. His father, Vishwanath Datta, was a successful attorney; his mother was Bhuvaneshwari Devi. He met Sri Ramakrishna at Dakshineswar in November 1881 and became his disciple; in 1887 he and his brother disciples took the formal vows of sannyasa and new monastic names. He left Bombay for America on 31 May 1893 and spoke at the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago in September 1893, which made him famous; he then spent nearly three and a half years teaching Vedanta, mostly in the eastern United States and London, and returned to India in January 1897. On 1 May 1897, at a meeting of the monastic and lay devotees of Sri Ramakrishna in Calcutta, he founded the Ramakrishna Mission Association; in February 1898 the monastery moved to a garden house at Belur on the west bank of the Ganga, and land for the Belur Math was bought in early 1898. In March and April 1897, soon after his return to India, he wrote that he had diabetes. On 16 March he wrote: "The work has broken me down completely, and I have got 'diabetes', an incurable disease"; on 26 March: "The result of this steady work in the West and the tremendous work of a month in India upon the Bengalee constitution is 'diabetes'. It is a hereditary foe," adding that "Eating only meat and drinking no water seems to be the only way to prolong life"; and on 7 April that he had been "attacked by 'Diabetes', a fell disease" and had gone to Darjeeling, cool and "very good for the disease," where the doctors did not want him to move about. A biography says he went to Darjeeling on the advice of physicians. On 29 May 1897, from Almora, he wrote to a physician disciple that he was taking the medicines sent to him, riding morning and evening and feeling very strong, but that "I never in my life could sleep as soon as I got into bed. I must toss for at least two hours," that he felt hot after the evening meal, that "There has not been any thirst even though the days are fearfully hot," and that he was afraid of "getting fat on a too much milk diet." In 1898 he went to Darjeeling on 30 March for the cool air, came down to Calcutta to organize relief during an outbreak of plague, and left on 11 May for Nainital, Almora and Kashmir. He returned to Belur on 18 October 1898. His brother disciples saw that he was "very pallid and ill. He suffered from suffocating attacks of asthma; when he emerged from its painful fits, his face looked blue, like that of a drowning man." On 26 January 1899 he wrote from the Belur Math: "The old diabetes has now disappeared. In its place has come what some doctors call asthma, others dyspepsia, owing to nervous prostration. However, it is a most worrying disease, giving one the sensation of suffocation - sometimes for days." He made a second visit to the West from June 1899 and returned to the Belur Math in December 1900. In 1901 he wrote that "Bengal brings the asthma now and then" and that "the terrible things - Bright's disease and diabetes - have disappeared altogether" (2 September), and later (12 December) that "the last three years I have been getting albuminuria now and then," that the kidneys were "structurally all right," that a cold always increased it, and that "a small blood vessel in the right eye" had burst; his doctors put him to bed and forbade meat. According to a biography by a monk of his order, in March 1901 he was "suffering from both asthma and diabetes"; in the last year and a half of his life "Diabetes took the form of dropsy. His feet swelled ... He could hardly close his eyes in sleep," and on a physician's strict regimen he avoided water and salt, for twenty-one days taking no water at all. He died at the Belur Math on the night of 4 July 1902, at 39, after an hour of meditation; a brother monk recalled "a little blood in his nostrils, about his mouth, and in his eyes." The physicians gave the cause as "apoplexy or sudden failure of the heart"; the monks held that he had left his body in samadhi.
- **R:** Retrospective interpretation: diabetes beginning at about 34 in a man of South Asian ancestry who called it hereditary, who lived five more years without insulin, and whose thirst and sugar came and went with diet and rest, is most consistent with type 2 diabetes; slowly progressive autoimmune diabetes cannot be excluded from the record. Retrospective interpretation: albuminuria from about 1898, a diagnosis of Bright's disease, a bleed in the eye and swelling of the feet late in his life are consistent with diabetic kidney disease, perhaps with high blood pressure, but no measurement survives and other kidney or heart disease cannot be excluded. Retrospective interpretation: his sudden death, which many accounts describe as the rupture of a blood vessel in the brain, could have been a brain hemorrhage or a heart event; the physicians' "apoplexy or sudden failure of the heart" does not decide between them, and his followers understand it as samadhi.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
