  • Irène Curie was born in Paris on 12 September 1897, the elder daughter of the physicist Pierre Curie and his wife, a physicist who also worked with radioactive materials. [D]

  • She became a Doctor of Science in 1925 with a thesis on the alpha rays of polonium, and in 1926 married Frédéric Joliot; the couple joined their surnames and worked together on natural and artificial radioactivity and nuclear physics. [D]
  • In 1935 she and Frédéric Joliot shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for their synthesis of new radioactive elements, after showing that bombarding aluminum foil with alpha particles produced artificial radioactivity. [D]
  • In 1936 she was appointed Undersecretary of State for Scientific Research; she became a professor in the Faculty of Science in Paris in 1937, and her research on the action of neutrons on heavy elements in 1938 was an important step toward the discovery of nuclear fission. [D]
  • She and Frédéric Joliot had a daughter and a son. [D]
  • She had always been of unstable health, and the couple took regular trips to the countryside to help her condition. [D]

  • Shortly after 1950 her health declined visibly. [D]
  • Her mother died on 4 July 1934; the director of the sanatorium where she died reported an aplastic pernicious anemia of rapid, feverish development, in which the bone marrow did not react, probably because it had been injured by a long accumulation of radiations. [D]

Assessment

  • Acute myeloid leukemia (C92.00). Confirmed: 34 percent marrow myeloblasts of myeloid phenotype. Acute promyelocytic leukemia is excluded by the phenotype, the normal coagulation tests and the negative rapid PML::RARA test, so there is no need for emergency all-trans retinoic acid. The multilineage dysplasia and the macrocytosis suggest a leukemia arising from a damaged marrow. Classification and risk await the cytogenetics and molecular panel. After long exposure to ionizing radiation, the most likely findings are a complex karyotype, loss of 5q or 7, and a TP53 mutation, which would mean adverse risk. The WHO and ICC labels for leukemia after cytotoxic therapy (therapy-related) are defined by earlier chemotherapy or radiotherapy given as treatment, so her leukemia should be classified by its genetics and her occupational exposure recorded in the history rather than as a therapy-related label.
