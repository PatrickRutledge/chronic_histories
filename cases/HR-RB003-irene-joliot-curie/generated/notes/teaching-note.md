# Teaching Note — Irène Joliot-Curie pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Irène Curie was born in Paris on 12 September 1897, the elder daughter of the physicist Pierre Curie and his wife, a physicist who also worked with radioactive materials. During the First World War she served as a nurse radiographer, operating X-ray machines in mobile field hospitals alongside her mother. She became a Doctor of Science in 1925 with a thesis on the alpha rays of polonium, and in 1926 married Frédéric Joliot; the couple joined their surnames and worked together on natural and artificial radioactivity and nuclear physics. In 1935 she and Frédéric Joliot shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for their synthesis of new radioactive elements, after showing that bombarding aluminum foil with alpha particles produced artificial radioactivity. In 1936 she was appointed Undersecretary of State for Scientific Research; she became a professor in the Faculty of Science in Paris in 1937, and her research on the action of neutrons on heavy elements in 1938 was an important step toward the discovery of nuclear fission. She and Frédéric Joliot had a daughter and a son. She had always been of unstable health, and the couple took regular trips to the countryside to help her condition. During the Second World War she spent time in Switzerland recovering from tuberculosis, and in 1944 brought her children with her to Switzerland. In 1946 she became director of the Radium Institute; as a commissioner for atomic energy for six years she took part in building the first French atomic pile in 1948, and she drew up the plans for the large nuclear physics center at Orsay. In 1946 a capsule of polonium exploded on her laboratory bench. Shortly after 1950 her health declined visibly. A stay in the mountains in 1956 brought no improvement, and she died of leukemia shortly after. She died of leukemia in Paris on 17 March 1956, at the age of 58. Her mother died on 4 July 1934; the director of the sanatorium where she died reported an aplastic pernicious anemia of rapid, feverish development, in which the bone marrow did not react, probably because it had been injured by a long accumulation of radiations.
- **R:** Smithsonian magazine reported that the 1946 polonium accident is thought to have possibly been responsible for her death from leukemia ten years later, and that she may have been the first person to die of polonium poisoning. Her leukemia is usually attributed to over-exposure to X-rays during the First World War and to radioactive materials during her life of research.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
