# Teaching Note — Elisabeth of Austria pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Elisabeth Amalie Eugenie, Duchess in Bavaria, called Sisi, was born on December 24, 1837, in Munich, the daughter of Duke Max in Bavaria and Duchess Ludovika. She married her cousin, Emperor Franz Joseph I, on April 24, 1854, in the Augustinerkirche in Vienna, and became Empress of Austria and, later, Queen of Hungary. She had four children. From about 1859 she was reported to suffer from a mysterious illness, with cough, poor nutrition, anemia and exhaustion; she spent the winter of 1860 to 1861 on Madeira and, after a relapse, four months on Corfu, and recovered quickly whenever she travelled. At 1.72 m she was unusually tall for a woman of her time, with an extremely slender figure. Through her life she followed many diets; for periods each meal was a single food, such as egg, a milk product, orange or broth, and her 'magic weight' was fifty kilos. In her last years she weighed herself up to three times a day, and in 1894 she weighed as little as 43.5 kg, a body mass index of about 14.7 at her height. Her waist measured only about 50 cm at its smallest, in tightly laced corsets and dresses sewn around it. She hiked for hours every day and had exercise rooms installed in her apartments; in the Vienna Hofburg the wall bars, high bar and rings are preserved, and she had a large gymnasium with heavy apparatus. She was one of the best horsewomen in Europe, but gave up riding at the beginning of the 1880s because of health problems. A psychiatrist's biographical essay writes that in her last years she was increasingly troubled by her undernutrition, with fluid accumulation in the body and severe bodily pains, and that she was diagnosed with chlorosis. A Swiss newspaper reported in 2024 that letters kept by her spa physician at Bad Kissingen, found in the city archive, describe complaints including rheumatic attacks, sciatica, joint pain and hunger edema at the ankles. On January 30, 1889, her son, Crown Prince Rudolf, born in 1858, was found dead with a young woman at the Mayerling hunting lodge; historians regard as certain only that he killed her and then himself. It is said that her son's death prompted her to wear only mourning from then on. She suffered from ever-deepening depressions, which in her last years are thought to have developed into a kind of death-wish. After her son's death she sought to master her inner unrest with extended travels and a retreat to Corfu, where she had the Achilleion, a palatial villa, built; she lost interest in it once it was finished and left the island six years after its completion. She often travelled incognito. On September 10, 1898, in Geneva, she was stabbed by an Italian anarchist and died; she was buried in the Imperial Crypt (Kapuzinergruft) in Vienna on September 17, 1898.
- **R:** Eating-disorder researchers write that she sometimes suffered from edema as a consequence of severe starvation. Psychiatrists and eating-disorder researchers have argued that she met the criteria for anorexia nervosa: one essay states that 'without doubt' she qualified, a German study describes fasting rituals, gymnastics, hours of riding, forced marches and a considerable fear of weight gain, and Hungarian researchers describe many features of anorexia nervosa and suggest it was closely related to depression.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
