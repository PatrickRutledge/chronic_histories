## Historical evidence

1. Baly ME, Matthew HCG. “Nightingale, Florence (1820–1910), reformer of Army Medical Services and of nursing organization.” *Oxford Dictionary of National Biography*, 2004; text reproduced by the MacTutor History of Mathematics archive, University of St Andrews. Supports her birth on May 12, 1820, in Florence; the party of thirty-eight nurses for Scutari; the Crimean fever of May 1855, “which she referred to as ‘typhus’ but which was probably” a Brucella infection (citing the 1995 BMJ article); her return in July 1856 and the royal commission of 1857; the 830-page report of 1858 and her election to the Statistical Society; the collapse with cardiac symptoms while working on the report, followed by nausea, insomnia, and palpitations; her taking to her bed or couch on doctors' orders while she “continued to work feverishly,” the beginning of twenty years of invalidism; the 1861 episode when she “was unable to walk and suffered from alarming spondylitis”; her final fifteen years in her South Street room, failing eyesight, and difficulty writing from 1902; the Order of Merit in 1907; the psychosomatic theory among diagnoses proposed for her invalidism; and her death in her sleep at 10 South Street, Park Lane, on August 13, 1910. https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/DNB/Nightingale.html
2. Cook ET. *The Life of Florence Nightingale*, Volume 1. London: Macmillan; 1913. Project Gutenberg eBook 40057. Authorized biography. Supports the onset of the Crimean fever a few days after she reached Balaclava, her removal by stretcher to the Castle Hospital, her later account that she was “very near to death,” the May 24 report that she was out of danger, and her overwork in 1857, with rest at Malvern in August, September, and December. https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40057
3. National Army Museum. “Florence Nightingale: The Lady with the Lamp.” Supports her departure from London on October 21, 1854, and arrival at Constantinople on November 3; her illness with “Crimean fever” within days of reaching Balaklava in May 1855; her return in August 1856; her election in 1859 as the first female member of the Royal Statistical Society; the statement that she “later suffered from what is now known as Chronic Fatigue Syndrome” and was bedridden for many years while still campaigning; the Order of Merit; and her death on August 13, 1910, aged 90. https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/florence-nightingale-lady-lamp
4. Science Museum Group Collection. “Nightingale, Florence 1820 - 1910.” Supports her birth in Florence, the belief that her illness was brucellosis, her being “often … confined to her sick bed,” some 13,000 letters and more than 200 books, reports, and pamphlets, the Order of Merit, and her death in her room at 10 South Street. https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/people/ap24367

## Editorial resolutions

- **Index age.** The modern episode presents her at 37, after the documented Crimean fever (at 35) and the 1857 decline (at 37), at the start of the decades she spent working from her bed or couch. The index visit is March 2, 2026 (true frame March 2, 1858); the synthetic date of birth is May 12, 1988, a shift of 168 years. The last chronic-care visit is September 14, 2026 (true frame September 14, 1858). The 1861 episode with “spondylitis,” her later years, and her death fall after the index date and remain only in the historical timeline.
- **Modern diagnosis.** The Crimean fever and her long invalidism are documented (**D**). Brucellosis and ME/CFS are retrospective, disputed interpretations (**R**), recorded as claims CLM-0004 and CLM-0010 and, for brucellosis, as an R past-medical-history entry. Neither is a historical diagnosis. The synthetic patient has post-infectious ME/CFS diagnosed by NICE and IOM criteria; the problem entry says the historical reading is disputed and links both D and R claims.
- **Brucellosis serology as history.** The synthetic history says a Brucella titer was positive abroad and treated with six weeks of doxycycline and rifampin. This is justified because NICE NG206 calls for serological tests after a history of infection, chronic brucellosis is the leading retrospective reading of her illness, and excluding relapse is the real reason for the infectious diseases consultation. Brucella serology is not repeated in the modern episode: agglutinating antibodies can persist after cure, so a repeat titer would not show relapse.
- **Other readings left out.** Retrospective psychiatric readings of her illness (for example bipolar disorder or post-traumatic stress) in the medical historian's essay are not used, and no psychiatric diagnosis is given. The ODNB's view that the 1857 collapse was “probably sequelae of the Crimean fever” is noted as interpretation in CLM-0006, not as fact.
- **Conflicting dates.** Her return from the war is July 1856 in the ODNB and August 1856 at the National Army Museum; her election to the Statistical Society is 1858 in the ODNB and 1859 at the museum. The claims give the year or both values. The exact date of the 1857 collapse is not given in the fetched sources; the timeline uses August 1857, when Cook places her at Malvern.
- **Family history.** Her parents and sister were alive at the true-frame index date, and no family illness before it was found in a fetched source, so family history is empty. Letters by relatives quoted in Cook's biography are not used.
- **Not verified.** The full text of the 1995 BMJ article could not be retrieved, so its argument is cited only as reported in the ODNB. No death certificate was reviewed; the cause of death given by the medical historian is recorded as his account.
- **Working life.** Her documented work from her bed (the 830-page report, some 13,000 letters, more than 200 publications) is part of the teaching point. The synthetic patient is a nurse administrator and hospital statistician whose goals are to keep working within her energy limits, and the plan includes workplace adjustments, not a push to “recover” activity.
- **Sick and preventive visits.** An unscheduled sick visit on July 20, 2026 (true frame July 20, 1858) for a common cold with a short ME/CFS flare, and an adult preventive visit on October 12, 2026 (true frame October 12, 1858; she is 38, so the preventive_adult type applies). The cold does not echo a documented illness. It was chosen because infection is a common trigger of ME/CFS flares and because it lets the record show the consultant's warning signs for brucellosis relapse in use. The preventive visit adds a synthetic copper intrauterine device, a synthetic normal cervical screening history, and names no real person as her health care agent.
- **Focused visits.** The three chronic-care follow-ups (two weeks, eleven weeks, and six months) are documented as focused visits on NICE NG206, since the CMID does not cover ME/CFS. Each lists the protocol items with interval and status. Two deviations are recorded as `deferred`, not filled: no ME/CFS specialist team was available (NICE 1.4.3), so primary care and the clinic occupational therapist wrote the care plan; and the 25-hydroxyvitamin D recheck was left off the three-month order and drawn at the six-month review (34 ng/mL, no harm). Synthetic findings introduced: the intake urine dipstick (negative) and examination findings (no spinal or sacroiliac tenderness, no organ enlargement).
- **Consultation.** The infectious diseases consultation on March 16, 2026 is synthetic. It answers the four referral questions; its recommendations (no antibiotics, no repeat serology, blood cultures only for warning signs, one-time HIV and hepatitis screening with preventive care) match what the later visits show: the sick visit applies the warning signs, and the preventive visit orders the screening tests, whose results are not yet in the record.
- Nightingale died in 1910, so the recent-death review rule does not apply. Ordinary clinical, provenance, and safe-messaging reviews remain required.
