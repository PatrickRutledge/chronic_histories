# Teaching Note — Florence Nightingale pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Florence Nightingale was born on May 12, 1820, in Florence, Italy, where her parents were on a European tour; her elder sister, Frances Parthenope, was born in 1819. In October 1854 she led a party of thirty-eight nurses from London to the British military hospitals at Scutari, arriving in November 1854. In May 1855, a few days after crossing to Balaclava to inspect the hospitals in the Crimea, she fell ill with what the army doctors called Crimean fever. She was carried to the Castle Hospital, was dangerously ill and, by her own later account, very near to death; she was reported out of danger on May 24 and made a slow and painful recovery before returning to work at Scutari. She returned to England in the summer of 1856, emaciated and tense, and pressed for a royal commission on the health of the army, which was established in 1857. In 1857, while working on her report for the commission, she was overworking and in failing health; she sought rest at Malvern in August and September and again in December, but would not stop working. She collapsed with cardiac symptoms so severe that it was thought she would die, and afterward suffered from nausea, insomnia, and palpitations. On her doctors' orders she took to her bed or couch but continued to work, the beginning of about twenty years of invalidism. Her own 830-page report, Notes on Matters Affecting the Health, Efficiency, and Hospital Administration of the British Army (1858), used statistical evidence to show how much army mortality was due to conditions in the hospitals. She was elected to the Statistical Society, the first woman member. Although often confined to her bed or her room for decades, she directed reform largely by correspondence, writing some 13,000 letters and publishing more than 200 books, reports, and pamphlets on hospital planning and organization. In 1861 she had a further severe episode in which she was unable to walk and suffered what her biographers describe as alarming spondylitis. She spent almost the whole of her last fifteen years in her room in South Street, London; her eyesight was failing by the late 1880s, and from 1902 she could write only with great difficulty. In 1907 she became the first woman appointed to the Order of Merit. She died in her sleep in her room at 10 South Street, Park Lane, London, on August 13, 1910, aged 90.
- **R:** Modern writers have interpreted her Crimean fever as brucellosis and her long later illness as chronic brucellosis. Her prolonged illness has also been read retrospectively as what is now called myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome, while other writers have proposed neurasthenia, a psychosomatic illness, or illness used to secure time for work.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
