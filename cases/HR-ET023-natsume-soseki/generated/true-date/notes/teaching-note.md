# Teaching Note — Natsume Sōseki pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Natsume Sōseki, whose name was Natsume Kinnosuke, was born on the 5th day of the 1st month of Keiō 3 in the Japanese calendar (9 February 1867) in Ushigome Babashita-Yokomachi in Edo, now Kikui-chō in Shinjuku, Tokyo, the son of a town headman. At the age of one he was adopted into the Shiobara family; his adoptive parents divorced when he was nine, and he was returned to his birth family. After graduating from the English department of the Imperial University in 1893 he taught at Matsuyama Middle School and at the Fifth Higher School in Kumamoto, and married Nakane Kyōko, eldest daughter of Nakane Shigeichi, chief secretary of the House of Peers, after taking up the Kumamoto post. In 1900, at 33, he was sent by the Ministry of Education to Britain to study English for two years, and he returned to Japan in 1903. During his stay in Britain he soon felt at odds with the study of English literature and fell into a state of severe nervous distress, which continued after his return. In 1907, at 40, he gave up all his teaching posts and joined the Asahi Shimbun as a staff writer, and about then moved to Waseda-Minami-chō; Sanshirō, Sorekara and Mon were serialized in the newspaper. He had made his name with I Am a Cat (1905 to 1906) and later wrote Kokoro (1914). From about May 1910, while writing Mon, he suffered from stomach illness, a gastric ulcer. On 18 June 1910 he entered the Nagayo Gastrointestinal Hospital in Tokyo, and he was discharged on 31 July. On 6 August 1910 he went to Shuzenji in Izu for a change of air; there he vomited a large amount of blood and fell into a critical condition but survived. His diary record marks 24 August 1910 as the day he was critically ill, the entry written for him by his wife. In his later years he suffered, besides the gastric ulcer, from hemorrhoids and nervous distress, and developed diabetes. In 1916, while he was writing Meian (Light and Dark), the gastric ulcer recurred with internal bleeding, and he died at his home in Waseda-Minami-chō on 9 December 1916, at 49, leaving the novel unfinished.
- **R:** The city of Shinjuku calls the London illness shinkei suijaku (nervous breakdown, neurasthenia), and the Sōseki museum calls it nervous depression; both are labels of the period or translations of it, not modern diagnoses.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
