# Teaching Note — Frederick Law Olmsted pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Frederick Law Olmsted was born in Hartford, Connecticut, on April 26, 1822. Olmsted died on August 28, 1903, in Belmont, Massachusetts, at 81. Olmsted ceased active practice in 1895, a victim of failing memory and vitality; later accounts say dementia, or senility, forced his retirement, and his sons took over the firm. His health continued to fail after he retired. A trip to England did not help, and he became depressed there; a summer house built on Deer Isle, Maine, in 1897 was laid out to stimulate his decreasing mental abilities, but after one summer there his health continued to deteriorate. Olmsted, who had chosen the Belmont site of McLean Hospital in 1872, was admitted there as a patient at the end of his life and spent his last five years there, until his death in 1903. His McLean Hospital medical record is sealed, and the cause of his decline is not known. In 1863, exhausted and ill after overwork as head of the U.S. Sanitary Commission, Olmsted resigned.
- **R:** Any specific type of dementia (for example Alzheimer's disease or vascular dementia) is a retrospective interpretation; the cited sources say only failing memory, senility, and dementia. A psychiatrist has argued retrospectively that losses in his childhood, beginning with his mother's death when he was a young child, left him with post-traumatic stress, and that his late illness made it worse.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
