# Teaching Note — Leonard Nimoy pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Leonard Nimoy was born in Boston on March 26, 1931. He played Mr. Spock in the Star Trek television series from 1966 to 1969, later acted in and directed Star Trek films, and directed other films, including the comedy Three Men and a Baby. In a Twitter post at the end of January 2014 he announced that he had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: "I quit smoking 30 yrs ago. Not soon enough. I have COPD. Grandpa says, quit now!! LLAP". In early February 2014 he posted "I'm doing OK. Just can't walk distances," and ABC News reported that he had been seen at an airport in a wheelchair. In February 2014 he urged smokers to quit: "Smokers, please understand. If you quit after you're diagnosed with lung damage it's too late." Later in February 2014 he wrote that he had tried to "smoke a little," had failed, and had learned he could not smoke at all, that quitting was not easy and he had gone through a program; in March 2014 he wrote that when he was addicted to cigarettes he ignored good medical advice. At his death UPI reported that he had had COPD for over a year, that the disease resulted from years of smoking, and that he had called himself an "Olympic championship smoker" in a television interview. He died at his home in Los Angeles on February 27, 2015, at 83; news reports citing his family gave the cause as end-stage chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
