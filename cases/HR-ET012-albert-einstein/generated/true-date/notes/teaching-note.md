# Teaching Note — Albert Einstein pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Albert Einstein was born on March 14, 1879, in Ulm, in the Kingdom of Wurttemberg, German Empire. Einstein died on April 18, 1955, at Princeton Hospital in Princeton, New Jersey, aged 76. He worked at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton from 1933 until his death and became a United States citizen in 1940. At about 69 he began to have intermittent episodes of severe abdominal pain, at times bad enough to make him vomit, which grew worse toward the end of 1948. On December 31, 1948, at the Jewish Hospital of Brooklyn, the surgeon Rudolph Nissen performed an exploratory laparotomy and found an abdominal aortic aneurysm described as the size of a grapefruit; because it could not be removed with the surgery of the time, he wrapped its front in cellophane to provoke scarring that might prevent growth and rupture. He recovered slowly and left the hospital about three weeks after the operation. In April 1955 the abdominal pain returned, more severe; the aneurysm had enlarged and ruptured. A surgeon advised an urgent operation, but Einstein, admitted to Princeton Hospital, declined it, saying in substance that he had lived long and did not wish to prolong his life artificially. An autopsy confirmed a ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm. Einstein was a long-time pipe smoker; a pipe was characteristic of his appearance, and an account of his aneurysm says he had smoked constantly since adolescence. In his later years he restricted his smoking on doctors' orders but kept holding a pipe; in 1953 he gave a pipe worn through at the bit to a friend. The picture of a ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm mimicking acute cholecystitis has been called the Einstein sign, after his final illness.
- **R:** Retrospective writers have suggested that he had features of the autism spectrum.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
