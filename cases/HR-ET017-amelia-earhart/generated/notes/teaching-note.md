# Teaching Note — Amelia Earhart pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Amelia Mary Earhart was born on July 24, 1897, in Atchison, Kansas, to Amy Otis and Edwin Stanton Earhart. After visiting her sister in Toronto at Christmas 1917, she moved there in February 1918 and worked in the Voluntary Aid Detachment at the Spadina Military Hospital, often on twelve-hour shifts, going wherever an extra pair of hands was needed. During the 1918 influenza epidemic she worked nights on the ward that held the influenza patients; within weeks she fell ill herself, with severe sinus pain and terrible headaches. Her case was serious enough that doctors operated, opening into her sinuses so that they could be drained regularly. It was a month before she was well enough to leave hospital, and she went to recover at her sister's home in Massachusetts. She suffered from chronic sinus problems for the rest of her life. She entered Columbia University as a pre-medical student in the fall of 1919 and left in 1920. She took her first airplane ride in December 1920, with the pilot Frank Hawks, and became a licensed pilot in the early 1920s: Purdue University dates her pilot's license to May 1923, and the Encyclopedia of the Great Plains to 1921. After undergoing a sinus operation to relieve her chronic sinus headaches, she returned to Columbia University for the winter of 1924 to 1925. She accompanied the pilot Wilmer Stultz and the mechanic Louis Gordon on their 1928 transatlantic flight in the Friendship, but did not fly the plane herself. In May 1932 she became the first woman, and the second person, to fly solo across the Atlantic. In February 1931 she married the publisher George Palmer Putnam in Noank, Connecticut. Radio contact with her aircraft was lost on July 2, 1937, during her attempt to fly around the world; she and her navigator, Fred Noonan, disappeared over the Pacific. She was declared legally dead in 1939 by the Superior Court in Los Angeles.
- **R:** In modern terms, sinus disease that began with a severe sinus infection during influenza, needed drainage operations, and caused lasting headaches and sinus trouble fits chronic rhinosinusitis. The fetched sources give no diagnosis in those terms, and nothing is known of her sinus anatomy or of nasal polyps.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
