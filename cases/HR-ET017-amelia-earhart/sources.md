## Clinical guidance

1. Rosenfeld RM, et al. Clinical practice guideline (update): adult sinusitis. *Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg* 2015;152(2 Suppl):S1-S39 (PMID 25832968). Confirm chronic rhinosinusitis with objective evidence (anterior rhinoscopy, nasal endoscopy, or CT); confirm the presence or absence of nasal polyps; assess for conditions that modify management (asthma, cystic fibrosis, immune compromise, ciliary dyskinesia); recommend saline irrigation, intranasal steroids, or both; allergy and immune testing as an option; no antifungals; amoxicillin with or without clavulanate as first-line antibiotic for acute bacterial sinusitis. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25832968/
2. Orlandi RR, et al. International consensus statement on allergy and rhinology: rhinosinusitis 2021. *Int Forum Allergy Rhinol* 2021;11(3):213-739 (PMID 33236525). Evidence-based recommendations for the medical and surgical management of rhinosinusitis, with a management algorithm. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33236525/
3. Rudmik L, et al. Defining appropriateness criteria for endoscopic sinus surgery during management of uncomplicated adult chronic rhinosinusitis: a RAND/UCLA appropriateness study. *Rhinology* 2016 (PMID 26934470). For chronic rhinosinusitis without polyps, surgery can be appropriately offered when the CT Lund-Mackay score is 1 or more after at least a topical intranasal steroid plus a short course of a broad-spectrum or culture-directed antibiotic (or a prolonged low-dose anti-inflammatory antibiotic), with a post-treatment SNOT-22 of 20 or more; the decision is preference-sensitive. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26934470/
4. Smith, Kim, Douglas, et al. Is there a role for antibiotics in the treatment of chronic rhinosinusitis? *J Allergy Clin Immunol* 2022 (PMID 35217148). Its summary table of guideline recommendations was used for ICAR 2021 on antibiotics (macrolides an option; non-macrolide courses under three weeks generally not for disease with polyps except in acute exacerbations) and for the AAO-HNS-era recommendation of antibiotics for acute exacerbations. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35217148/
5. Federal Aviation Administration. Guide for Aviation Medical Examiners: Over-the-Counter (OTC) Medications Reference Guide (updated August 28, 2024). 14 CFR 61.53 prohibits flight with a known medical deficiency; never fly after a new medication until at least 48 hours have passed without side effects; wait five dosage intervals after a no-go medicine; 60 hours after diphenhydramine; nasal steroids, saline, oxymetazoline, phenylephrine, pseudoephedrine and acetaminophen listed as generally safe; sedating antihistamines no-go. https://www.faa.gov/pilots/medical_certification/media/OTCMedicationsforPilots.pdf
6. Federal Aviation Administration. Guide for Aviation Medical Examiners: Allergy - Antihistamine and Immunotherapy Medication. Airmen with symptoms must not fly regardless of treatment; a mandatory 48-hour ground trial after a new medication; nasal corticosteroid sprays, pseudoephedrine and oxymetazoline listed as acceptable; the examiner asks about barotitis (ear block) and barosinusitis (sinus block). https://www.faa.gov/ame_guide/media/AllergyAntihistamineImmunotherapyMedication.pdf

## Editorial resolutions

- **Recent-death rule.** She disappeared in 1937, so the rule does not apply. Her disappearance and the 1939 legal declaration are recorded as history only (CLM-0010), with no speculation about her fate.
- **Death date.** The date of her death is unknown. The preferred date is July 2, 1937, the day radio contact was lost (Purdue; Encyclopedia of the Great Plains), with precision “approximate”; the 1939 legal declaration is recorded as an unresolved alternative because it is the date of a court decision, not of death; the roster year 1937 agrees.
- **Source quality.** Much of what is written online about her sinus illness comes from encyclopedia mirrors, blogs, a Wikipedia talk page, and pirated copies of biographies. Only Purdue University’s archive biography, a university encyclopedia, a government publication, Legion Magazine, and one Toronto historian’s article were used. Purdue’s finding aid and Canada’s History magazine (Bill Zuk, “Magnetic North”, 2021) could not be fetched; the published biographies (Butler, Rich, Backus’s *Letters from Amelia*) were not consulted.
- **Not verified, left out.** The following were found only in unreliable or secondhand sources and are not claimed: that the 1918 infection was pneumonia, pneumococcal, or of the frontal or maxillary sinus; the number of drainage operations and a drainage tube in her cheek; that the 1924 operation succeeded or failed and where it was done; and that sinus trouble affected specific flights (1932, 1935) or her flying in general. The roster’s “lifelong sinus trouble while flying” therefore rests only on the general statement that she had chronic sinus problems for the rest of her life (CLM-0004).
- **Grading.** Her birth, Toronto hospital work, studies, flying career, marriage and disappearance are **D**, confirmed. The 1918 illness and its drainage treatment (CLM-0003) and the lifelong sinus problems (CLM-0004) are **D** with certainty “probable”, because they rest on one fetched popular-history source; the 1924 sinus operation for chronic sinus headaches (CLM-0006) is **D** from Purdue. Reading her history as chronic rhinosinusitis (CLM-0007) is **R**, possible; the sources name no diagnosis and say nothing about nasal polyps, so the modern episode uses disease without polyps.
- **License date.** Purdue dates her pilot’s license to May 1923 and the Encyclopedia of the Great Plains to 1921; both are recorded in CLM-0005. The index date falls after both.
- **Index age and dates.** The index visit on June 8, 2026 maps to June 8, 1923, when she was 25 and, by both sources, a licensed pilot; the age was chosen in her mid-twenties, between the 1918 illness and the documented 1924 operation for chronic sinus headaches. The synthetic date of birth is July 24, 2000 (shift 103 years). The visits on June 22, August 17, and October 12, 2026, the preventive visit on July 6, 2026, the sick visit on July 20, 2026, and the surgery on September 3, 2026 map to the same days of 1923. The 1924 operation (CLM-0006) is after the index date and is not shown in the intake documents; the modern surgery is synthetic and is not a claim about it, or about where she was in 1923.
- **Real people.** Her parents, Frank Hawks, Wilmer Stultz, Louis Gordon, George Palmer Putnam and Fred Noonan appear only in the claims. No modern decision, statement, or health fact is attributed to any real person; the sister she visits in the sick visit and the health care agent are unnamed and have no role. No family history is recorded. The alias “Amelia Mary Earhart” appears in no other record.
- **Presenting problem.** Nasal blockage with suspected chronic rhinosinusitis (R09.81) is the presenting problem at intake, resolved at the consultation on June 22, 2026, when nasal endoscopy showed edema and pus in the left middle meatus without polyps and chronic rhinosinusitis without nasal polyps (J32.9) was recorded. The intake documents state only what was known then.
- **Modern management.** Saline irrigation and fluticasone were started at intake and the topical decongestant and diphenhydramine night tablet stopped. At the consultation, a culture-directed amoxicillin-clavulanate course was given for an acute exacerbation with pus; no oral steroid was given, because the evidence for one is in disease with polyps, and no antifungal. After ten weeks of appropriate medical therapy the SNOT-22 was 41 and the CT Lund-Mackay score 9, so she met the appropriateness criteria; after a shared decision she chose left endoscopic sinus surgery, followed by a budesonide rinse in place of the spray. SNOT-22 fell from 52 at intake to 12 five weeks after surgery. The SNOT-22, the CT findings and Lund-Mackay score, the culture and the pathology are recorded in the note text, because no LOINC code for the SNOT-22 total score or the Lund-Mackay score was found.
- **Aviation medicine.** The record follows the FAA Guide for Aviation Medical Examiners: no flying while symptomatic (14 CFR 61.53), a 48-hour ground trial of new medicines, nasal steroids and saline acceptable, sedating antihistamines no-go with a 60-hour wait after diphenhydramine, and return to flying after surgery only when the surgeon cleared her. She is advised to report the diagnosis and surgery at her next aviation medical examination; the record makes no claim about FAA special-issuance rules, which were not reviewed.
- **Sick visit.** A same-day visit for left middle ear barotrauma (T70.0XXA) after an airline flight taken during a cold, chosen because it is common, follows directly from the case (a blocked nose cannot equalize pressure on descent), teaches the no-fly rule, and insinuates no undocumented real diagnosis. Treated with acetaminophen and grounding; resolved by the ten-week visit.
- **Preventive visit.** A periodic adult preventive visit at age 25 (CPT 99395 and 99497 in plain text): PHQ-2 0, AUDIT-C 0, cervical cytology up to date, HIV and hepatitis C tests (negative), contraception and folic acid counseling, sun and noise protection for a pilot, immunizations, and advance care planning with an unnamed agent. The influenza vaccine was given at the last visit; she declined the COVID-19 vaccine for now.
- **Focused visits.** The three chronic-care follow-ups (two weeks with the consultation, ten weeks, and five weeks after surgery) are documented as focused visits on the AAO-HNS guideline, ICAR 2021, the RAND/UCLA appropriateness criteria, and the FAA guidance. One deviation is recorded instead of an invented result: the phone check planned for the end of the antibiotic course was not made, because it was not booked as a task; she was better at the preventive visit five days later, no harm resulted, and checks are now booked when the order is placed.
- **Consultation.** The otolaryngology referral and the consultation by Felix Lindgren, MD (a fictional consultant), with nasal endoscopy on the second visit, are **S**. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: chronic rhinosinusitis without polyps; the irrigation and steroid continued, a culture-directed antibiotic for the flare, and no oral steroid; a CT only after about eight more weeks, and surgery if the CT shows disease and the SNOT-22 stays at 20 or more (both happened, and she chose surgery); a dental check (normal); and the flying and medication rules (followed, apart from the airline flight with a cold that caused the barotrauma).
- **Synthetic content.** All visits, laboratory values, scores, endoscopy, CT and pathology findings, vital signs, medicines and doses, her height (173 cm), her modern work and medical certificate class, and the surgery are synthetic.
