# Teaching Note — Rachel Carson pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Rachel Louise Carson was born on May 27, 1907, on a family farm in Springdale, Pennsylvania. Carson was hired by the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries in 1936 as a junior aquatic biologist and rose to be editor-in-chief of the publications of its successor, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. She resigned in 1952 to write full time. Carson was a marine biologist and writer. Her books included Under the Sea Wind (1941) and the best-selling The Sea Around Us. Silent Spring, published on September 27, 1962, warned of the harm done by the widespread use of chemical pesticides. In 1950, while preparing her second book, Carson had her first breast operation, to remove a benign tumor from her left breast. While writing Silent Spring Carson worked through what she called "a catalogue of illnesses": flu, a sinus infection, heart trouble, arthritis, and an ulcer. She told her publisher that her disability was arthritis, and only a few friends knew the truth. In early 1960, while writing Silent Spring, Carson found two lumps in her left breast. In April 1960 Carson had a radical mastectomy for tumors of her left breast. Carson's surgeon did not tell her she had cancer, even after the radical mastectomy and when later swelling showed that the cancer had spread; when she asked direct questions he told her she had "a condition bordering on malignancy." A review of her standard biography calls this a lie on the part of the surgeon, never forgiven by the patient. Within months of the operation the cancer had spread to her ribs and she began radiation treatment. Her biographer writes that Carson had to fight the medical profession to tell her the truth about her own illness; she later wrote to George Crile Jr. of the Cleveland Clinic, her cancer surgeon and friend, asking for his honest view of her prognosis. Early in 1963 Carson wrote that a cardiologist had diagnosed angina, that hard, tender lymph nodes above her left collarbone had been found to be diseased and were being treated with radiation, and that an X-ray showed erosion of a bone of her left shoulder. Her cancer spread to lymph nodes, bones, and liver, with more radiation and hormone treatment. Carson kept her cancer hidden from all but a handful of friends, in part so that her illness could not be used against Silent Spring. On June 4, 1963, Carson testified before the Senate Government Operations Subcommittee on Reorganization and International Organizations on the control of pesticides and other chemical poisons, and she also testified before the Senate Commerce Committee. She was dying of breast cancer; her pelvis was so riddled with fractures that she could barely walk to her seat, and she wore a wig to hide hair loss. Carson died of cancer at her home in Silver Spring, Maryland, on April 14, 1964, at 56. In a 1961 survey of physicians, 90 percent preferred not to tell a cancer patient the diagnosis; when the same questionnaire was given in 1977, 97 percent preferred to tell.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
