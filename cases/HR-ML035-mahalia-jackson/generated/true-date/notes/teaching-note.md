# Teaching Note — Mahalia Jackson pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Mahalia Jackson was born in New Orleans on October 26, 1911, the third of six children of a man who was a longshoreman by day, a barber by night and a clergyman on Sunday. Her mother died when she was six, and she was raised by an aunt in a poor but respectable home; she left school in the eighth grade to work as a cook and washerwoman. In 1928 she moved to Chicago, worked as a hotel maid and laundress, and joined the Greater Salem Baptist Church, where she became a soloist. She never learned to read music. After her Carnegie Hall debut on October 4, 1950, she began touring abroad in 1952, and toured widely for the rest of her life. She was closely associated with the civil rights movement and sang at the March on Washington in 1963 before Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.'s speech. When she moved into an all-white neighborhood on the South Side of Chicago, a bullet was fired through a window of her home. Touring the segregated South for as many as 200 dates a year, she and her company had trouble finding safe places to sleep, eat and buy gas, sometimes slept in her car, lived on bags of fruit and drove half the night, and she arrived so exhausted she was almost dizzy when she sang; she insisted on being paid in cash because checks were often refused. Before and during her first European tour in 1952 she had difficulty breathing during and after performances and severe abdominal cramping, sang many shows in pain, lost a great deal of weight and finally cancelled the tour. On her return she had a hysterectomy, at which numerous granulomas were found in her abdomen, and she was diagnosed with sarcoidosis. From then on she had occasional bouts of fatigue and shortness of breath that became more frequent as her schedule filled. In 1964, weeks after her marriage, she could not stop coughing on the drive home from a concert in St. Louis and admitted herself to a Chicago hospital. The diagnosis given in public was heart strain and exhaustion; her doctors told her privately that she had had a heart attack and that sarcoidosis was now in her heart. Recovery took a year, during which she could not tour or record and lost about 50 pounds; afterwards she had near-constant fatigue, bouts of tachycardia and high blood pressure. From 1964 she was in and out of hospitals, and physicians warned her of exhaustion from her itineraries. She was hospitalized for heart trouble in the fall of 1967. By the late 1960s she had inflammation in her eyes and painful cramps in her legs and hands; she cut short a European tour in 1968 for health reasons. In 1971 she toured Japan, India and Europe; she became ill on tour in Germany, where she gave her last concert in Munich, and was flown home to Chicago and hospitalized in the fall. She died on January 27, 1972, at Little Company of Mary Hospital in Evergreen Park, Illinois, at 60, after several years of poor health.
- **R:** The cause of her death is recorded differently: a heart seizure (The New York Times), heart failure (PBS), a heart attack (Biography.com), heart complications at home (New Orleans Historical), and death in recovery after surgery for a bowel obstruction, which one account attributes to sarcoidosis (Wikipedia, TheWrap). That her heart disease, and the heart trouble that recurred until her death, was cardiac sarcoidosis is a retrospective interpretation. It rests on the private account of her doctors in 1964 reported by her biographer; the public diagnoses were heart strain, exhaustion and heart trouble.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
