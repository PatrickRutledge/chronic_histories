# Teaching Note — Charles Darwin pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Charles Darwin was born on February 12, 1809, in Shrewsbury, Shropshire, England. Darwin died on April 19, 1882, in Downe, Kent, aged 73. While waiting at Plymouth to sail on the Beagle in late 1831, Darwin was troubled with palpitations and pain about the heart, and consulted no doctor for fear of being judged unfit for the voyage. In September 1837 he had palpitations and, on his doctors' advice, went to the country to recover. Darwin first mentioned periodical vomiting in a letter to W. D. Fox in June 1840. In February 1849 he described incessant vomiting to Richard Owen, and in March 1849 he wrote that he had been bad all winter, with dreadful vomiting every week, that his hands trembled, and that his head was often swimming. In March 1849 Darwin took his family to Dr. James Manby Gully's water-cure establishment at Great Malvern for about three months; the treatment included heating by spirit lamp, cold towel rubs, cold foot-baths, a wet compress on the stomach, simple food, and short walks. Darwin kept a health diary from July 1, 1849, to January 16, 1855, which records near-daily flatulence and notes the acidity of his vomit. Over his adult life he had gastrointestinal distress, including vomiting, retching, and bowel irregularity, with headaches, fatigue, trembling, faintness, dizziness, and skin problems including eczema. In April 1861 he wrote that a short talk at the Linnean Society brought on 24 hours of vomiting. A British Medical Journal note on his medical history, published after his death, said that in his last years the heart and great vessels were degenerating, anginal attacks became more frequent, signs of heart failure appeared, and he died in one of these attacks.
- **R:** Cyclic vomiting syndrome, perhaps from a mitochondrial DNA disorder, has been proposed as the cause of his illness. Chagas disease, contracted during the Beagle voyage, has been proposed as the cause of his illness. Systemic lactose intolerance has been proposed as the cause of his illness. Panic disorder with agoraphobia has been proposed as the explanation of his illness and secluded life. Other proposed explanations include hyperventilation, neurasthenia, hypoglycemia, other allergies, Crohn's disease, Helicobacter pylori infection, systemic lupus, Meniere's disease, and psychological or psychosomatic causes.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
