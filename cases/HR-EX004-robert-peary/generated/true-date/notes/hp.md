## Historical basis

- Robert Edwin Peary was born on May 6, 1856, in Cresson, Pennsylvania. His father died within three years of his birth, and his mother raised him in southern Maine. **D**
- He graduated from Bowdoin College as a civil engineer in 1877, joined the Coast and Geodetic Survey in Washington in 1879 and the U.S. Navy Civil Engineer Corps in 1881, and surveyed in Nicaragua for a proposed canal; there he hired Matthew Henson, the African American who proved indispensable to his Arctic work. **D**
- From 1886 to 1909 he planned and led eight Arctic expeditions, one of them four years long, relying on the help of Inughuit (Polar Inuit) men and women of northwestern Greenland, who hunted, sledged, sewed and overwintered with his parties. On the 1891 to 1892 expedition he showed that Greenland is an island. **D**
- On July 11, 1891, aboard the Kite in the ice of Melville Bay, the iron tiller, jammed over by an ice cake, caught his leg against the deckhouse and broke both bones just above the ankle; the leg was set and he was carried ashore at the winter camp. **D**
- In 1897, at the request of staff of the American Museum of Natural History, who had asked for one Inughuit man to work with the museum for a year, he brought six Inughuit people from Greenland to New York: three men, a woman and two children. Within months four of them had died; a boy of about seven, whose father was among the dead, was left an orphan far from home, and the museum kept his father's bones instead of burying them. **D**
- On the expedition of 1898 to 1902, reaching the old station at Fort Conger on Ellesmere Island at midnight on January 6, 1899, he found that both feet were frozen; in March a doctor amputated the affected toes. He lost most of his toes, and the stumps were often left bloody and aching by knocks against rough ice on later journeys. **D**
- The number of toes he lost is uncertain: the roster says eight, and a historian of the expedition notes that the exact number and circumstances have been mythologized. **R**
- He married in 1888 and had two children with his wife. He also had two sons with an Inughuit woman in Greenland, born during his expeditions; his daughter's papers name them as her half-brothers, and one of them was an infant in the winter of 1900 to 1901. **D**
- On April 6, 1909, he reported reaching the North Pole with Matthew Henson and four Inughuit men, Ootah, Egingwah, Seegloo and Ooqueah (Odaq, Iggianguaq, Sigluk and Ukkujaaq in today's spelling). Frederick Cook announced a competing claim days before Peary returned; most geographic bodies came to credit Peary, but the claim has long been disputed. **D**
- He retired from the Navy in 1911 as a rear admiral with the Thanks of Congress, and lived his last years with his family in Washington, D.C., spending summers on Eagle Island on the Maine coast; the dispute over his claim cast a pall over those years. **D**
