## Historical evidence

1. National Park Service, Chiricahua National Monument. “The Apache Wars Part II: Geronimo.” Supports that he “was born on the Gila River in New Mexico, not far from the Gila Cliff Dwellings”; that “his birth name, Goyakla, meant ‘one who yawns’”; that he “was not a chief, but a medicine man of the Bedonkehe band of the Chiricahua Apache”; that as a young man he “had lost one of his wives, some of his children, and his mother to a massacre” by Mexican soldiers; and that he heard Ussen's voice say “You will never die in battle, nor will you die by gun,” which he “believed … to be true for the rest of his life.” https://www.nps.gov/chir/learn/historyculture/apache-wars-geronimo.htm
2. Wilson R. “Geronimo.” *The Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History and Culture* (Oklahoma Historical Society). Supports a birth “in the 1820s, perhaps near present Clifton, Arizona”; that “his Apache name was Goyahkla (One Who Yawns)”; that “Mexican troops killed his mother, wife, and children in 1850”; the surrender to Miles in September 1886; exile to Florida, Alabama and, in 1894, Fort Sill; farming at Fort Sill; appearances at the 1898 Omaha, 1901 Buffalo and 1904 St. Louis expositions; riding in an inauguration parade in 1905; and his death at Fort Sill on February 17, 1909, and burial in the fort's Apache cemetery. https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry?entry=GE009
3. Barrett SM, ed. *Geronimo's Story of His Life* (New York: Duffield, 1906), Project Gutenberg eBook 31318. His own account, told through the interpreter Asa Deklugie: “I was born in No-doyohn Cañon, Arizona, June, 1829”; the attack at Kas-ki-yeh “in the summer of 1858,” when “my aged mother, my young wife, and my three small children were among the slain,” after which “I had no purpose left,” “I spoke to no one and no one spoke to me,” he burned his family's belongings and “was never again contented in our quiet home” (with the editor's note that by custom the property of deceased relatives was not kept); his eight wounds, including “shot in the right leg above the knee, and still carry the bullet” and “wounded on top of the head with the butt of a musket”; hard labor at Fort Pickens and no sight of the families “until May, 1887”; that in Alabama “so many of our people died”; that “we are held as prisoners of war in this Military Reservation (Fort Sill)”; that he asked to be relieved from labor “on account of my age” but likes to work; family separations and a marriage in December 1905; and that “since I have been a prisoner of war I have been arrested and placed in the guardhouse twice for drinking whisky.” https://www.gutenberg.org/files/31318/31318-h/31318-h.htm
4. National Park Service, Chiricahua National Monument. “Post Apache Wars.” Supports the final surrender “in Skeleton Canyon in southern Arizona, the summer of 1886”; that the men were not reunited with their families as promised; that “many Chiricahua died of malaria while in confinement”; that in Alabama many children “caught tuberculosis and died”; that “there were only 119 left” on the move to Oklahoma; his request to the president to return to the Southwest, which was refused; and that in 1909 he “was returning from a drinking binge one night when he fell off his horse into a ditch,” “stayed in the ditch until morning and contracted pneumonia,” and “passed away days later.” https://www.nps.gov/chir/learn/historyculture/post-apache-wars.htm
5. National Archives at Riverside. “Indictment of Geronimo, an Apache Indian, 1887.” Supports that “in 1851, when Geronimo was a young man, the Mexican Army attacked his camp, resulting in the death of his wife, children, and mother,” and that after his capture in 1886 he “spent the next 23 years of his life as a prisoner of war,” allowed out only for fairs, exhibits and an inauguration. https://www.archives.gov/riverside/highlights/geronimo
6. Jackson RJ. “Geronimo's bones.” *Oklahoma Gazette*, February 15, 2011. Supports that “on a cold February day, Geronimo rode into Lawton,” obtained whisky through a young man who bought it for him, “tumbled from his horse” after nightfall, was found the next morning “lying partly in water a short distance from his house,” “contracted pneumonia,” was taken to the post hospital, and died on the morning of February 17, 1909, “79 years old” by his own account; and that “bullet-hole scars checkered Geronimo's body.” https://www.okgazette.com/?p=6988
7. HISTMED Master Roster v3. Internal project source; not treated as independent corroboration.

## Editorial resolutions

- **Sources.** The case relies on two National Park Service pages (Chiricahua National Monument), the Oklahoma Historical Society encyclopedia, the National Archives at Riverside, his own autobiography as told to S. M. Barrett (public domain, read in full from Project Gutenberg), and an Oklahoma Gazette feature for the circumstances of his last illness, which the National Park Service corroborates. Angie Debo's biography was not read, and no reliable source quoting it was found, so nothing in the record rests on it. The Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, the Fort Sill National Historic Landmark and Museum, and a Fort Bowie National Historic Site page (the address tried returned not found) were not read. Popular and fan sites found in searches were not used. No Apache-authored source other than his own dictated account was read.
- **Recent-death rule.** He died in 1909, so the rule does not apply. A sick visit is included.
- **Birth date.** He gave June 1829 and No-doyohn Cañon; the National Park Service gives the Gila River in New Mexico near the Gila Cliff Dwellings without a year; the Oklahoma Historical Society gives the 1820s, perhaps near Clifton, Arizona. The preferred birth is June 1829 (precision month), with the 1820s kept as an unresolved alternative. A day of June 16 appears on popular sites but not in any source read and is not used. Because the day is unknown, the synthetic date of birth uses June 1 as a placeholder: 1949-06-01, a shift of 120 years. The true-date variant therefore places his birth on June 1, 1829, a modeling artifact, not a claim.
- **Index age and dates.** He is 77 at the index visit on July 8, 2026, which maps to July 8, 1906, at Fort Sill, where he was held as a prisoner of war and where he dictated his autobiography in 1905 and 1906 (the editor's letters are dated at Fort Sill from August 1905). The visits on July 22, August 19, September 2, September 23, October 7 and October 28, 2026 map to the same days of 1906, within his lifetime. His day-to-day whereabouts in those months are not documented in the sources read; his travels under guard to expositions (1898, 1901, 1904) and the 1905 inauguration fall outside the episode. Past medical history includes only events before the index date. The 1909 death claim is linked only to the 1909 timeline event and is omitted from intake documents.
- **Name.** He is known as Geronimo, a single name, and has no family name in the Western sense. identity.name_parts places the single name in the family-name field with an empty given-name list, as cases/HR-NI015-sitting-bull does: the C-CDA carries the required given-name element as a null flavor, the FHIR name has no given part, and the FHIR name text and display name are “Geronimo.” The notes call him Geronimo, as he asked in the modern episode, never “Mr. Geronimo.” His birth name is spelled Goyakla by the National Park Service and Goyahkla by the Oklahoma Historical Society; both are aliases and appear in no other record. The form Goyaałé, used by some library and language references, was not confirmed in a reliable source that could be read (one library cooperative page was blocked) and is not used. Barrett's spelling of the interpreter's name, Asa Deklugie, is kept, with the common spelling Daklugie noted.
- **Medicine man and spiritual power.** The National Park Service calls him “a medicine man”; that phrase appears only as quoted in CLM-0002. His belief that Usen told him he would never die in battle or by a gun (CLM-0003) is recorded as religious history. It is not interpreted, coded or treated as a symptom, and it is not linked to his drinking or his last illness. No ceremony is described in the modern episode; traditional healing is offered through the clinic's traditional medicine program at his choice, and the clinic asks nothing about his practices, following IHS policy. Ceremonial tobacco is not asked about; only commercial tobacco is.
- **The killings at Kas-ki-yeh.** That Mexican troops killed his mother, his wife and his three children is documented by his own account and by the National Archives and the Oklahoma Historical Society (CLM-0004, D, probable). The year is disputed: 1858 in his own account, 1851 by the National Archives, 1850 by the Oklahoma Historical Society; the National Park Service gives no year and says “one of his wives” and “some of his children.” The assignment's lead of Janos and 1851 or 1858 is reflected only through these sources: his own account names the town Kas-ki-yeh and does not name Janos, and no source read identifies Kas-ki-yeh with Janos, so Janos is not used. The timeline uses 1851 (National Archives) with the other years in its display text. The family history records the mother and the three children as killed in an attack (not a medical cause of death); the wife is a spouse and is not entered in the family history, but appears in the claim and the past medical history.
