Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Sources. The case relies on two National Park Service pages (Chiricahua National Monument), the Oklahoma Historical Society encyclopedia, the National Archives at Riverside, his own autobiography as told to S. M. Barrett (public domain, read in full from Project Gutenberg), and an Oklahoma Gazette feature for the circumstances of his last illness, which the National Park Service corroborates. Angie Debo's biography was not read, and no reliable source quoting it was found, so nothing in the record rests on it. The Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, the Fort Sill National Historic Landmark and Museum, and a Fort Bowie National Historic Site page (the address tried returned not found) were not read. Popular and fan sites found in searches were not used. No Apache-authored source other than his own dictated account was read.
  2. Recent-death rule. He died in 1909, so the rule does not apply. A sick visit is included.
  3. Birth date. He gave June 1829 and No-doyohn Cañon; the National Park Service gives the Gila River in New Mexico near the Gila Cliff Dwellings without a year; the Oklahoma Historical Society gives the 1820s, perhaps near Clifton, Arizona. The preferred birth is June 1829 (precision month), with the 1820s kept as an unresolved alternative. A day of June 16 appears on popular sites but not in any source read and is not used. Because the day is unknown, the synthetic date of birth uses June 1 as a placeholder: 1949-06-01, a shift of 120 years. The true-date variant therefore places his birth on June 1, 1829, a modeling artifact, not a claim.
  4. Index age and dates. He is 77 at the index visit on July 8, 2026, which maps to July 8, 1906, at Fort Sill, where he was held as a prisoner of war and where he dictated his autobiography in 1905 and 1906 (the editor's letters are dated at Fort Sill from August 1905). The visits on July 22, August 19, September 2, September 23, October 7 and October 28, 2026 map to the same days of 1906, within his lifetime. His day-to-day whereabouts in those months are not documented in the sources read; his travels under guard to expositions (1898, 1901, 1904) and the 1905 inauguration fall outside the episode. Past medical history includes only events before the index date. The 1909 death claim is linked only to the 1909 timeline event and is omitted from intake documents.
  5. Name. He is known as Geronimo, a single name, and has no family name in the Western sense. identity.name_parts places the single name in the family-name field with an empty given-name list, as cases/HR-NI015-sitting-bull does: the C-CDA carries the required given-name element as a null flavor, the FHIR name has no given part, and the FHIR name text and display name are “Geronimo.” The notes call him Geronimo, as he asked in the modern episode, never “Mr. Geronimo.” His birth name is spelled Goyakla by the National Park Service and Goyahkla by the Oklahoma Historical Society; both are aliases and appear in no other record. The form Goyaałé, used by some library and language references, was not confirmed in a reliable source that could be read (one library cooperative page was blocked) and is not used. Barrett's spelling of the interpreter's name, Asa Deklugie, is kept, with the common spelling Daklugie noted.
  6. Medicine man and spiritual power. The National Park Service calls him “a medicine man”; that phrase appears only as quoted in CLM-0002. His belief that Usen told him he would never die in battle or by a gun (CLM-0003) is recorded as religious history. It is not interpreted, coded or treated as a symptom, and it is not linked to his drinking or his last illness. No ceremony is described in the modern episode; traditional healing is offered through the clinic's traditional medicine program at his choice, and the clinic asks nothing about his practices, following IHS policy. Ceremonial tobacco is not asked about; only commercial tobacco is.
  7. The killings at Kas-ki-yeh. That Mexican troops killed his mother, his wife and his three children is documented by his own account and by the National Archives and the Oklahoma Historical Society (CLM-0004, D, probable). The year is disputed: 1858 in his own account, 1851 by the National Archives, 1850 by the Oklahoma Historical Society; the National Park Service gives no year and says “one of his wives” and “some of his children.” The assignment's lead of Janos and 1851 or 1858 is reflected only through these sources: his own account names the town Kas-ki-yeh and does not name Janos, and no source read identifies Kas-ki-yeh with Janos, so Janos is not used. The timeline uses 1851 (National Archives) with the other years in its display text. The family history records the mother and the three children as killed in an attack (not a medical cause of death); the wife is a spouse and is not entered in the family history, but appears in the claim and the past medical history.
  8. His own words about grief. CLM-0005 quotes his description of the night of the killings and the time after (sensitive). It is history, not a retrospective diagnosis. The editor's footnote that by custom the property of deceased relatives was not kept is recorded so that burning his family's belongings is read as mourning practice, not as a symptom.
  9. Wounds and head injury. His own list of eight wounds (it names seven) is recorded in the past medical history (CLM-0006), including a bullet he still carried in the right thigh and a blow to the head that knocked him senseless. No lasting effect is documented. The modern episode notes the scars and uses the old head injury only as one reason for a Mini-Cog; no retained-fragment work-up or brain injury diagnosis is added, since no source documents a lasting problem.
  10. Captivity. The surrender in 1886, hard labor at Fort Pickens apart from his family, the deaths of many Chiricahua prisoners from malaria and tuberculosis, and his status as a prisoner of war at Fort Sill (CLM-0007 to CLM-0009) are recorded as history and social history. In the modern frame he lives on the military reservation near Fort Sill under federal restrictions on travel; this is a synthetic rendering of his documented status, not a claim about any modern detention. No code is assigned for his custody or social circumstances. The inauguration of 1905 is recorded as “a presidential inauguration parade” without naming the president, who has his own record in the dataset.
  11. Alcohol. His own statement that he was twice jailed in the guardhouse for drinking whisky as a prisoner of war (CLM-0012) anchors the synthetic moderate alcohol use disorder (F10.20); it is marked sensitive and is not a retrospective diagnosis. The 1909 drinking, fall and pneumonia (CLM-0014, sensitive) are history after the episode, recorded plainly. The modern fall (PRB-004) is synthetic and is not the fall of 1909; the sick-visit bronchitis is not the pneumonia of 1909; neither is presented as foreshadowing or as a claim that modern care would have changed the outcome.
  12. Diagnoses and presenting problem. Poor sleep with nightmares and intrusive memories (G47.00) is the presenting problem, resolved at the consultation when chronic post-traumatic stress disorder (F43.12) was confirmed on the CAPS-5. Both F43.12 and F10.20 are placed both_with_link, because each is a synthetic modern diagnosis anchored to documented history. The roster's PTSD and traumatic grief leads are kept as one R claim (CLM-0015, possible) and are not made historical diagnoses.
  13. Prolonged grief disorder. Considered and not diagnosed (judgment call): his grief is lasting but not the near-daily, consuming yearning or preoccupation that DSM-5-TR requires, and his mourning follows the customs of his people, which the criteria say must be exceeded. The distress that brought him in is attributed to post-traumatic stress. This avoids turning mourning into a disorder.
  14. CAPS-5 and instruments. The CAPS-5 has no LOINC code on the terminology server; its result (criteria met in all four clusters, severity 44) is recorded as narrative. The PC-PTSD-5 (102017-1), PCL-5 (101698-9), AUDIT (75624-7), PHQ-2, PHQ-9, Mini-Cog (72233-0) and PEth (74661-0) are coded. The Timed Up and Go is narrative. Instruments were given aloud through the interpreter; the PCL-5 and AUDIT have not been validated in Chiricahua Apache, which is a limit of the scores, noted here.
  15. Prazosin. The assignment said prazosin is not recommended for nightmares by the VA/DoD 2023 guideline. The guideline's Quick Reference Guide shows the opposite for nightmares: recommendation 32 suggests prazosin for nightmares associated with PTSD (weak for), while recommendation 18 suggests against prazosin for PTSD overall. The record follows the guideline as published. Prazosin is not started (judgment call) because of orthostatic falls risk in a man of 77 who had just fallen and was drinking, and it is to be reconsidered with standing blood pressures if nightmares persist; by sixteen weeks it was not needed.
  16. Therapy and medicine choices. Cognitive processing therapy was chosen over prolonged exposure because it can be delivered without a written trauma account, its worksheets can be done aloud through an interpreter, and it does not require detailed retelling, which he declined (judgment call). He chose to start therapy first and sertraline four weeks later; sertraline began at 25 mg and rose to 50 mg; paroxetine was avoided at his age; venlafaxine was the alternative. No benzodiazepine was used. The therapist follows his lead on how to speak of those who died; the record does not assert a specific custom about naming the dead, because no source read documents one.
  17. Naltrexone. Started at the consultation with AST under three times the upper limit, normal kidney function and a urine test negative for opioids; acamprosate was the documented alternative. No withdrawal medicine was given because he had no history of complicated withdrawal and had cut down without symptoms (judgment call).
