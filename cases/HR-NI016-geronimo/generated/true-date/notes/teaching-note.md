# Teaching Note — Geronimo pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** By his own account he was born in June 1829 in No-doyohn Cañon, in the country around the headwaters of the Gila River; the National Park Service places his birth on the Gila River in New Mexico, not far from the Gila Cliff Dwellings, and the Oklahoma Historical Society gives the 1820s, perhaps near present Clifton, Arizona. His Apache birth name is written Goyakla by the National Park Service and Goyahkla by the Oklahoma Historical Society, meaning one who yawns; he belonged to the Bedonkohe (Bedonkehe) band of the Chiricahua Apache and, in the Park Service's words, was not a chief but a medicine man. He prayed to Usen, the Apache word for God, and believed throughout his life that Usen had told him he would never die in battle or by a gun. While his band was trading at a Mexican town the Apaches called Kas-ki-yeh, Mexican troops attacked the camp; when he returned he found that his aged mother, his young wife and his three small children were among the dead. His own account dates this to the summer of 1858; the National Archives gives 1851 and the Oklahoma Historical Society 1850. In his own words, that night he stood alone by the river, did not vote in the council, had no weapon and hardly wished to fight, and 'had no purpose left'; on the march home he did not eat and spoke to no one. At home he burned his family's belongings, vowed vengeance, and wrote that he was never again contented in his quiet home. He listed eight wounds received in his wars with Mexican forces, among them a gunshot to the right leg above the knee (the bullet still carried), a gunshot through the left forearm, a saber wound below the right knee, a blow to the top of the head with a musket butt that knocked him senseless, a shot just below the outer corner of the left eye, and shots in the left side and the back. His final surrender was in Skeleton Canyon in southern Arizona in the summer of 1886 (September 1886, by the Oklahoma Historical Society). The promise that the men would see their families in Florida was not kept: he and his warriors were held at Fort Pickens, at hard labor sawing logs, and did not see their families until May 1887. As prisoners of war the Chiricahua Apache were held in Florida, then at Mount Vernon in Alabama, and from 1894 at Fort Sill; many died of malaria in Florida, many children died of tuberculosis in Alabama, and only 119 remained when they were moved to Oklahoma. In his words, so many of his people died in Alabama that he let one of his wives go to the Mescalero Agency. At Fort Sill he was still held as a prisoner of war; the Apaches lived in houses the government built, raised cattle and farmed, and he was relieved from assigned labor because of his age but worked to help his people. He was held as a prisoner of war for the last 23 years of his life. He was allowed to leave Fort Sill under guard to appear at national expositions in Omaha (1898), Buffalo (1901) and St. Louis (1904), and he rode in a presidential inauguration parade in 1905, where he asked to be allowed to return to the Southwest with his people and was refused. In 1905 and 1906, with permission from the President and the officers at Fort Sill, he told his life story to S. M. Barrett through the interpreter Asa Deklugie (also written Daklugie), on condition that Barrett write what he had spoken; it was published in 1906 as Geronimo's Story of His Life. In his autobiography he said that since he had been a prisoner of war he had twice been arrested and placed in the guardhouse for drinking whisky. He described further losses and separations in his family during captivity: a wife who left for Mescalero with their two small children, the death of another wife, and a marriage in December 1905 that soon ended in separation. He died at Fort Sill on February 17, 1909, of pneumonia. On a cold February day he had ridden into Lawton and obtained whisky; returning after nightfall he fell from his horse and lay until morning near his house, partly in water. He was taken to the post hospital and died days later, aged 79 by his own reckoning, and was buried in the Apache cemetery at Fort Sill.
