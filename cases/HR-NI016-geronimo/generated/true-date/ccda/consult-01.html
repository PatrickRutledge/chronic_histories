History of Present Illness

Geronimo, a Chiricahua Apache elder of 77, is seen with the clinic interpreter at the request of Dr. Whitehorse-Lindgren. He asked to be called Geronimo. He told me, in his own words and only as far as he chose, that as a young man he came back to his camp to find his mother, his wife and his three children killed by soldiers, that he fought for many years afterwards, and that since his surrender he and his people have been held far from their country. For many years he has slept badly; the killings and old battles come back in dreams most nights and in his mind by day, he wakes ready to fight, he startles at sudden sounds, and he keeps away from talk of the past. He drinks whisky at night to sleep and has cut down since intake. He has not thought of ending his life. He grieves his family and his people, and he said that this grief is his and is kept in the way his people keep it. [S]

Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • By his own account he was born in June 1829 in No-doyohn Cañon, in the country around the headwaters of the Gila River; the National Park Service places his birth on the Gila River in New Mexico, not far from the Gila Cliff Dwellings, and the Oklahoma Historical Society gives the 1820s, perhaps near present Clifton, Arizona. [D]
  • His Apache birth name is written Goyakla by the National Park Service and Goyahkla by the Oklahoma Historical Society, meaning one who yawns; he belonged to the Bedonkohe (Bedonkehe) band of the Chiricahua Apache and, in the Park Service's words, was not a chief but a medicine man. [D]
  • He prayed to Usen, the Apache word for God, and believed throughout his life that Usen had told him he would never die in battle or by a gun. [D]
  • While his band was trading at a Mexican town the Apaches called Kas-ki-yeh, Mexican troops attacked the camp; when he returned he found that his aged mother, his young wife and his three small children were among the dead. His own account dates this to the summer of 1858; the National Archives gives 1851 and the Oklahoma Historical Society 1850. [D]
  • In his own words, that night he stood alone by the river, did not vote in the council, had no weapon and hardly wished to fight, and 'had no purpose left'; on the march home he did not eat and spoke to no one. At home he burned his family's belongings, vowed vengeance, and wrote that he was never again contented in his quiet home. [D]
  • He listed eight wounds received in his wars with Mexican forces, among them a gunshot to the right leg above the knee (the bullet still carried), a gunshot through the left forearm, a saber wound below the right knee, a blow to the top of the head with a musket butt that knocked him senseless, a shot just below the outer corner of the left eye, and shots in the left side and the back. [D]
  • His final surrender was in Skeleton Canyon in southern Arizona in the summer of 1886 (September 1886, by the Oklahoma Historical Society). The promise that the men would see their families in Florida was not kept: he and his warriors were held at Fort Pickens, at hard labor sawing logs, and did not see their families until May 1887. [D]
  • As prisoners of war the Chiricahua Apache were held in Florida, then at Mount Vernon in Alabama, and from 1894 at Fort Sill; many died of malaria in Florida, many children died of tuberculosis in Alabama, and only 119 remained when they were moved to Oklahoma. In his words, so many of his people died in Alabama that he let one of his wives go to the Mescalero Agency. [D]
  • At Fort Sill he was still held as a prisoner of war; the Apaches lived in houses the government built, raised cattle and farmed, and he was relieved from assigned labor because of his age but worked to help his people. He was held as a prisoner of war for the last 23 years of his life. [D]
  • He was allowed to leave Fort Sill under guard to appear at national expositions in Omaha (1898), Buffalo (1901) and St. Louis (1904), and he rode in a presidential inauguration parade in 1905, where he asked to be allowed to return to the Southwest with his people and was refused. [D]
  • In 1905 and 1906, with permission from the President and the officers at Fort Sill, he told his life story to S. M. Barrett through the interpreter Asa Deklugie (also written Daklugie), on condition that Barrett write what he had spoken; it was published in 1906 as Geronimo's Story of His Life. [D]
  • In his autobiography he said that since he had been a prisoner of war he had twice been arrested and placed in the guardhouse for drinking whisky. [D]
  • He described further losses and separations in his family during captivity: a wife who left for Mescalero with their two small children, the death of another wife, and a marriage in December 1905 that soon ended in separation. [D]

Assessment

  • Post-traumatic stress disorder, chronic (F43.12). On the CAPS-5 he meets criteria in all four symptom clusters (intrusion, avoidance, negative changes in thinking and mood, and arousal), with a severity score of 44, lasting decades and impairing his sleep and daily life. The diagnosis is chronic post-traumatic stress disorder, rooted in the killing of his family, years of combat and captivity. His Mini-Cog of 4 does not suggest dementia; cognition should be rechecked once he sleeps better and drinks less. [S]
  • Grief and prolonged grief disorder. Prolonged grief disorder requires, nearly every day for at least the past month, intense yearning for or preoccupation with the dead with further symptoms such as identity disruption, disbelief or intense emotional pain, beyond what his culture would expect. His grief is lasting and deep but is not that daily, consuming yearning, and the way he mourns follows the customs of his people. The criteria are not met. His grief should be respected as grief, not treated as a disorder; the distress that brings him in is driven by the post-traumatic symptoms. [S]
  • Psychotherapy (F43.12). The VA/DoD guideline recommends an individual, manualized trauma-focused psychotherapy (cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure or EMDR) over medicine, and the APA guideline strongly recommends cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure. Cognitive processing therapy suits him: it can be given without the written trauma account, its worksheets can be done aloud through an interpreter, and it does not require him to retell the killings in detail, which he has said he will not do. For a man of 77: a steady pace, shorter sessions when he tires, a consistent interpreter, and video sessions when he cannot come in, which the guideline supports. The therapist should ask how he wishes to speak of those who died and follow his lead. [S]
  • Medicines (F43.12). Sertraline, paroxetine and venlafaxine are recommended by the VA/DoD guideline; the APA guideline suggests fluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline and venlafaxine. Paroxetine is best avoided at his age because of its anticholinergic effects. If he wants a medicine, sertraline 25 mg rising to 50 mg is my choice, with sodium checked within the first month because SSRIs can lower sodium in older adults, and attention to bleeding and falls. Benzodiazepines should not be used. The VA/DoD guideline suggests prazosin for nightmares associated with PTSD, though not for PTSD as a whole; in a man of 77 who has just fallen and is drinking, its first-dose and standing drops in blood pressure outweigh the benefit for now. Reconsider it, with standing pressures, if nightmares persist after therapy. [S]
  • Alcohol use disorder, moderate (F10.20). I agree with four DSM-5-TR criteria. The VA/DoD guideline advises that a substance use disorder should not delay trauma-focused therapy, so both are treated together. Naltrexone suits him: AST is under three times the upper limit, his kidneys are normal, and he takes no opioids; liver tests should be repeated at four to six weeks. Acamprosate, dosed by kidney function, is the alternative if naltrexone cannot be used. He has no history of withdrawal seizures or delirium and has cut down without symptoms, so no withdrawal medicine is needed. [S]
