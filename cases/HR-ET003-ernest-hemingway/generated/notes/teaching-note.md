# Teaching Note — Ernest Hemingway pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Ernest Hemingway was born on July 21, 1899, in Oak Park, Illinois, the son of a physician. In the First World War he joined a volunteer ambulance unit in the Italian army; in 1918 he was wounded at the front near Fossalta di Piave by a mortar shell, was decorated by the Italian government, and spent a long time in hospitals. He drank heavily for most of his adult life. In January 1954, on a safari in Africa, he survived two plane crashes on consecutive days in Uganda. In the second he forced a jammed door open with his head and suffered a concussion and a skull fracture; he also reported a ruptured right kidney and injuries to his liver and spleen. The Nobel Prize biography records that the accidents caused severe pain for the rest of his life. He was awarded the 1954 Nobel Prize in Literature and could not attend the ceremony in Stockholm because of ill health. His home from 1939 to 1960 was Finca Vigía, a farm on a hillside outside Havana, Cuba, where he wrote much of his work. His father, a physician who was described as depressed and diabetic, died by suicide in 1928. Several earlier members of his family also had depression and died by suicide, and he understood himself to be descended from men who had died by suicide on both sides of his family. In December 1960 he was admitted to the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, under a false name, publicly for high blood pressure but in fact for severe depression; he was treated with electroconvulsive therapy, discharged in January 1961, and readmitted after a relapse in April 1961. The clinic recorded severe hypertension, prediabetes and dyslipidemia. In his last years he had paranoid fears, including a belief that he was under FBI surveillance; the FBI did keep a file on him. He complained that the electroconvulsive treatments had damaged his memory, which he saw as essential to his work as a writer. He died by suicide at his home in Ketchum, Idaho, on July 2, 1961.
- **R:** A family history of hemochromatosis has been described. Whether he had hereditary hemochromatosis is disputed. Some accounts state that he had it; one review says it was suspected but that a liver biopsy was not done because of his poor health; a retired physician is reported to have recalled a normal serum iron level. Bipolar disorder has been proposed retrospectively, but a psychiatrist who studied his history found no true manic episode. The psychiatrist Andrew Farah, in Hemingway's Brain (2017), proposed that repeated concussions, at least nine major ones, caused chronic traumatic encephalopathy, complicated by alcohol, diabetes and hypertension, with a mixed dementia; a 2022 neurology review also describes cognitive decline in his last years.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
