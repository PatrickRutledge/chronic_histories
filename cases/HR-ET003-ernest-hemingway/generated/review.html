  1. Sources. The case uses three NobelPrize.org pages, a peer-reviewed neurology review, a medical historian's PBS article, a medical humanities essay, an interview with Andrew Farah in Psychiatric Times, a Smithsonian article quoting his own 1954 letter, the Fresh Air archive and a WBUR report. Pages from the presidential library that holds his papers were not used, because the library's name is another record's name. Dealer listings and quotation sites found in searches were not used.
  2. Not verified and left out. The exact number of electroconvulsive treatments (the PBS article says “believed” at least 15); the exact wording of his complaint about the treatments erasing his memory (quoted only on secondary pages); the reported illness in the autumn of 1955 (found only in a dealer listing); and the specific relatives with hemochromatosis. None is a claim.
  3. Hemochromatosis (CLM-0013, R, disputed; CLM-0009, R, possible). Two sources say he had it, one says it was only suspected and not confirmed by biopsy, and Farah reports a recollection of a normal serum iron. The modern hereditary hemochromatosis is synthetic and teaches the modern diagnosis; it is not offered as proof. No relative is named as having it, and the modern record attributes no test or result to any real relative; testing for first-degree relatives is offered only as advice to him.
  4. Bipolar disorder (CLM-0014, R, disputed). The roster lists probable bipolar I disorder, and two sources name bipolar disorder, but Farah found no true manic episode. It is not a modern diagnosis; the modern depression is synthetic, single-episode, and the Mood Disorder Questionnaire is negative.
  5. Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CLM-0015, R, possible). Farah's proposal is recorded as a retrospective claim only. CTE can be confirmed only by examining the brain after death, so the modern record codes post-concussion symptoms (F07.81) on the documented head injuries (CLM-0002, CLM-0004), records the MoCA as a survey, and states in the problem and plan that CTE is not diagnosed. The claim is linked to the 1960 timeline event so that it does not appear in intake documents as if known in 1955.
  6. PTSD. The roster lists PTSD. No source read documents a diagnosis or describes symptoms specifically, so it is neither a claim nor a modern diagnosis.
  7. Diabetes and hypertension. The roster lists both. His father's diabetes is documented (CLM-0007) and appears in the family history.
  8. Suicide, his father's death, and family history.   9. Mental illness in his last years. The Mayo Clinic admissions, electroconvulsive therapy, paranoid fears and his complaint about memory (CLM-0010 to CLM-0012) are history after the episode, recorded without detail and linked only to the 1960 timeline event.
  10. Living and real relatives. No relative is named or quoted in the modern episode, and the household history names no one. His wife at the time of the crashes is not named. The friend and biographer interviewed on Fresh Air is named only as a source.
  11. Index age, dates and place.   12. Index age choice. Age 55 was chosen, after the 1954 crashes, so that the episode can teach post-concussion care, hemochromatosis and alcohol care together while he was still writing, before the documented decline of his last years.
  13. Consultant specialty. The consultant is Ingrid Solheim, MD, in hepatology rather than psychiatry: the referral question at intake (raised iron studies and liver tests in a heavy drinker) needed liver staging and a phlebotomy plan, while depression is treated in primary care with an in-clinic behavioral health clinician (a collaborative-care model), which keeps the record to one referral.
  14. Synthetic clinical picture. Everything in the modern episode is synthetic: the drinking history (about ten drinks a day) and the DSM-5-TR criteria, every score (AUDIT 26, AUDIT-C, PHQ-9 with item 9 scored 1 at intake, MoCA 25 and 27; the C-SSRS screen and Mood Disorder Questionnaire are narrative), all laboratory values (transferrin saturation 76%, ferritin 1,460 to 268 ng/mL, aminotransferases, HbA1c 6.1 and 5.9%), the HFE genotype (narrative), the elastography and MRI results, the ECG and echocardiogram, the firearms at home and their storage at a shooting club, the medicines and the outcomes. Height (183 cm) and weights are synthetic. HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C screens are narrative.
  15. Clinical judgment calls. (1) Naltrexone was chosen over acamprosate because his first goal was to cut down, and was judged safe with aminotransferases under three times the upper limit, with liver tests repeated. (2) He reduced gradually and then stopped without medicine for withdrawal, because his intake was reduced over weeks and he had no history of complicated withdrawal; a medically managed withdrawal would be needed for an abrupt stop at ten drinks a day. (3) Sertraline was chosen because duloxetine carries liver risk with heavy drinking, bupropion lowers the seizure threshold during alcohol reduction, and sedating antidepressants add to alcohol; tricyclics are dangerous in overdose. (4) The liver was staged by elastography and MRI, not biopsy, despite a ferritin over 1,000 ng/mL, as the ACG guideline allows; a biopsy remained the fallback. (5) Liver cancer surveillance was not started because the MRI showed no advanced fibrosis. (6) Lethal-means counseling was framed around his own hunting and his choice of off-site storage; when he asked to bring the guns home he chose to wait, and safe storage was planned for their return. (7) Amlodipine was chosen for hypertension because it needs no laboratory monitoring. (8) Brain imaging was not ordered for chronic post-concussion symptoms without warning signs.
  16. Sick visit. A fishhook puncture of the hand with early cellulitis after saltwater fishing, chosen because it fits his documented fishing and tests the record: iron overload and liver disease raise the risk of severe Vibrio vulnificus infection, so doxycycline was added to cephalexin; naltrexone means opioids would not work and none was needed; acetaminophen was limited to 2,000 mg a day; Tdap was given; that week's phlebotomy was held. It is not a historical claim.
  17. Preventive visit. A periodic adult preventive visit at 56 (CPT 99396 with 99497 in plain text). Judgment calls: colonoscopy ordered; PSA declined after shared decision-making; lung cancer screening not indicated for a never-smoker; PCV20 for chronic liver disease and alcohol use disorder; recombinant zoster; combined hepatitis A and B vaccine because hepatitis B tests were nonreactive and he has chronic liver disease; the statin decision deferred to the six-month lipids; advance care planning records an unnamed agent and no specific treatment preferences.
  18. Focused visits. The four chronic-care follow-ups (two weeks with the consultation, six weeks, fourteen weeks and six months) are documented as focused visits on the AASLD 2011 and ACG 2019 hemochromatosis guidelines, the APA 2018 alcohol guideline with the ASAM withdrawal guideline, the CMID hypertension and depression monographs with the VA/DoD depression and suicide-risk guidelines, the VA/DoD mild TBI guideline and USPSTF diabetes screening. Two deviations are recorded instead of invented results: (1) the PHQ-9 was not repeated for eight weeks between the six-week and fourteen-week visits, longer than the monthly check advised; no harm resulted (his therapist saw steady improvement and the score fell), and the PHQ-9 was then done monthly by the behavioral health clinician; (2) the echocardiogram ordered at intake for stage 2 hypertension was not done by fourteen weeks because the order went to an outside imaging center that never contacted him; no harm was evident (no symptoms or murmur, a borderline ECG), it was rebooked through the clinic and done on the morning of the six-month visit. Synthetic findings introduced: nausea for three days on naltrexone, loose stools on sertraline, two nights of poor sleep after stopping alcohol, mild fibrosis on MRI, mild concentric left ventricular hypertrophy on echocardiography, and trace ankle edema on amlodipine 10 mg.
  19. Consultation. The hepatology referral and the consultation by Ingrid Solheim, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the two-week visit, are S. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: hereditary hemochromatosis with mostly genetic iron overload made more dangerous by alcohol; staging by MRI iron quantification and elastography instead of biopsy (done at six weeks: mild fibrosis); weekly phlebotomy with a hemoglobin check to a ferritin of 50 to 100 ng/mL, then maintenance; naltrexone continued with liver tests at about one month and later; no alcohol as the best course for the liver, with his own goal revisited (he chose to stop after the MRI); surveillance for liver cancer only with advanced fibrosis (not started); testing for relatives through a letter; and no iron or vitamin C supplements or raw shellfish. Her suggestion to consider an echocardiogram agrees with the hypertension echocardiogram, which was delayed (a recorded deviation) and then showed no iron-related cardiomyopathy.
