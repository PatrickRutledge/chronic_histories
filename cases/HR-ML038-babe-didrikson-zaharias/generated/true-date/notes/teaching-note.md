# Teaching Note — Babe Didrikson Zaharias pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Mildred Ella Didrikson was born in Port Arthur, Texas, on June 26, 1911, the sixth of seven children of the Norwegian immigrants Ole Nickolene and Hannah Marie (Olson) Didriksen; she later changed the spelling of her surname. In 1915 the family moved to Beaumont, Texas. Her birth year is given variously: she wrote 1914 on her application for the 1932 Olympics, and some sources, including a historical marker at her grave, give 1913, 1914 or 1915; her gravestone and baptismal certificate support 1911. At the 1932 Olympic Games in Los Angeles she won gold medals in the javelin and the 80-meter hurdles and a silver medal in the high jump, where her head-first technique was ruled a foul. She met the professional wrestler George Zaharias at the 1938 Los Angeles Open, and they married on December 23, 1938; he became her manager. She took up golf in 1933, won 17 amateur tournaments in a row between 1943 and 1947, co-founded the Ladies Professional Golf Association in 1949, and was voted the Associated Press Woman Athlete of the Half-Century in 1950. About 1950 she and her husband bought the Tampa Golf and Country Club in Florida and moved into its converted clubhouse. By the end of the 1952 golf season she was feeling extremely fatigued. She was diagnosed with cancer in April 1953 and had an operation that included a colostomy on April 17, 1953, in Beaumont, Texas. She missed the 1953 U.S. Women's Open while she recovered. She was back in tournament competition 14 weeks after the operation, received the Ben Hogan Comeback Player of the Year award for 1954, and in 1954 won five tournaments, including the U.S. Women's Open, played on July 1 to 3 at Salem Country Club in Peabody, Massachusetts, which she won by twelve strokes. The cancer returned in 1955: back surgery that year found cancer near her sacrum, and in June 1955 she re-entered John Sealy Hospital at the University of Texas Medical Branch in Galveston, where treatment could not contain its spread. She was one of the first public figures to speak openly about her cancer, and raised money and awareness for cancer education and research; in September 1955 she and her husband set up the Babe Didrikson Zaharias Fund, which financed a tumor clinic at the University of Texas Medical Branch. The American Cancer Society gave her a certificate of appreciation. She died of cancer at John Sealy Hospital in Galveston, Texas, on September 27, 1956, at 45, and was buried in Beaumont. One reference work gives September 26, and the contemporary New York Times report gave her age as 42.
- **R:** The sources read call her cancer colon cancer and do not name the operation or give any pathology. That it was a low rectal cancer removed by abdominoperineal resection is a retrospective reading of the permanent colostomy, which was then the usual result of that operation; some popular accounts call it rectal cancer.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
