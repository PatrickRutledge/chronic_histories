# Teaching Note — Wilma Rudolph pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Wilma Glodean Rudolph was born on June 23, 1940, in Saint Bethlehem, Tennessee, near Clarksville, the twentieth of twenty-two children in her father's family. She grew up in a large, poor Black family in the segregated South. She was born prematurely and weighed about 4.5 pounds at birth. She was often ill as a small child: at about age four she had double pneumonia followed by scarlet fever, and she had a number of other childhood illnesses before polio. In early childhood she had polio, which left her left leg paralyzed or partly paralyzed, with the foot turned inward. Sources give her age at the illness as an infant, about four, or about five. She wore a metal leg brace for several years (from about age six to ten in one account) and a specially made shoe. Once a week her mother took her by bus to Nashville, a 90-minute round trip, to the closest doctors who would treat her in the segregated South: physicians at Meharry Medical College, a historically Black medical school, fitted the brace and prescribed massage and heat. At home her family massaged the leg several times a day. She later wrote that a doctor told her she would never walk again, and that her mother told her she would. Accounts of her recovery differ: she took the brace off in public at about nine and a half, could move about with the brace by eight, was running by eleven, and no longer needed the brace by about twelve. At sixteen she won a bronze medal in the 4 x 100-meter relay at the 1956 Olympic Games in Melbourne. At the 1960 Games in Rome she won gold in the 100 meters, the 200 meters and the 4 x 100-meter relay, the first American woman to win three gold medals in track and field at one Olympics. She ran for the Tennessee State University Tigerbelles. After retiring from competition she worked in teaching, coaching and community work, founded an organization to help amateur track and field athletes, and in 1992 returned to Tennessee as a vice president of a Nashville hospital. In 1994, shortly after her mother's death, she was diagnosed with brain cancer; two accounts add throat cancer. Afterwards she seldom appeared in public, and she was in and out of hospitals for more than five months as the cancer spread. She died of brain cancer at her home in Brentwood, Tennessee, on November 12, 1994, at 54.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
