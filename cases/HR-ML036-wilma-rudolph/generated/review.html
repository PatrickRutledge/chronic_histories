Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Recent-death rule. Rudolph died in 1994, so the rule applies. The case uses only what was reported publicly during her life or in obituaries and reference accounts: the Deseret News obituary, the National Women’s History Museum, the U.S. Olympic & Paralympic Museum, the Tennessee Encyclopedia, Women in World History and Olympedia. Her childhood illnesses come from these accounts and from her own published account of being told she would never walk (quoted by the museums). No sick visit is added. Her death is recorded once (CLM-0010), in plain words. No claim concerns mental health or substance use; the modern mood screens are synthetic and below the threshold for a safety assessment.
  2. What is not public. The type, location, grade and treatment of her brain cancer were never made public. Every clinical detail in the modern episode (the symptoms, the MRI, the left frontal location, glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, MGMT methylation, the surgery, chemoradiation, tumor-treating fields, all values) is synthetic and is not a claim about her real illness. The throat cancer reported by two sources is recorded in CLM-0009 without detail and is not used in the modern episode.
  3. Choice of modern episode. Option (a), a primary brain tumor, was chosen: brain cancer is documented as her cause of death, and no source fetched describes new weakness, fatigue or pain in adult life, so post-polio syndrome (option b) has no public basis. Polio survivorship is kept in the history (PMH-003 to PMH-005), on the problem list as late effects of poliomyelitis (B91, linked to the polio claims), and in the modern frame as a factor in mobility and falls: the new right-sided weakness from the left frontal tumor adds to a left leg weakened in childhood, and each visit checks for new symptoms that could signal post-polio syndrome (none are found). The modern finding of a slightly smaller left calf is synthetic.
  4. Glioblastoma rather than another tumor. A primary brain tumor in a woman of 54 is most often glioblastoma, and glioblastoma teaches the full WHO CNS5 integrated diagnosis and the Stupp protocol. MGMT promoter methylation and a gross total resection were chosen so that the course teaches standard treatment, tumor-treating fields and the first post-treatment MRI without progression; the record ends during adjuvant cycle 1, before her real death, and implies nothing about the course of her real illness.
  5. Real people. No relative is named in the modern episode, and no decision, statement or health fact is attributed to a relative. Her mother appears only in the documented history (the bus trips to Nashville and her own published words about her mother), and in CLM-0009, which follows the Tennessee Encyclopedia in dating the diagnosis to shortly after her mother’s death without any health fact about her mother. Her four children are mentioned in the modern social history only as “four grown children” (the obituary says she was survived by two sons and two daughters). The modern family history is left empty. Other athletes, coaches and officials named in the sources are not used.
  6. Conflicts. (1) Birthplace: Saint Bethlehem (Olympedia, the U.S. Olympic & Paralympic Museum, the National Women’s History Museum) or Clarksville (the Tennessee Encyclopedia, the Deseret News); Saint Bethlehem is a community near Clarksville, and CLM-0001 gives both. (2) Age at polio: “as an infant” (Olympedia), about four (the museum, with pneumonia and scarlet fever at four) or five (secondary accounts not used); CLM-0004 states the range. (3) The brace: worn from six to ten (Tennessee Encyclopedia), taken off in public at 9½ and returned by 12 (Women in World History), braces that kept her from walking until about nine (Deseret News), and running by eleven (the museum); CLM-0006 records the range, and the past medical history says she walked without the brace by about twelve. (4) Length and frequency of the Meharry treatment: weekly for several years (Women in World History), weekly (the museum); other figures in secondary sites (twice a week, two years) are not used. (5) Cancer: brain cancer in every source; “brain and throat cancer” in the Tennessee Encyclopedia and the museum; Wikipedia states a diagnosis in July 1994 without a citation, and a search summary attributed that date to a relative quoted in The New York Times, so the month is not used and the diagnosis is dated only to 1994. (6) Why the family went to Nashville: the museum says it was the closest place with doctors who would treat her in the segregated South; no source fetched states that a Clarksville hospital refused her, so CLM-0005 uses the museum’s wording.
  7. Sources not used. The New York Times obituary (Litsky, November 13, 1994) and Britannica returned access errors and are not cited; a copy of the Times obituary hosted by a third party was not read. Her 1977 autobiography Wilma was not available; it is used only through the museums’ quotation of it, and its title and year are not stated as a claim. The Tennessee State Library and Archives was searched without finding a page on her medical history.
  8. Index age and dates. The index visit on June 29, 2026 maps to June 29, 1994, six days after her 54th birthday and before the 1994 diagnosis, which the sources date to shortly after her mother’s death. The synthetic date of birth is June 23, 1972 (shift 32 years).   9. Equity. As a child she was taken by bus to Nashville, a 90-minute round trip, for care at a historically Black medical school, because no closer doctor would treat her in the segregated South. The modern episode does not invent statistics about disparities in brain tumor care; it models prompt imaging, specialist review within three days, complete access to mapping surgery, molecular testing, tumor-treating fields and trial screening, and plain explanation.
