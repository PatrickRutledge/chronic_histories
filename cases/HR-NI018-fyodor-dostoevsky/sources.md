## Historical evidence

1. Young W. “Dostojewski, Fjodor Michailowitsch.” *Stadtlexikon Wiesbaden* (City of Wiesbaden, English edition). Supports his birth in Moscow on 11 November 1821 and death in St. Petersburg on 9 February 1881; his visits to Wiesbaden from 1862; that his gambling addiction began there and dominated about ten years, with losses also at Baden-Baden and Bad Homburg; that from 2 August 1865 he was stranded in Wiesbaden for about two months after gambling away his travel funds; that he wrote *The Gambler*, set in the fictional spa town Roulettenburg, in 26 days after returning to Russia; and that after his April 1871 visit he wrote “That really was the very last time!” and never returned to the roulette table. https://www.wiesbaden.de/en/stadtlexikon/stadtlexikon-a-z/dostojewski-fjodor-michailowitsch
2. Dobson S. “How Dostoevsky overcame his gambling addiction.” *The Conversation*, January 24, 2024. Supports that his gambling mania first seized him in 1863 on a tour of Europe; that he fell into a pattern of chasing his losses and often pawned belongings to gamble further; and quotes his 1871 letter (“I have rid myself of the abominable delusion that has tormented me for almost 10 years”; “This was the very last time”). https://theconversation.com/how-dostoevsky-overcame-his-gambling-addiction-220655
3. Iniesta I. “Epilepsy in Dostoevsky.” *Progress in Brain Research* 2013;205:277–93 (PubMed abstract). Supports that he had epilepsy throughout his literary career; that the first symptoms appeared in early adulthood and the diagnosis came about a decade later; that in 1863 he went abroad seeking advice from Romberg and Trousseau; that he portrayed up to six characters with epilepsy, from Murin (*The Landlady*, 1847) to Smerdyakov (*The Brothers Karamazov*, 1880); and interprets the epilepsy as cryptogenic and localization-related, of frontomedial or temporal origin. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24290270/
4. Markel H. “For Dostoevsky, epilepsy was a matter of both life and literature.” *PBS NewsHour*, November 10, 2017. Supports seizures in his student years (1838 to 1843) and a generalized tonic-clonic seizure in 1844, by one biography's account; a diagnosis of epilepsy in 1849 shortly before he was taken to the prison at Omsk; four years of forced labor; worsening seizures in prison, so that by 1853 he was quite debilitated; that he described lack of sleep, alcohol, and overwork as triggers; and that he felt the illness had damaged his memory. https://www.pbs.org/newshour/health/for-dostoevsky-epilepsy-was-a-matter-of-both-life-and-literature
5. Hughes JR. “The idiosyncratic aspects of the epilepsy of Fyodor Dostoevsky.” *Epilepsy & Behavior* 2005;7:531–8 (PubMed abstract). Retrospective interpretation: onset controversial; stress and sleep deprivation, including the stress of gambling, aggravated his seizures; doubts the ecstatic aura; proposes an idiopathic generalized epilepsy with minor temporal involvement. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16194626/
6. Rayport SM, Rayport M, Schell CA. “Dostoevsky's epilepsy: a new approach to retrospective diagnosis.” *Epilepsy & Behavior* 2011;22:557–70 (PubMed abstract). Retrospective interpretation: temporolimbic epilepsy, with the ecstatic experience an epileptic phenomenon. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21982255/
7. Tényi D, Rajna P, Janszky J, et al. “Dostoyevsky's epilepsy in the light of recent neurobiological data.” *Ideggyógyászati Szemle* 2014;67:52–5 (English abstract of a Hungarian article). Retrospective interpretation: ecstatic seizures, including his, may arise in the insular cortex. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24654447/
8. HISTMED Master Roster v3. Internal project source. Its clinical claims were provisional and were not treated as independent corroboration.

## Editorial resolutions

- **Dates.** The record uses the New Style (Gregorian) dates of birth and death given by the Wiesbaden city lexicon: 11 November 1821 and 9 February 1881. The Old Style birth date, 30 October 1821, appears in the roster brief and in search summaries, but no fetched source states it, so it is not recorded as an alternative.
- **Index age and dates.** The episode presents him at 43, between his documented first roulette losses and the Wiesbaden losses of August and September 1865, while his epilepsy was still untreated in any modern sense. The synthetic date of birth is November 11, 1982 (shift 161 years). The index visit on February 3, 2026 maps to February 3, 1865; the chronic-care visits on March 3, May 5, July 7, and October 6, 2026 map to the same days of 1865; the sick visit on June 9 and the preventive visit on October 20, 2026 map to June 9 and October 20, 1865. All fall within his lifetime. No visit falls in August or September, when the true frame places him in Wiesbaden; the synthetic gambling lapse abroad in that period is anchored to the documented Wiesbaden losses (CLM-0010) without naming the town.
- **Epilepsy.** That he had epilepsy is graded **D**. When it began and was diagnosed is graded **R** (disputed): one account gives seizures from his student years, a generalized tonic-clonic seizure in 1844, and a diagnosis in 1849; another gives a diagnosis about a decade after the first symptoms; a third calls the onset controversial. The PBS account says his 1844 seizure was described by friends; to avoid attributing observations to other real people, the record states only that the seizure is documented. The seizure type and syndrome (temporolimbic, idiopathic generalized, frontomedial or temporal, or insular) and the nature of the ecstatic aura are graded **R** (disputed). The modern problem is coded G40.909 (epilepsy, unspecified) with placement both_with_link, and the modern seizures are described as generalized tonic-clonic seizures of unknown onset, an epilepsy of unknown type under the ILAE classification, with no aura, so that the record neither settles nor echoes the retrospective debate. The ecstatic quotation in the PBS essay is not used.
- **Gambling.** His roulette gambling from 1863, chasing losses, pawning, the Wiesbaden losses of 1865, and his 1871 letter are graded **D** and marked sensitive. The modern problem is coded F63.0 (pathological gambling) with placement both_with_link; F63.0 triggers the structured safety assessment, which records no current risk, gives the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline and the National Problem Gambling Helpline (named without a number), and is repeated at every visit. Family history is left empty: no fetched source gave a family medical history in his own writing, and the retrospective claims about relatives' seizures in one review are not used.
