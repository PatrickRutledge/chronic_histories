History of Present Illness

Mr. Dostoevsky, a 43-year-old writer, is seen at the request of Dr. Lindahl. He has had generalized convulsive seizures since early adulthood, never treated, now every four to six weeks. Most are unwitnessed; those described to him by bystanders are generalized tonic-clonic, lasting one to two minutes, with tongue biting and hours of confusion afterwards. He recalls no aura and no focal features, and he has had no myoclonic jerks, absences, or seizures in clusters. The most recent, three weeks ago, followed a night without sleep. Sleep loss is the clearest trigger. He has gambling disorder in early treatment and mild depressive and anxiety symptoms. He lives alone and does not drive. [S]

Assessment

  • Epilepsy of unknown type (G40.909). Epilepsy is confirmed by many unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart; a normal EEG and MRI do not exclude it. Under the ILAE classification the seizures are generalized tonic-clonic seizures of unknown onset and the epilepsy is of unknown type, with unknown cause, because neither the history nor the tests show whether the seizures begin focally or in both hemispheres. A sleep-deprived EEG may still clarify this. Treatment is clearly indicated, because the recurrence risk is high and every generalized tonic-clonic seizure carries a risk of injury and of SUDEP. [S]
  • Choice of antiseizure medicine. For an epilepsy of unknown type the medicine should work for both focal and generalized seizures. Valproate is the most effective for generalized and unclassified epilepsy, but it causes weight gain, tremor, and liver and metabolic effects. Levetiracetam starts quickly but can cause irritability and low mood, a concern with his depressive symptoms and gambling. Lamotrigine is effective and well tolerated, has mood-stabilizing effects, and is not an enzyme inducer, but it must be increased slowly to lower the risk of a serious rash. After discussion he chose lamotrigine. [S]
  • Safety and SUDEP. Living alone, sleeping irregularly, and having uncontrolled generalized tonic-clonic seizures, some at night, are the main SUDEP risk factors. Good seizure control lowers the risk most. [S]
  • Gambling disorder and mood in relation to his epilepsy (F63.0). Nights at the tables mean lost sleep and missed doses, so his gambling and his seizures share triggers. Depression and anxiety are common in epilepsy, and all antiseizure medicines carry a warning about suicidal thoughts, which matters more because gambling disorder raises suicide risk. Lamotrigine has no interaction with the treatments planned for his gambling. [S]

Data reviewed: Dr. Lindahl's first-visit note and laboratory results; the routine EEG with photic stimulation and hyperventilation, which is normal with no epileptiform discharges; and the epilepsy-protocol MRI, which is normal with no hippocampal sclerosis, cortical malformation, or other lesion.
