# Teaching Note — Fyodor Dostoevsky pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Fyodor Mikhailovich Dostoevsky was born in Moscow on 11 November 1821 and died in St. Petersburg on 9 February 1881. Dostoevsky had epilepsy throughout his literary career; in 1863 he went abroad to seek expert advice from the neurologists Romberg and Trousseau. He was sentenced to four years of forced labor at the Siberian prison in Omsk; his seizures worsened during his imprisonment, and by 1853 he was quite debilitated by epilepsy. He described lack of sleep, alcohol, and overwork as triggers of his seizures, and he felt that the illness had damaged his memory. He portrayed up to six characters with epilepsy in his fiction, from Murin in The Landlady (1847) to Smerdyakov in The Brothers Karamazov (1880). His passion for roulette began on a tour of Europe in 1863. He fell into a pattern of chasing his losses and often pawned belongings so that he could gamble further. He gambled at Wiesbaden, Baden-Baden, and Bad Homburg, and the compulsion caused repeated financial trouble for about ten years. In August 1865 he gambled away his travel funds at Wiesbaden and was stranded there for about two months, until he obtained money to leave. After returning to Russia he wrote the novella The Gambler, set in a fictional spa town called Roulettenburg, in 26 days. In a letter of April 1871 he wrote that he had rid himself of "the abominable delusion that has tormented me for almost 10 years" and that "this was the very last time"; he never returned to the roulette table.
- **R:** Accounts of when his epilepsy began and was diagnosed differ. One account places seizures in his student years (1838 to 1843), a generalized tonic-clonic seizure in 1844, and a diagnosis of epilepsy in 1849, shortly before he was taken to the prison at Omsk; another says the first symptoms appeared in early adulthood and the diagnosis came about a decade later; a third calls the onset controversial. The type of his epilepsy is disputed: it has been interpreted as temporolimbic epilepsy, as an idiopathic generalized epilepsy with minor temporal involvement, as a cryptogenic focal epilepsy of frontomedial or temporal origin, and, for the ecstatic onset he described, as insular in origin. Whether the ecstatic experience he described before some seizures, and gave to characters in his fiction, was an epileptic aura is disputed. One retrospective review links his gambling to his epilepsy: the stress of gambling, together with sleep deprivation, is described as aggravating his seizures.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
