History of Present Illness

Ms. Kahlo is seen at the request of Dr. Ortega for chronic post-traumatic pain. She describes constant low back and pelvic pain with burning in the right leg, a little less at night since she started duloxetine two weeks ago, and right ankle pain on standing that the diclofenac gel eases. She can stand at the easel for about 15 minutes before she has to sit, and she paints mostly seated or reclining. She knows a slow opioid taper is planned and asks that it stay slow. Her own goals are to stand longer at the easel and to keep teaching and painting every day. [S]

Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Magdalena Carmen Frida Kahlo Calderón was born on July 6, 1907, in her parents' house in Coyoacán, Mexico City, now known as the Blue House, the daughter of Guillermo Kahlo and Matilde Calderón. [D]
  • In 1913, at the age of six, Kahlo fell ill with polio; afterward her right leg was thinner, slightly shorter, and less developed, with the foot turned outward, and her father encouraged cycling and swimming. [D]
  • A neurologist's retrospective review proposes that Kahlo also had a congenital spinal anomaly (spina bifida). [R]
  • On September 17, 1925, at 18, Kahlo was riding a bus that was struck by a streetcar; a metal handrail impaled her through the hip and fractured her pelvis, and she had three fractures of the spinal column, a fractured collarbone, two rib fractures, a dislocated right shoulder, and eleven fractures of the right leg with a dislocated foot. [D]
  • While immobilized for several months after the accident, Kahlo began to paint, using an easel adapted for painting in bed. [D]
  • Kahlo lived with chronic pain for the rest of her life, underwent many operations, some with long convalescences and serious consequences, and used about 25 different corsets. [D]
  • In 1944, because of increasingly limited mobility, Kahlo had to stop attending her classes at the La Esmeralda school of painting and sculpture, and some students continued to work with her at the Blue House. [D]
  • Her pain has been interpreted retrospectively as including chronic neuropathic pain and central sensitization. [R]
  • Kahlo's father, Guillermo Kahlo, had epilepsy and died in 1941; her mother, Matilde Calderón, died in 1932. [D]

Assessment

  • Chronic post-traumatic low back and pelvic pain with right leg neuropathic features (G89.21). Mechanical pain from the old spinal and pelvic injuries and surgery, made worse by the uneven gait, with burning in the right leg that is neuropathic in character. There are no red flags and no new deficit, so imaging would not change management. Active rehabilitation is the main treatment, with duloxetine for the neuropathic component. [S]
  • Right leg weakness and shortening after childhood polio (B91). The shorter, weaker right leg tilts the pelvis and loads the back and the right ankle, and a shoe lift should ease both. Strengthening of the weak leg must be paced to avoid overuse fatigue. [S]

  • Long-term opioid therapy (Z79.891). Rehabilitation, duloxetine, and pain psychology give her other ways to control pain and support the slow, collaborative taper Dr. Ortega has planned. Opioid prescribing stays with Dr. Ortega alone. [S]

Plan of Treatment

  • Shoe lift for the shorter right leg, prescribed today, worn in the shoes she stands and walks in. [S]
  • Keep the back brace for bad days only, not all day, so the trunk muscles do not weaken; check the skin under it. [S]
  • Continue the cane, and the wheelchair for longer outings for now. No ankle brace is needed at present. [S]
  • Function goal, chosen by her: stand at the easel for 30 minutes at a time by the six-week review, alternating sitting and standing as she builds up. [S]
  • Continue duloxetine, increasing to 60 mg daily as Dr. Ortega plans, scheduled acetaminophen up to 3 g a day, and diclofenac gel for the ankle; no oral anti-inflammatory. [S]
  • Cognitive behavioral therapy for chronic pain, with pain neuroscience education in physical therapy. [S]
  • No spinal imaging, nerve conduction studies, or injections now; reconsider them for new weakness, a change in bladder or bowel control, or failure to progress with rehabilitation. [S]
  • Support a slow opioid taper of about 10 percent a month, starting at the four-week visit and paused if function worsens; judge progress by the PEG and easel time rather than by pain alone. [S]
