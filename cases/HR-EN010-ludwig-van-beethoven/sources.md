## Historical evidence

1. Beethoven-Haus Bonn. "Beethoven: a picture biography." Supports his birth in December 1770 in the house at Bonngasse no. 363; the baptismal register entry naming him as the first of two children baptized on December 17, 1770, with the birth "probably on the 16th or 17th"; his father's retirement in 1789 because of acute alcoholism; his move to Vienna in November 1792; the Mälzel ear trumpets of 1812–13; the conversation notebooks from about 1818; the Heiligenstadt Testament's request that his physician describe his illness; and his death on the afternoon of March 26, 1827 (dated through the caption of the deathbed lithograph of March 27), with some 20,000 people at the funeral. https://internet.beethoven.de/biography/picturebiography.html
2. Schindler A. *The Life of Beethoven, including his correspondence with his friends.* Edited by Ignace Moscheles. London, 1841. Project Gutenberg eBook 39093. English translations of his letters to Franz Gerhard Wegeler of June 29, 1801 (three years of weakening hearing; the stomach complaint said to cause it; Frank's tonics and oil of almonds; the tepid Danube bath; ears "ringing and singing night and day"; two years of shunning society because he could not say "I am deaf"; leaning close to the orchestra in the theater; not hearing high notes at a distance; hearing the sounds but not the words of soft speech; not bearing shouting; "I have often cursed my existence"; his request for secrecy) and of November 16, 1801 (the ringing less, "especially in my left ear, in which the disease began"; Vering's blisters of bark on both arms; mixing in society again). https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/39093
3. Beethoven-Haus Bonn. Work page for the Three Sonatas for Piano and Violin, op. 30. Supports the date of the Wegeler letter, June 29, 1801, the "continuous hissing" that made him avoid people, and his breakdown and the Heiligenstadt Testament in October 1802. https://www.beethoven.de/en/work/view/6017517575208960
4. Beethoven-Haus Bonn. "Mit Beethovens Ohr gehört" (exhibition text on his hearing). Supports the Testament's date, October 6, 1802, and its "six years"; the tinnitus, high-frequency loss, and intolerance of loud sound; the ear trumpets and the 138 surviving conversation notebooks in the Berlin State Library; his withdrawal from public performance as a pianist in 1814; near-deafness in the right ear by about 1814–15 and no longer hearing music from about 1817; his not hearing the applause at the first performance of the Ninth Symphony on May 7, 1824; and its description of the loss as sensorineural, with infection, otosclerosis, or loss of hair cells as possible causes. https://www.beethoven.de/en/g/mit-beethovens-ohr-gehoert
5. National Arts Centre, Canada. "Beethoven's complete Heiligenstadt Testament" (2012). English translation of the Testament with its date lines of October 6 and October 10, 1802; the translator is not named. Quoted briefly: "but little more and I would have put an end to my life"; "only art it was that withheld me." https://nac-cna.ca/en/stories/story/beethovens-complete-heiligenstadt-testament
6. Begg TJA, Schmidt A, Kocher A, et al. "Genomic analyses of hair from Ludwig van Beethoven." *Curr Biol* 2023;33(8):1431–1447.e22. PMID 36958333. Five of eight locks came from one European man and were deemed authentic; no genetic explanation for the hearing loss or gastrointestinal problems; a genetic predisposition to liver disease; hepatitis B infection in at least the months before death; with his "broadly accepted alcohol consumption," plausible explanations for the liver disease that culminated in his death. Graded **R**. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36958333/
7. Shearer PD. "The deafness of Beethoven: an audiologic and medical overview." *Am J Otol* 1990;11(5):370–374. PMID 2240185. Concludes, with the majority of otologists, that cochlear otosclerosis is the most likely diagnosis, and discusses Paget disease in the differential. Graded **R**. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2240185/
8. Stevens MH, Jacobsen T, Crofts AK. "Lead and the deafness of Ludwig van Beethoven." *Laryngoscope* 2013;123(11):2854–2858. PMID 23686526. Notes that no otosclerotic foci were found in the middle ears at autopsy, argues against autoimmune disease and syphilis, attributes the deafness to lead-tainted wine, and reports that his physicians thought he was dependent on alcohol. Graded **R**. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23686526/

## Editorial resolutions

- **Birth date and synthetic date of birth.** Only the baptism is recorded, on December 17, 1770; the Beethoven-Haus gives the birth as probably December 16 or 17. The preferred birth is recorded with month precision (December 1770), and December 16 as an unresolved alternative. Because the day is unknown, the rule to keep the true month and day applies only to the month. The synthetic date of birth uses December 16, the day before the baptism and the date the Beethoven-Haus names first, and is 1995-12-16. The shift is 225 years, so the true-date variant places his birth on December 16, 1770, which is a modeling choice, not a claim.
- **Index age.** The modern episode presents him at 30. His letter of June 29, 1801 dates the decline to about three years earlier, which puts the onset in his late twenties. The index visit is April 13, 2026 (true frame April 13, 1801) and the last chronic-care visit is October 19, 2026 (true frame October 19, 1801), so the episode spans the year of the two Wegeler letters. The Heiligenstadt Testament of October 1802 falls after the episode and appears only in the historical timeline and claims.
- **Modern diagnoses.** His progressive deafness and tinnitus are documented (**D**). The Beethoven-Haus describes the loss as sensorineural; the synthetic audiogram makes it a sloping sensorineural loss, worse on the left because his November 1801 letter says the disease began in the left ear. The adjustment disorder with depressed mood is synthetic, anchored to his documented withdrawal and despair, and makes no claim about his real mental health. No modern cause is assigned to the hearing loss.
- **Retrospective theories.** Otosclerosis and Paget disease (**R**, possible), lead poisoning (**R**, disputed), the 2023 genome study's hepatitis B and liver-disease findings (**R**, probable), and his drinking (**R**, possible) are recorded as claims only. The modern episode does not turn any into a diagnosis: its audiogram shows no conductive component, it does not test for lead because he reports no exposure, its hepatitis B panel shows immunity from vaccination and no infection, and its alcohol screen is below the risk threshold. The roster's statements that lead poisoning was "documented" and that alcoholic cirrhosis was the probable cause of death were not confirmed in the fetched sources and are not used.
- **Deafness and the Deaf community.** The record presents the change in his hearing as a change in how he communicates and works as well as a loss: communication preferences recorded at intake, written summaries, assistive listening and speech-to-text, the right to workplace accommodation on his terms, and information about the Deaf community and American Sign Language, which he takes up by his own choice. His later life, when he kept composing through deafness with ear trumpets and conversation books, is recorded in the timeline.
- **Safety assessment.** The adjustment disorder code requires a structured safety assessment. It records no current suicidal ideation. The Testament's words about ending his life are quoted from the translation as a sensitive historical claim; the modern record does not convert them into a current finding.
- **Not used.** His letter to Karl Amenda of 1801, the earlier hair-lock lead studies, the autopsy report itself, and the cause of death were not fetched or confirmed and are not recorded. The Gutenberg translation gives the doctor's name as "Frank"; the full name Johann Peter Frank is given on the Beethoven-Haus page cited in claim CLM-0004. A synthetic long-standing intermittent bowel complaint without alarm features, noted at the preventive visit, echoes his documented stomach complaint and is not diagnosed.
- **Sick and preventive visits.** A periodic adult preventive visit (age 30) on August 24, 2026 (true frame August 24, 1801) and a same-day sick visit on September 21, 2026 (true frame September 21, 1801) were added. The sick visit is a left cerumen impaction in a hearing aid wearer; it was chosen because it interacts with his hearing aids and teaches the distinction between a conductive and a sudden sensorineural loss, and it does not echo any documented illness.
- **Focused visits.** The four chronic-care follow-ups (two weeks, five weeks, three months, and six months) are documented as focused visits on the AAO-HNS tinnitus, age-related hearing loss, and sudden hearing loss guidelines and the CMID depression monograph. The audiograms at two weeks and six months, the normal MRI, and the hearing aid fitting are synthetic. The Tinnitus Handicap Inventory and the word recognition scores are recorded in the notes only, because no LOINC code was found for them. Two protocol deviations are recorded: the Mood Disorder Questionnaire was given at the second visit instead of at intake, and no PHQ-9 was taken in the six weeks between the three-month visit and the preventive visit, with no harm and a corrective action.
- **Consultation.** The otolaryngology consultation by the fictional Dr. Albrecht on the second visit is synthetic. Its recommendations (MRI, hearing aids with a music program, musicians' earplugs, cognitive behavioral therapy and no tinnitus medication, a repeat audiogram at six months, and same-week assessment of sudden loss) are those the later visits carry out.
- Beethoven died in 1827, so the recent-death review rule does not apply. Ordinary clinical, provenance, and safe-messaging reviews remain required.
