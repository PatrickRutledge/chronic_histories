## Assessment

1. **Bilateral sensorineural hearing loss, asymmetric** (H90.3). A cochlear-pattern sensorineural loss sloping into the high frequencies, mild on the right and moderate on the left. There is no conductive component, which makes otosclerosis of the middle ear unlikely. The left ear is 15 dB worse at four adjacent frequencies, with a 24-point gap in word recognition. That asymmetry needs MRI of the internal auditory canals with gadolinium to exclude a vestibular schwannoma or other retrocochlear lesion. Blood tests for treatable causes are normal. Many progressive losses at his age have no cause that can be found or reversed; noise has probably added to it. **S**
2. **Persistent, bothersome tinnitus with reduced loudness tolerance** (H93.13). Present for more than six months, bothersome, and tied to the hearing loss; nonpulsatile and without neurologic signs, so it needs no separate imaging. Hearing aids, sound therapy, and cognitive behavioral therapy have the best evidence. Medicines, supplements, and magnetic stimulation do not, and are not recommended. Reduced loudness tolerance is common with cochlear loss and usually eases with sound therapy and well-fitted aids. **S**
3. **Hearing rehabilitation for a musician**. A good candidate for bilateral prescription hearing aids. A professional musician needs more than over-the-counter devices offer: real-ear verification, a music program with a wider input range and less compression, feedback control that does not distort music, and a remote microphone for teaching and meetings. Assistive listening, captioning, and speech-to-text will help where aids are not enough. Many musicians with hearing loss keep working, and some draw on vibration, visual cues, and the Deaf community as well as on hearing. **S**

## Recommendations

1. MRI of the internal auditory canals with gadolinium.
2. Bilateral prescription hearing aids with real-ear verification, a music program, tinnitus sound generators, and a remote microphone; audiology fine-tuning two to four weeks after fitting.
3. Custom musicians' earplugs with flat attenuation for loud rehearsals and performances.
4. Tinnitus education, sound enrichment at night, and cognitive behavioral therapy; no medicines or supplements for tinnitus.
5. Repeat audiogram at six months while the loss is progressing, then yearly and with any change.
6. Same-week evaluation for any sudden drop in hearing, because sudden sensorineural loss is treated within days; check first for a blocked canal.
7. Communication access he chooses, including the right to ask for accommodations at work, and information on hearing loss peer groups and Deaf community resources.
