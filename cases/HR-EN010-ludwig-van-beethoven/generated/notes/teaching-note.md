# Teaching Note — Ludwig van Beethoven pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Ludwig van Beethoven was born in December 1770 in the house at Bonngasse in Bonn. Only his baptism is recorded: he is entered in the register of the parish church of St. Remigius as the first of two children baptized on December 17, 1770. The day of his birth is not recorded; the Beethoven-Haus gives it as probably the 16th or 17th. In November 1792 Beethoven left Bonn for Vienna to study with Haydn; he intended to stay a year and lived there for over 33 years. His father's acute alcoholism forced him to retire in 1789. In a letter to his friend the physician Franz Gerhard Wegeler dated June 29, 1801, Beethoven wrote that his hearing had become weaker and weaker for the last three years; that a stomach complaint was said to be its cause; that Johann Peter Frank had treated him with tonics and his hearing with oil of almonds, and a tepid Danube bath had helped his general health but not his hearing; that his ears were "ringing and singing night and day"; that for two years he had shunned all society because he could not say to people "I am deaf"; that in the theater he had to lean close to the orchestra to understand the actors and could not hear the high notes of instruments or singers at a distance; that he heard the sounds but not the words of people speaking softly, yet could not bear shouting; and he asked Wegeler to tell no one. In the same letter he wrote, "I have often cursed my existence; Plutarch has won me back to resignation," and that he would defy his fate if he could. In a second letter to Wegeler, dated November 16, 1801, he wrote that the ringing in his ears was somewhat less, "especially in my left ear, in which the disease began," but that his hearing was no better; that his physician, Dr. Vering, had applied blistering bark to both his arms; and that he had begun to mix in society again after two deserted and miserable years. At Heiligenstadt on October 6, 1802, with an addition dated October 10, he wrote the document known as the Heiligenstadt Testament to his brothers Carl and Johann. In it he wrote that he had been afflicted for six years, that he had been compelled early to isolate himself, that others beside him heard a distant flute or a shepherd singing when he heard nothing, that "but little more and I would have put an end to my life," and that "only art it was that withheld me"; he asked that after his death his physician describe his illness. Johann Nepomuk Maelzel made ear trumpets for him from about 1812, and from about 1818 he communicated mainly through conversation books in which others wrote; 138 of the books survive in the Berlin State Library. He withdrew from public performance as a pianist in 1814; by about 1814 or 1815 he was as good as deaf in his right ear, and from about 1817 he could no longer hear music. He kept composing. At the first performance of his Ninth Symphony on May 7, 1824, he could not hear the applause and had to be turned around to see it. Beethoven died in Vienna on the afternoon of March 26, 1827, aged 56; some 20,000 people gathered for his funeral.
- **R:** Cochlear otosclerosis has been the cause most often proposed by otologists, with Paget disease of bone among the alternatives; a later review notes that the middle ears examined at his autopsy showed no otosclerotic foci. Some authors attribute his hearing loss and abdominal complaints to chronic lead poisoning, citing high lead levels in hair and bone, and in 2024 high lead levels were reported in two authenticated locks of his hair; others favor other causes, and the question is not settled. A 2023 genome study of authenticated locks of his hair found no genetic explanation for his hearing loss or gastrointestinal problems, but found a genetic predisposition to liver disease and evidence of hepatitis B infection in at least the months before his death; with his alcohol consumption, the authors offered these as plausible explanations for the severe liver disease that culminated in his death. His alcohol consumption is described by modern authors as broadly accepted, and his physicians are said to have thought he was dependent on alcohol.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
