## Historical evidence

1. Cavell J. “Franklin, Sir John.” *Dictionary of Canadian Biography*, vol. 7 (University of Toronto and Université Laval). Born April 16, 1786, in Spilsby “over his father's shop,” the ninth child and youngest son in a family of twelve; entered the navy in October 1800; Copenhagen and Trafalgar, with “a mild degree of hearing loss from the constant noise of the cannons and guns”; slightly wounded at Lake Borgne on December 14, 1814; the 1819 to 1822 expedition, when the men lived partly on “lichen and maggots” and 11 of the 20 died; the 1825 to 1827 expedition; knighted April 29, 1829; marriage to Eleanor Anne Porden in 1823, their daughter in 1824, and her death on February 22, 1825, after doctors “failed for many months” to recognize tuberculosis; marriage to Jane Griffin in 1828; arrival in Van Diemen's Land on January 6, 1837, recall in a letter that “harshly censured his conduct,” and return to England in June 1844; “Although he was almost 60”; “129 officers and men, sailed on 19 May 1845”; death on June 11, 1847; “Neither the cause nor the exact place … was given in the document signed on 25 April 1848”; cannibalism in the retreat. http://www.biographi.ca/en/bio/franklin_john_7E.html
2. Parks Canada. “Who's who in the Franklin expedition” (Wrecks of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror National Historic Site). ## Editorial resolutions

- **Sources.** The case relies on the *Dictionary of Canadian Biography*, two Parks Canada pages, the MysteryQuest educational resource and the Victory Point transcription of the Great Unsolved Mysteries in Canadian History project, the Royal Museums Greenwich catalogue, the Linda Hall Library, Bayliss (2002) in full text, Swanston et al. (2018) in open text, the PubMed records of Amy et al. (1986), Horowitz (2003) and Byard (2021), and the University of Glasgow release on Millar et al. (2015). The Royal Museums Greenwich image of the Victory Point record and the *Australian Dictionary of Biography* entry refused access; no Scott Polar Research Institute or National Archives (UK) page was read; the book by Beattie and Geiger was not read, and their forensic work is cited through Amy et al. (1986), on which Beattie is an author, and through Swanston et al. and Bayliss.
- **Name.** The display name is “Sir John Franklin”; the alias “John Franklin” appears in no other record. “Franklin” alone is not an alias, because it is part of other records' names; the name of the other record that shares it is never written. No other polar explorer with a record is named. Crewmen of the 1845 expedition are not named.
- **Thin chart (batch 13, rule 26).** His documented health history is thin: a mild hearing loss from gunfire, a slight wound in 1814, prolonged starvation in 1821, officials' doubts about his age, “portly” build and tolerance of cold, and a severe influenza in the winter before sailing. Nothing else about his health before 1845 was found, and no weight, blood pressure or medical examination from his lifetime is recorded. The modern episode is built around a common finding at a pre-expedition medical (stage 2 hypertension and class 1 obesity) and a preventive, risk-focused visit pattern that fits the documented life. The hypertension, obesity and vitamin D insufficiency are synthetic; the obesity is anchored only to the word “portly” in the officials' doubts.
- **Index age and dates.** The index visit on January 13, 2026 maps to January 13, 1845, when he was 58 and in London as the Admiralty chose a commander; the synthetic date of birth is April 16, 1967 (shift 181 years). The visits on January 27, February 10 and 24, March 10, April 7 and May 5, 2026 map to the same days of 1845, all before the ships sailed on May 19, 1845. He turned 59 on April 16, so the last two visits fall at 59; the preventive visit is at 58. The second rabies dose (March 17) is a nurse appointment, not an encounter.
- **Influenza (CLM-0009, D, probable).** The source says he had a severe influenza in the winter of 1844 to 1845 and was still suffering from it after sailing; it does not say when. The modern sick visit places the illness in February and gives it a synthetic test, treatment (oseltamivir) and course; the lingering cough echoes the source. The timeline event is dated February 1845 so that the intake documents do not show it.
- **Officials' doubts (CLM-0008, D, probable).** Only one source read (an educational resource of a university history project) reports the doubts about his age, build and cold tolerance, so the claim is graded probable. The word “portly” is kept only as a quotation; the modern record describes weight neutrally and lets him set his own goals.
- **Death (CLM-0011, D; CLM-0012, R).** The date of June 11, 1847 is from the note of April 25, 1848 on the Victory Point record. The record gives no cause or place; Bayliss's “died suddenly on board” is recorded as a retrospective statement (R, possible). No cause of death is used in the modern episode.
- **The crew (CLM-0010, CLM-0013 to CLM-0016).** The deaths of the crew, the lead found in their remains, tuberculosis and pneumonia in the men buried on Beechey Island, the tin-solder hypothesis, its challenge by Millar et al. (2015) and Swanston et al. (2018), and the scurvy, botulism and zinc hypotheses are history about others. They are recorded once each as context for the modern food-safety and vitamin plan and are not attributed to him. The party size on the Coppermine journey is 20 in the *Dictionary of Canadian Biography* and 19 at Parks Canada; the claim says eleven died. The 1845 complement is 129 at sailing (Bayliss, DCB); Swanston gives 128 entering Lancaster Sound.
- **Roster leads not used (CLM-0017, R, disputed).** The roster's “lead poisoning (probable),” “scurvy” and “probable lead encephalopathy affecting decision-making” are not his documented conditions: no examination of his body exists and the role of lead in the crew is disputed. The roster's “cannibalism … confirmed by bone analysis” is recorded only as the plain statement that cannibalism occurred in the retreat, from the *Dictionary of Canadian Biography* and Byard.
- **Relatives.** His first wife's death from tuberculosis is documented and is used only as a past household contact, to justify a tuberculosis blood test; his second wife and daughter appear only as documented history and as “married; one adult daughter.” The advance care planning agent is unnamed. No relative has any statement, decision or health fact in the modern episode. No family medical history is recorded, because none was found.

(2) Amlodipine chosen over a thiazide or a renin-angiotensin blocker because it needs no laboratory monitoring and avoids low sodium, low potassium and kidney injury with the dehydration and cold diuresis of polar work; kept at 5 mg because he reached goal by eight weeks. (3) Statin discussed at a PREVENT risk of about 6 percent and Pooled Cohort risk of about 8 percent (the USPSTF selective range) and declined for now. (4) Echocardiogram for stage 2 hypertension (CMID) and an exercise treadmill test before clearance for strenuous work beyond rescue; the test was postponed after influenza. (5) No weight-loss medicine before departure; weight goals set by him. (6) Vitamin D3 25 micrograms daily for midwinter insufficiency and polar darkness; vitamin C 250 mg daily from the pre-departure visit. (7) Rabies pre-exposure prophylaxis (two doses a week apart) for Arctic wildlife and no access to post-exposure care; hepatitis B vaccine under the universal adult recommendation. (8) A fecal immunochemical test instead of colonoscopy because colonoscopy could not be fitted in before sailing. (9) Written dose rules for the ship's surgeons, and his own written wishes for serious illness on board.
