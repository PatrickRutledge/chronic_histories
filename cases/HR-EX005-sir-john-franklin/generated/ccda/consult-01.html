  • John Franklin was born on April 16, 1786, in the market town of Spilsby, Lincolnshire, England, over his father's shop; his father was a dry-goods and cloth merchant, and he was the ninth child and youngest son in a family of twelve. [D]
  • He entered the Royal Navy at 14 in 1800 and fought at the battles of Copenhagen (1801) and Trafalgar (1805); the constant noise of the guns left him with a mild degree of hearing loss. [D]
  • He was slightly wounded in the battle of Lake Borgne near New Orleans on December 14, 1814. [D]
  • On his first overland Arctic expedition, from 1819 to 1822, which charted the coast east of the mouth of the Coppermine River, supplies ran out: in the autumn of 1821 the men lived on lichen, deer skins, bones, maggots and boiled boot leather, and eleven of the party died of starvation, exposure and violence before Dene hunters brought food. He became known as the man who ate his boots. [D]
  • He led a second overland expedition from 1825 to 1827 and was knighted on April 29, 1829. [D]
  • He married Eleanor Anne Porden in 1823; their daughter was born in 1824, and Eleanor died on February 22, 1825, of an illness her doctors were slow to recognize as tuberculosis. In 1828 he married Jane Griffin; they had no children. [D]
  • He was Lieutenant-Governor of Van Diemen's Land (now Tasmania) from January 1837; after a dispute with his colonial secretary he was recalled by a letter that censured his conduct, and he was back in England by June 1844. [D]
  • He sought the command of the 1845 Northwest Passage expedition at 58 to 59. Several senior officials privately questioned his age, his portly build and his alleged intolerance of cold; he answered their doubts and was given the command. [D]

Assessment

  • Fitness for the command (Z71.84). No barrier found so far. Age alone does not exclude him; what matters is his heart, his blood pressure and his capacity for hard work in the cold. Because he will be beyond rescue, the bar for testing is lower than for ordinary life: an echocardiogram for stage 2 hypertension and a symptom-limited exercise treadmill test, done once he is free of any acute illness, are advised before final clearance. Weight is one factor among many and is his to set goals for; it is not a reason to refuse him the command. [S]
  • Hypertension (I10). Stage 2 hypertension confirmed at home, with no target-organ damage so far; a urine albumin is still needed. Treatment with medicine is indicated, with a goal below 130/80. Amlodipine is the best fit for his circumstances: it needs no blood tests, keeps for years in blister packs, and does not cause the low sodium, low potassium or kidney injury that a diuretic or a renin-angiotensin blocker can cause with the dehydration and cold diuresis of polar work. Ankle swelling is its main side effect. A home monitor with a spare cuff and written dose rules for the ship's surgeons should go with him. [S]
  • Nutrition, vitamins and food safety (E55.9). Vitamin C stores last only weeks to a few months without intake, and the vitamin C in stored juice and food loses strength over time, so a daily vitamin C tablet stored sealed, cool and dark is advised for the whole expedition, with the stores checked and scurvy signs taught. His vitamin D is insufficient in midwinter and polar darkness will lower it further: vitamin D3 daily is advised. Rations should be energy-dense, because work in the cold can need far more calories than at home. Lead-soldered food cans have long been abandoned in the United States, but imported or old cans should be checked; his baseline blood lead is normal. Botulism is rare but cannot be treated at sea: bulging, leaking, dented or spurting cans should be discarded unopened, no home-canned food carried, and the early signs (double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, descending weakness) taught to the surgeons. [S]
  • Cold injury and hypothermia (Z71.84). Prevention is the main tool: layered clothing with vapor control, dry socks and mitts, enough food and fluid, no alcohol or tobacco before exposure, and buddy checks of faces, hands and feet. Frostbitten tissue should be rewarmed in warm water only when it can be kept from refreezing, without rubbing; hypothermia should be recognized early, with insulation, shelter and gentle handling of the cold, confused or stiff man. Amlodipine does not raise the risk of cold injury. [S]
  • Vaccinations, teeth, mind and evacuation (Z71.84). Bring tetanus up to date and give the vaccines due at his age (PCV20, recombinant zoster, hepatitis B), and give rabies pre-exposure prophylaxis, because Arctic foxes carry rabies and rabies immune globulin will be out of reach. A dental examination before departure is essential: dental pain is among the commonest emergencies on long expeditions. Baseline mood and anxiety are normal; long isolation and months of darkness call for a daily routine of work, light, exercise and sleep, peer support among the officers, and watchfulness for low mood. There is no evacuation for months at a time, so the plan must be a kit, written instructions and his own written wishes for serious illness on board. [S]
