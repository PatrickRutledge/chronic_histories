## Assessment

1. **Fitness for the command** (Z71.84). No barrier found so far. Age alone does not exclude him; what matters is his heart, his blood pressure and his capacity for hard work in the cold. Because he will be beyond rescue, the bar for testing is lower than for ordinary life: an echocardiogram for stage 2 hypertension and a symptom-limited exercise treadmill test, done once he is free of any acute illness, are advised before final clearance. Weight is one factor among many and is his to set goals for; it is not a reason to refuse him the command. **S**
2. **Hypertension** (I10). Stage 2 hypertension confirmed at home, with no target-organ damage so far; a urine albumin is still needed. Treatment with medicine is indicated, with a goal below 130/80. Amlodipine is the best fit for his circumstances: it needs no blood tests, keeps for years in blister packs, and does not cause the low sodium, low potassium or kidney injury that a diuretic or a renin-angiotensin blocker can cause with the dehydration and cold diuresis of polar work. Ankle swelling is its main side effect. A home monitor with a spare cuff and written dose rules for the ship's surgeons should go with him. **S**
3. **Nutrition, vitamins and food safety** (E55.9). Vitamin C stores last only weeks to a few months without intake, and the vitamin C in stored juice and food loses strength over time, so a daily vitamin C tablet stored sealed, cool and dark is advised for the whole expedition, with the stores checked and scurvy signs taught. His vitamin D is insufficient in midwinter and polar darkness will lower it further: vitamin D3 daily is advised. Rations should be energy-dense, because work in the cold can need far more calories than at home. Lead-soldered food cans have long been abandoned in the United States, but imported or old cans should be checked; his baseline blood lead is normal. Botulism is rare but cannot be treated at sea: bulging, leaking, dented or spurting cans should be discarded unopened, no home-canned food carried, and the early signs (double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, descending weakness) taught to the surgeons. **S**
4. **Cold injury and hypothermia** (Z71.84). Prevention is the main tool: layered clothing with vapor control, dry socks and mitts, enough food and fluid, no alcohol or tobacco before exposure, and buddy checks of faces, hands and feet. Frostbitten tissue should be rewarmed in warm water only when it can be kept from refreezing, without rubbing; hypothermia should be recognized early, with insulation, shelter and gentle handling of the cold, confused or stiff man. Amlodipine does not raise the risk of cold injury. **S**
5. **Vaccinations, teeth, mind and evacuation** (Z71.84). Bring tetanus up to date and give the vaccines due at his age (PCV20, recombinant zoster, hepatitis B), and give rabies pre-exposure prophylaxis, because Arctic foxes carry rabies and rabies immune globulin will be out of reach. A dental examination before departure is essential: dental pain is among the commonest emergencies on long expeditions. Baseline mood and anxiety are normal; long isolation and months of darkness call for a daily routine of work, light, exercise and sleep, peer support among the officers, and watchfulness for low mood. There is no evacuation for months at a time, so the plan must be a kit, written instructions and his own written wishes for serious illness on board. **S**

## Recommendations

1. Echocardiogram now, and a symptom-limited exercise treadmill test before final clearance, once he is free of any acute illness.
2. Amlodipine 5 mg daily now, aiming for home readings below 130/80; raise to 10 mg if not at goal after weight change settles; a three-year supply, a home monitor with a spare cuff, and written dose rules for the ship's surgeons.
3. Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio and a repeat basic metabolic panel before departure as a baseline.
4. Vitamin D3 25 micrograms daily now and through the expedition, rechecked before departure; vitamin C 250 mg daily from the pre-departure visit and through the expedition, stored sealed, cool and dark.
5. Energy-dense rations; canned food without lead solder, with damaged or bulging cans discarded unopened; no home-canned food; botulism signs taught.
6. Cold-weather clothing fitted and cold-injury and hypothermia training before departure.
7. Tdap, PCV20, recombinant zoster, hepatitis B and rabies pre-exposure prophylaxis, and a dental examination before departure.
8. A routine for the polar night, peer support, baseline PHQ-9 and GAD-7, a medical kit, and his written wishes for serious illness on board.
