# Teaching Note — Sir John Franklin pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** John Franklin was born on April 16, 1786, in the market town of Spilsby, Lincolnshire, England, over his father's shop; his father was a dry-goods and cloth merchant, and he was the ninth child and youngest son in a family of twelve. He entered the Royal Navy at 14 in 1800 and fought at the battles of Copenhagen (1801) and Trafalgar (1805); the constant noise of the guns left him with a mild degree of hearing loss. He was slightly wounded in the battle of Lake Borgne near New Orleans on December 14, 1814. On his first overland Arctic expedition, from 1819 to 1822, which charted the coast east of the mouth of the Coppermine River, supplies ran out: in the autumn of 1821 the men lived on lichen, deer skins, bones, maggots and boiled boot leather, and eleven of the party died of starvation, exposure and violence before Dene hunters brought food. He became known as the man who ate his boots. He led a second overland expedition from 1825 to 1827 and was knighted on April 29, 1829. He married Eleanor Anne Porden in 1823; their daughter was born in 1824, and Eleanor died on February 22, 1825, of an illness her doctors were slow to recognize as tuberculosis. In 1828 he married Jane Griffin; they had no children. He was Lieutenant-Governor of Van Diemen's Land (now Tasmania) from January 1837; after a dispute with his colonial secretary he was recalled by a letter that censured his conduct, and he was back in England by June 1844. He sought the command of the 1845 Northwest Passage expedition at 58 to 59. Several senior officials privately questioned his age, his portly build and his alleged intolerance of cold; he answered their doubts and was given the command. During the winter of 1844 to 1845 he had a severe bout of influenza, from which he acknowledged he was still suffering after the expedition sailed in May 1845. HMS Erebus, under his command, and HMS Terror sailed from the Thames on May 19, 1845, with 129 officers and men. The first winter was spent at Beechey Island, where three crewmen died and were buried; in September 1846 the ships were beset in the ice off the northwest coast of King William Island. He died on June 11, 1847, during the expedition, near King William Island. The date comes from a note added on April 25, 1848, to the record left in a cairn near Victory Point and found in 1859; it gives neither the cause nor the place of his death. In April 1848, with the crew reduced to 105, the ships were abandoned and the men set out south on foot; none survived, and some men resorted to cannibalism during the retreat. The wrecks of HMS Erebus and HMS Terror were found in 2014 and 2016, when Inuit traditional knowledge combined with modern technology led to the sites; Inuit along the expedition's route were the last people to see the men alive.
- **R:** The cause of his death is not known. A medical historian writes that he died suddenly on board Erebus; no examination was made and his grave has never been found. Examinations of crewmen's remains found elevated lead in bone, hair and soft tissue, and tuberculosis and pneumonia in men buried on Beechey Island; lead from the solder of the expedition's tinned food was long proposed as a cause of the disaster. Later analyses challenged the tinned-food lead hypothesis: a statistical re-analysis found great variation in lead among the crew, with levels perhaps not exceptional for nineteenth-century Britain, and a synchrotron study of bone found no unusually high lead exposure compared with Royal Navy sailors buried in Antigua. Scurvy, botulism, zinc deficiency, starvation, hypothermia and infection have all been proposed, and the deaths were most likely due to many causes together. The roster's leads of lead encephalopathy that impaired decision-making, and of lead poisoning and scurvy as his own conditions, are not supported for him by the sources read: no examination of his body exists, and the role of lead in the crew is disputed.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
