Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Sources. The case relies on the Dictionary of Canadian Biography, two Parks Canada pages, the MysteryQuest educational resource and the Victory Point transcription of the Great Unsolved Mysteries in Canadian History project, the Royal Museums Greenwich catalogue, the Linda Hall Library, Bayliss (2002) in full text, Swanston et al. (2018) in open text, the PubMed records of Amy et al. (1986), Horowitz (2003) and Byard (2021), and the University of Glasgow release on Millar et al. (2015). The Royal Museums Greenwich image of the Victory Point record and the Australian Dictionary of Biography entry refused access; no Scott Polar Research Institute or National Archives (UK) page was read; the book by Beattie and Geiger was not read, and their forensic work is cited through Amy et al. (1986), on which Beattie is an author, and through Swanston et al. and Bayliss.
  2. Name. The display name is “Sir John Franklin”; the alias “John Franklin” appears in no other record. “Franklin” alone is not an alias, because it is part of other records' names; the name of the other record that shares it is never written. No other polar explorer with a record is named. Crewmen of the 1845 expedition are not named.
  3. Thin chart (batch 13, rule 26). His documented health history is thin: a mild hearing loss from gunfire, a slight wound in 1814, prolonged starvation in 1821, officials' doubts about his age, “portly” build and tolerance of cold, and a severe influenza in the winter before sailing. Nothing else about his health before 1845 was found, and no weight, blood pressure or medical examination from his lifetime is recorded. The modern episode is built around a common finding at a pre-expedition medical (stage 2 hypertension and class 1 obesity) and a preventive, risk-focused visit pattern that fits the documented life. The hypertension, obesity and vitamin D insufficiency are synthetic; the obesity is anchored only to the word “portly” in the officials' doubts.
  4. Index age and dates. The index visit on January 13, 2026 maps to January 13, 1845, when he was 58 and in London as the Admiralty chose a commander; the synthetic date of birth is April 16, 1967 (shift 181 years). The visits on January 27, February 10 and 24, March 10, April 7 and May 5, 2026 map to the same days of 1845, all before the ships sailed on May 19, 1845. He turned 59 on April 16, so the last two visits fall at 59; the preventive visit is at 58. The second rabies dose (March 17) is a nurse appointment, not an encounter.
  5. Influenza (CLM-0009, D, probable). The source says he had a severe influenza in the winter of 1844 to 1845 and was still suffering from it after sailing; it does not say when. The modern sick visit places the illness in February and gives it a synthetic test, treatment (oseltamivir) and course; the lingering cough echoes the source. The timeline event is dated February 1845 so that the intake documents do not show it.
  6. Officials' doubts (CLM-0008, D, probable). Only one source read (an educational resource of a university history project) reports the doubts about his age, build and cold tolerance, so the claim is graded probable. The word “portly” is kept only as a quotation; the modern record describes weight neutrally and lets him set his own goals.
  7. Death (CLM-0011, D; CLM-0012, R). The date of June 11, 1847 is from the note of April 25, 1848 on the Victory Point record. The record gives no cause or place; Bayliss's “died suddenly on board” is recorded as a retrospective statement (R, possible). No cause of death is used in the modern episode.
  8. The crew (CLM-0010, CLM-0013 to CLM-0016). The deaths of the crew, the lead found in their remains, tuberculosis and pneumonia in the men buried on Beechey Island, the tin-solder hypothesis, its challenge by Millar et al. (2015) and Swanston et al. (2018), and the scurvy, botulism and zinc hypotheses are history about others. They are recorded once each as context for the modern food-safety and vitamin plan and are not attributed to him. The party size on the Coppermine journey is 20 in the Dictionary of Canadian Biography and 19 at Parks Canada; the claim says eleven died. The 1845 complement is 129 at sailing (Bayliss, DCB); Swanston gives 128 entering Lancaster Sound.
  9. Roster leads not used (CLM-0017, R, disputed). The roster's “lead poisoning (probable),” “scurvy” and “probable lead encephalopathy affecting decision-making” are not his documented conditions: no examination of his body exists and the role of lead in the crew is disputed. The roster's “cannibalism … confirmed by bone analysis” is recorded only as the plain statement that cannibalism occurred in the retreat, from the Dictionary of Canadian Biography and Byard.
  10. Relatives. His first wife's death from tuberculosis is documented and is used only as a past household contact, to justify a tuberculosis blood test; his second wife and daughter appear only as documented history and as “married; one adult daughter.” The advance care planning agent is unnamed. No relative has any statement, decision or health fact in the modern episode. No family medical history is recorded, because none was found.
  11. Clinical judgment calls. (1) Stage 2 hypertension confirmed by a week of home readings (average 144/90) and treated with medicine at once under the 2025 guideline. (2) Amlodipine chosen over a thiazide or a renin-angiotensin blocker because it needs no laboratory monitoring and avoids low sodium, low potassium and kidney injury with the dehydration and cold diuresis of polar work; kept at 5 mg because he reached goal by eight weeks. (3) Statin discussed at a PREVENT risk of about 6 percent and Pooled Cohort risk of about 8 percent (the USPSTF selective range) and declined for now. (4) Echocardiogram for stage 2 hypertension (CMID) and an exercise treadmill test before clearance for strenuous work beyond rescue; the test was postponed after influenza. (5) No weight-loss medicine before departure; weight goals set by him. (6) Vitamin D3 25 micrograms daily for midwinter insufficiency and polar darkness; vitamin C 250 mg daily from the pre-departure visit. (7) Rabies pre-exposure prophylaxis (two doses a week apart) for Arctic wildlife and no access to post-exposure care; hepatitis B vaccine under the universal adult recommendation. (8) A fecal immunochemical test instead of colonoscopy because colonoscopy could not be fitted in before sailing. (9) Written dose rules for the ship's surgeons, and his own written wishes for serious illness on board.
  12. Synthetic clinical picture. Everything in the modern episode is synthetic: every result, the ECG, audiogram, echocardiogram and exercise test, the tuberculosis, fecal, HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C results (recorded as narrative), the examination findings, the scores (PHQ-9, PHQ-2, GAD-7, AUDIT-C), the medicines and the outcomes. Height (170 cm, for a man described as short and stocky) and weights are synthetic.
  13. Preventive visit. A periodic adult preventive visit at 58 (CPT 99396 with 99497 in plain text), nine weeks before sailing. Judgment calls: Tdap, PCV20, recombinant zoster and hepatitis B vaccines and rabies pre-exposure prophylaxis; PSA declined after shared decision-making; one-time HIV, hepatitis C and hepatitis B screening; a dental examination by his own dentist the week before, recorded as up to date; advance care planning that addresses serious illness beyond the reach of evacuation.
  14. Sick visit. Influenza A confirmed by a rapid molecular test, treated with oseltamivir, chosen because it is anchored to his documented influenza of that winter and tests the plan: decongestants and anti-inflammatory painkillers raise blood pressure, amlodipine need not be held, and an exercise test during recovery would be unsafe and misleading, so it was postponed.
  15. Focused visits. The four chronic-care follow-ups (two weeks with the consultation, four weeks, eight weeks with the exercise test, and the pre-departure visit) are documented as focused visits on the CMID hypertension monograph and the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline, with the USPSTF obesity and statin recommendations, the Wilderness Medical Society frostbite and hypothermia guidelines, the CDC botulism guideline and the adult lead management recommendations for the expedition plan. One deviation is recorded instead of an invented result: the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio due at diagnosis was not collected at intake or at two weeks; no harm was likely with normal kidney function and fundi, it was done at four weeks (12 mg/g, normal), and the corrective action was a urine cup and a standing order. Synthetic findings introduced: mild concentric remodeling on the echocardiogram and a post-influenza cough that resolved.
  16. Consultation. The wilderness and expedition medicine referral and the consultation by Magnus Okafor-Lindqvist, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the two-week visit, are S. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: no barrier to the command, with an echocardiogram and an exercise test before clearance; amlodipine with home monitoring and written dose rules; vitamin D3 now and vitamin C from the pre-departure visit, energy-dense rations and rules for cans, lead and botulism; cold-injury and hypothermia prevention; the vaccines, dental examination, routine for the polar night, kit and written wishes that followed.
