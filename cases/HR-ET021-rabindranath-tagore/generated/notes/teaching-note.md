# Teaching Note — Rabindranath Tagore pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Rabindranath Tagore was born on 7 May 1861 in the Jorasanko mansion in Calcutta, the youngest son of Debendranath Tagore, a leader of the Brahmo Samaj; the name Tagore is the anglicised transliteration of Thakur. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1913 "because of his profoundly sensitive, fresh and beautiful verse, by which, with consummate skill, he has made his poetic thought, expressed in his own English words, a part of the literature of the West". He started a school at Santiniketan that began formally on 22 December 1901 with five students, and around it he built Visva-Bharati, an unconventional university. He was known by the sobriquets Gurudeb, Kobiguru, and Bishwokobi. His last five years were marked by chronic pain and two long periods of illness: in late 1937 he lost consciousness and remained comatose and near death for a time, and in late 1940 a similar spell followed, from which he never recovered. On 15 September 1940, while visiting Kalimpong, he fell seriously ill with severe pain in his lower abdomen; doctors diagnosed a severe urinary tract infection, and he was brought back, unconscious, to Calcutta. In the last two years of his life illness confined him to the house, with swollen feet, numbness of the fingers, and frequent fevers; women of his family and household nursed him at home. He sought cure in allopathy, homeopathy, and ayurvedic medicine; physicians including Bidhan Chandra Roy and Nilratan Sarkar supervised his treatment; he is reported to have refused surgery despite repeated insistence from his doctors, to have sought a herbal cure, and to have said that he was more afraid of complicated medical treatment than of death. He was very anxious about the operation and asked several times whether it would hurt; a few days before it he asked for a prayer-poem asking not for rescue from danger but for courage in it. He was operated on at Jorasanko, on a veranda outside his room made into an operating theatre, on 30 July 1941; one reference work instead says he dictated a poem on 30 July, a day before a scheduled operation. He died on 7 August 1941, aged 80, at the Jorasanko house, after a prolonged period of suffering. He suffered the untimely deaths of his wife and three of his children; he took his daughter Renuka to hill stations in the Himalayas for her recovery from tuberculosis.
- **R:** Doctors reviewing his records for a 2016 exhibition said he had an enlarged prostate that was stopping his urine flow. Accounts of the operation differ: one says the doctors could not operate on the enlarged prostate and instead performed a bypass to drain the accumulated urine and relieve pain; another says the operation was performed and was successful. Present-day doctors believe that infection after the operation led to septicaemia and his death. A university vice-chancellor said in 2018 that he was suffering from cancer in his prostate; other accounts describe only an enlarged prostate.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
