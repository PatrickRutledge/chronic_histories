# Teaching Note — Lorraine Hansberry pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Lorraine Vivian Hansberry was born in Chicago on May 19, 1930, the youngest of four children of Carl Augustus Hansberry, a successful real estate broker who founded one of the city's first Black banks, and Nannie Louise (Perry) Hansberry, a schoolteacher. Her grandfather had been born enslaved. In 1937 her family moved into a house in the all-white Washington Park subdivision of Woodlawn in defiance of a racially restrictive covenant. The resulting case, Hansberry v. Lee, was decided in the family's favor by the U.S. Supreme Court in 1940. Her father died suddenly in Mexico in 1946 of a cerebral hemorrhage while planning to move the family there. She enrolled at the University of Wisconsin in 1948, left in 1950 for New York, and wrote for the newspaper Freedom. In June 1953 she married Robert Nemiroff. Sources differ on when they divorced (1962 or 1964); they continued to work together. A Raisin in the Sun opened at the Ethel Barrymore Theatre on Broadway on March 11, 1959, the first play by a Black woman to be produced on Broadway. It won the New York Drama Critics' Circle Award; she was the first Black playwright and, at 29, the youngest American to win it. In 1962 she moved to Croton-on-Hudson, New York. One account dates the start of her illness to April 1963, when she began to suffer from fainting spells and attacks of nausea; tests pointed at first to what was suspected to be a duodenal ulcer. In 1963 she had an attack, collapsed in pain and was in hospital for ten days, during which doctors diagnosed cancer; she was told she had anemia and bleeding ulcers. She had an unsuccessful operation in New York in June 1963 and surgery at the Lahey Clinic at the New England Baptist Hospital in Boston in August 1963; one account dates the operations to June 24 and August 2, and neither cured the cancer. She kept writing and speaking through her illness. In May 1964, released from the hospital for the afternoon, she gave the speech that became known as To Be Young, Gifted and Black; in June she left her sickbed for a Town Hall debate; and in the fall she moved to a hotel near the rehearsals of The Sign in Sidney Brustein's Window, attending its opening night in October 1964. She died of cancer at University Hospital in New York on January 12, 1965, at 34; The Sign in Sidney Brustein's Window closed that night. Her funeral was held in Harlem on January 15.
- **R:** The primary site of her cancer is recorded differently: most reference works give pancreatic cancer; the PBS American Masters timeline gives cancer of the duodenum; and Women in World History describes colon cancer that had spread to the pancreas. One biography dates the diagnosis to 1964 rather than 1963.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
