# Teaching Note — Ulysses S. Grant pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Hiram Ulysses Grant was born on April 27, 1822, in Point Pleasant, Ohio, the first child of Jesse and Hannah Grant. Grant died of throat cancer on July 23, 1885, at Mount McGregor, New York, days after completing his memoirs. Grant was a heavy cigar smoker: his tobacco use rose during the Civil War to about eighteen to twenty cigars a day, and he reported smoking nine or ten a day in peacetime. In June 1884, at his summer home in Long Branch, New Jersey, Grant began to have stinging throat pain and pain on swallowing. On October 22, 1884, his physician Dr. Fordyce Barker referred Grant to the throat specialist Dr. John H. Douglas, who told him the disease was serious and epithelial in character. Grant's physicians recorded a carcinoma of the right tonsillar pillar; a biopsy examined by Dr. George B. Elliot confirmed cancer at a consultation on February 19, 1885, and the doctors judged it beyond surgery. Swallowing became so painful that Grant often went without food and water; his care was aimed at comfort, with cocaine applied to numb his throat and opiates taken reluctantly for pain, while he kept writing and, by May 1885, dictating his memoirs. Grant's father, Jesse Grant, died in 1873 at age 79; his mother, Hannah Grant, died in 1883. Late in life Grant lost his personal fortune to a Wall Street swindle and wrote his Civil War memoirs to offset the loss and provide for his wife. In June 1885 Grant moved with his family to a cottage on Mount McGregor in Wilton, New York, chosen for its cool, dry air, where he died.
- **R:** Grant's cancer has been attributed to his heavy cigar smoking; the specialist he consulted in 1884 suggested a link, and cigar smoking is now known to cause cancers of the mouth and throat. Grant had recurrent severe, incapacitating headaches, interpreted retrospectively as most likely migraines. In 1858 Grant had fever and chills, possibly from malaria or typhoid, and he had periodic fevers believed to have been malaria.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
