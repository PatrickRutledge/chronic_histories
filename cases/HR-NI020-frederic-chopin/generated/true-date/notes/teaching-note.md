# Teaching Note — Frédéric Chopin pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Fryderyk Franciszek Chopin was born in the village of Żelazowa Wola, in Mazovia, on March 1, 1810, according to his own statements and his family's; his baptismal certificate, written several weeks after the birth, gives February 22. His father, Mikołaj (Nicolas) Chopin, was a Frenchman from Lorraine who settled permanently in Poland and brought up his children as Poles; the family moved to Warsaw a few months after Fryderyk's birth. In 1827 the youngest of the Chopin children, his sister Emilia, died of tuberculosis. A review of the documentary sources reports recurrent episodes of cough, fever, headaches, and swollen lymph nodes beginning in his teens. He left Warsaw on November 2, 1830, never to return, reached Paris in September 1831, and in 1834 chose the status of a political refugee rather than apply to the Russian embassy for a passport. In Paris his most important source of income was giving lessons; he became a popular teacher, and although ranked among the greatest pianists of his time, he disliked public performances, appeared rarely, and preferred an intimate group of listeners. In 1836 a proposal of marriage was accepted on condition that he take better care of his health; the engagement ended a year later, the family of his fiancée being disturbed by the bad state of his health, as he had been seriously ill in the winter. He met the writer George Sand in Paris in 1836; their relationship, which began soon after, lasted nearly ten years. In the winter of 1838 to 1839 he stayed on Majorca, arriving in Palma on November 8, 1838. In early December the rented villa became cold and damp, his health worsened, and doctors were called; he wrote on December 3 that he had become "sick as a dog" over the previous two weeks. Neighbors fearing an infectious disease led the owner to force them to leave. At the former Carthusian monastery in Valldemossa, from December 15 to February 11, he became gravely ill and showed symptoms of tuberculosis, and for many weeks was too weak to leave the house, yet composed most of the 24 Preludes. On Majorca he suffered long periods of doubt and depression, associated with his illness, and the hostility of local people who feared his illness worsened his state of mind. After Majorca he convalesced in Marseille from late February to May 1839, cared for by a physician, and moved to Nohant in central France still greatly weakened. The final parting from George Sand in July 1847 and the loss of Nohant had a devastating effect on his mental and physical state, and he almost gave up composing. From April to November 1848 he toured England and Scotland; the travel, the many performances, and a climate harmful to his lungs further damaged his health, and on November 16, 1848, despite frailty and a fever, he gave his last concert, in London. In 1849, after two hemorrhages on June 22, a physician diagnosed tuberculosis in its final stage. He died on October 17, 1849, in his apartment on the Place Vendôme in Paris, of pulmonary tuberculosis according to the Fryderyk Chopin Institute, and was buried in the Père-Lachaise cemetery; at his request his heart was taken to Warsaw and placed in a pillar of the Holy Cross Church. The same review reports that later in life he had chest pain, hemoptysis, hematemesis, neuralgia, and joint pain, and that exhaustion and breathlessness characterized all his adult life.
- **R:** A macroscopic examination of his preserved heart in 2014 found it enlarged and covered with a fibrinous coating with multiple small nodular foci, which the examiners interpreted as tuberculous pericarditis; they concluded that chronic tuberculosis was the most plausible diagnosis and that rapidly progressing tuberculous pericarditis was probably the immediate cause of death. An earlier review of his case history also judged tuberculosis the most probable diagnosis. Cystic fibrosis has been proposed as the cause of his lifelong illness and death; the roster lists a "probable cystic fibrosis variant." The heart examination's authors later argued that the disease was infectious, not genetic. Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency has also been proposed, while noting that the lost autopsy record makes any diagnosis hard to confirm. Another documentary review proposed that he had dilated cardiomyopathy with heart failure and cirrhosis, rather than tuberculosis, as the cause of death.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
