# Teaching Note — Bruce Lee pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Bruce Jun Fan Lee was born on November 27, 1940, at the Jackson Street Hospital in San Francisco's Chinatown, while his parents were on tour with the Chinese opera; his parents named him Jun Fan, and a nurse at the hospital gave him the English name Bruce. At three months old he returned with his parents to Hong Kong, where he was raised until the age of 18. His Chinese name was 李振藩 (Lee Jun-fan in Cantonese romanization); as a child actor he took the stage name 李小龍 (Lee Siu-lung, "Little Dragon"). Raised in Hong Kong, he was a child actor in some 20 films; at 13 he began studying wing chun gung fu under Yip Man, and he was also an accomplished dancer. In 1959 he returned to the United States, settled in Seattle, studied philosophy at the University of Washington and began teaching gung fu, opening schools in Seattle, Oakland and Los Angeles; he developed his own martial art, Jeet Kune Do ("the way of the intercepting fist"), grounded in simplicity, directness and personal freedom. He is known for the words "Be water, my friend." In 1970, without warming up, he lifted a 125-pound barbell in a "good morning" exercise and severely injured his back; after many tests an injury to the fourth sacral nerve was found. He was ordered to complete bed rest, told he would never do gung fu again, and stayed in bed for about six months before beginning his own recovery program; the injury became a chronic source of pain for the rest of his life. He returned to Hong Kong to make films for Golden Harvest: The Big Boss (1971), Fist of Fury and The Way of the Dragon, which he wrote, directed and starred in; Enter the Dragon, the first Hong Kong and Hollywood co-production, was released after his death. On May 10, 1973, while dubbing lines for Enter the Dragon at Golden Harvest's Hammer Hill Road studios in Hong Kong, he felt faint, collapsed in the bathroom, was helped back to the dubbing studio, vomited and fainted again; he was taken to hospital in critical condition, where a neurosurgeon diagnosed cerebral oedema and gave mannitol to reduce the swelling. He recovered. His biographer Matthew Polly, as quoted in the Clinical Kidney Journal, wrote that on May 10 "his body began convulsing" after he lost consciousness; a seizure was considered a potential cause of the episode, but because it was a single episode, epilepsy was not diagnosed. The air conditioning in the dubbing room had been turned off to avoid sound problems in the recording, and he had high temperature, weakness, vomiting and loss of consciousness, according to his biographer. He had used cannabis (hashish) on the day of the May 10 collapse; the Hong Kong neurosurgeon who treated him thought it was a factor, an explanation he rejected. On May 29 and 30, 1973, he underwent a full neurological evaluation, including a brain flow study and an electroencephalogram, that found no abnormality; phenytoin was nevertheless prescribed and he took it until his death. He was also reported to take dextropropoxyphene (Doloxene), an opioid analgesic. He died in Hong Kong on July 20, 1973, at the age of 32. That evening, at a private home in Kowloon, he complained of a bad headache, took an Equagesic tablet (a prescription analgesic combining meprobamate and aspirin), lay down to rest and could not be revived; the report from Queen Elizabeth Hospital gave the cause of death as acute cerebral oedema. His body was returned to Seattle for burial. A nine-day coroner's inquest with testimony from pathologists from several countries followed; doctors at the inquest disagreed about what had caused the cerebral oedema, the medical evidence was insufficient to prove that the drug caused it, and a verdict of death by misadventure was recorded. Traces of cannabis were found in his stomach and intestine. His biographer reported that some months before May 1973 he had the sweat glands removed from his armpits. He married in 1964; a son was born in 1965 and a daughter in 1969. His father, the Cantonese opera performer and actor Lee Hoi Chuen, died in Hong Kong in 1965, a week after the birth of his first grandchild. The Bruce Lee Foundation, founded in 2002, shares his teachings and legacy through martial arts and mindfulness programs for youth mental wellness; Hong Kong's Leisure and Cultural Services Department described him as having grown up and become famous in Hong Kong, "the pride of Hong Kong".
- **R:** The explanation favored at the inquest: a hypersensitivity reaction to the aspirin or meprobamate in Equagesic, or both, caused the cerebral oedema, as proposed by a London professor of forensic medicine and supported by the hospital pathologist who performed the postmortem. Critics note that neither drug is known to cause cerebral oedema and that he had taken the drug before. In 2022 nephrologists in Madrid proposed, from publicly available information, that the cerebral oedema was caused by hyponatraemia: that his kidneys could not excrete excess water, with risk factors that may have included high chronic fluid intake, cannabis, prescription drugs, alcohol, low solute intake, a past acute kidney injury and exercise. His biographer proposed heat stroke: the dubbing room on May 10 was unventilated, he had lost weight from overwork, and he had had the sweat glands removed from his armpits months before. The Madrid authors judged axillary sweat gland removal insufficient to impair heat loss. Others have proposed epilepsy, including sudden unexpected death in epilepsy; there is no record that he had epilepsy, and the neurologist who evaluated him after May 10 did not diagnose it. Some Hong Kong doctors believed cannabis brought on the cerebral oedema; no causal link between cannabis and cerebral oedema has been documented, and this explanation has been dismissed as speculation.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
