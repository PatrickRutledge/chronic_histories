Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Name. “Mumtaz Mahal” is a title, not a given and family name; split at the last space it would give the family name “Mahal”. Following the dataset's practice for single names, `identity.name_parts` is given [], family "Mumtaz Mahal", so the C-CDA and FHIR names are both “Mumtaz Mahal” with no given name, and the notes call her Mumtaz Mahal, the form she is recorded as preferring. Her birth name, “Arjumand Banu Begum”, appears in no other record and is the alias; “Begum” is an honorific, so it is not treated as a family name. The chronicle's “Nawab 'Aliya Begam” is recorded in the claim text only.
  2. Dates and calendars. Mughal court chronicles date events in the Hijri (Islamic lunar) calendar. Lahori gives her death as 17 Zi-l ka'da (Dhu al-Qa'da) 1040 AH; by the tabular Islamic calendar this is 17 June 1631 (Gregorian), the date in the other sources, checked by computing Julian day numbers; a date fixed by sighting the new moon can differ by a day. The state tourism page says she died on a Wednesday; 17 June 1631 was a Tuesday, and the Islamic day begins at sunset, so a death in the evening would fall on the Wednesday; this is noted, not resolved. Lahori's “fortieth year of her age” is a lunar count; by the Gregorian calendar she was 38. No Hijri birth date was found; the preferred birth date is 27 April 1593 (the encyclopedia, citing Kamboh), with the tourism page's “April 1593” agreeing. Dates in 1593 to 1631 given by modern sources are Gregorian.
  3. Index age and dates. The episode presents her at 37 in her fourteenth pregnancy. The synthetic date of birth is April 27, 1988, so the whole-year shift is 395 years and 2026 maps to 1631. She died on June 17, 1631, so every visit had to fall between January 1 and June 16, 2026; that rules out the late-1630 index the assignment suggested (a late-2026 index would leave only weeks before the November 1 cutoff, too short for prenatal care). The index visit is January 6, 2026, at 13 weeks 2 days by ultrasound (true frame January 6, 1631, when the court was at Burhanpur); the follow-ups are February 3 (consultation), March 3, April 14, and June 2, and the sick visit March 24, 2026 (true frame the same days of 1631). She is 37 until April 27 and 38 at the last visit. The ultrasound dating gives an estimated due date of July 12, 2026, so in the true frame her documented delivery on June 17, 1631, falls at about 36 and a half weeks; the gestation at her real delivery is not recorded, so this contradicts no documented fact. The episode ends at 34 weeks 2 days, before delivery, because a modeled birth would fall at or after the true date of her death; a safe delivery is planned but not recorded. The modern episode is a counterfactual and makes no claim about what happened to her.
  4. Number of children. Lahori says she bore eight sons and six daughters (fourteen), and the encyclopedia's table lists fourteen with dates; the state tourism page lists thirteen and calls her last child the thirteenth, apparently omitting the son who died soon after birth in 1622. Fourteen is preferred (Lahori's count, CLM-0010, with the encyclopedia's thirteen births before 1631, CLM-0006, and the fourteenth in June 1631, CLM-0015) and the conflict is recorded (CLM-0012, D, disputed). The fourteenth birth falls after the index date, so it is linked only to the timeline event of her death and appears in no intake document. The modern obstetric history (gravida 14, para 13, seven living children) follows the preferred count; the youngest living child in the modern frame corresponds to Husn Ara, born in April 1630.
  5. Labor and cause of death. Her death in childbirth at Burhanpur is D (Lahori; the tourism page). The labor of about 30 hours is D, probable, reported by the encyclopedia citing a 2014 case report (Kumar, Case Reports in Women's Health) and Kamboh's chronicle; neither was read. Postpartum hemorrhage as the cause is R, probable: no contemporary description of bleeding was read, and the term is a modern reading. These claims appear only in the postpartum hemorrhage planning, never as a diagnosis.
  6. Interpregnancy interval. The documented birth of her thirteenth child on 23 April 1630 and of her fourteenth on 17 June 1631 imply, if the last pregnancy reached term, a conception about five months after the previous birth. The modern episode makes the short interpregnancy interval (O09.899) a problem and the reason for anemia, growth surveillance, and contraception counseling.
  7. Whereabouts. Lahori places the emperor and court at Burhanpur in 1040 AH (from August 1630), during the Deccan famine, and she died there in June 1631, so she was with the court on campaign at the index date in the true frame. The modern episode echoes this only as frequent travel with her family.
  8. Sources not read. Encyclopaedia Iranica, Britannica, UNESCO's Taj Mahal page, and Archnet returned HTTP 403 or 429; the Archaeological Survey of India's Taj Mahal page returned only its home page; Kumar (2014) was behind a publisher's bot check; Ebba Koch's The Complete Taj Mahal (2006) is available only through lending libraries. None is cited for a claim. The state tourism page's description of her as an “exotic beauty”, its praise of her looks, and the encyclopedia's remarks on her marriage and her husband's other wives are not used.
  9. Respect and culture. The record describes her as a Shi'a Muslim empress of Persian descent, without exoticizing her. The modern episode records her own preferences (women clinicians, a chaperone, languages, daily prayer), her own choice not to fast during Ramadan in pregnancy, and her question about which contraceptive methods her faith permits, answered by explaining that many Muslim scholars permit reversible contraception and that she may consult a religious adviser of her choosing. The clinicians do not decide for her.
  10. Relatives. Her husband, children, father, and aunt appear only in documented claims. The modern episode says only that she is married with seven living children and that six children died young; it names no relative and attributes no statement, decision, or health fact to her husband or any other relative. The family history records the six documented deaths of her children before the index date (two of smallpox, the others with causes not recorded in the cited source).
  11. Visit types. The index visit is the first prenatal visit (the H&P). The four prenatal follow-ups are focused chronic-care visits. The unscheduled sick visit is acute cystitis at 24 weeks, chosen because urinary infection is common in pregnancy and after many births, must always be treated in pregnancy, and teaches drug choice by trimester (nitrofurantoin in the second trimester, not at term) and a test-of-cure culture; it echoes no documented illness. No `preventive_adult` visit is added: prenatal care is the preventive care of pregnancy, and its preventive elements (screening tests, influenza and Tdap vaccines, folic acid, aspirin, mood and intimate partner violence screening) are in the prenatal visits. The RSV vaccine is not offered because the 32- to 36-week window falls outside the season.
  12. Drug choices. Oral iron every other day first, then ferric carboxymaltose 750 mg intravenously twice, four weeks apart, in the second trimester (ACOG Practice Bulletin 233); the RxNorm code is the 15 mL vial (750 mg). Aspirin 81 mg from 13 weeks for two moderate risk factors under the USPSTF (age 35 or older and a previous adverse pregnancy outcome, a baby who died soon after birth). Nitrofurantoin for cystitis at 24 weeks. Oxytocin and tranexamic acid appear only in the birth plan, not as medication records, because the episode ends before delivery.
  13. Labs and instruments. The prenatal panel (blood group and antibody screen, rubella, hepatitis B, HIV, syphilis, hepatitis C), urine cultures, hemoglobin electrophoresis, urine dipsticks, and cell-free DNA results are qualitative and kept in the note text. EPDS (99046-5) is coded in each trimester; all scores are below the threshold with item 10 scored 0, so no safety assessment is required. No `safety_assessment` is recorded.
  14. Focused visits. The four prenatal follow-ups (17, 21, 27, and 34 weeks) are documented as focused visits on ACOG Obstetric Care Consensus No. 11, ACOG Practice Bulletins 183 and 233, the WHO recommendations, and routine prenatal care: blood pressure and urine protein, fetal heart and fundal height, weight, hemoglobin and ferritin, aspirin, anatomy and placental assessment, glucose screening, Tdap, mood screening, travel planning, and the postpartum hemorrhage risk assessment. One deviation is recorded as `deferred`, with its outcome and corrective action, and no result was invented: the visits due at 30 and 32 weeks were missed during six weeks of family travel, so no blood pressure was checked; at 34 weeks blood pressure, urine protein, and growth were normal, and she now has weekly visits, a home blood pressure cuff, and stays near the hospital.
  15. Consultation. The maternal-fetal medicine referral and consultation (Adaeze Montgomery-Rahimi, MD, a fictional consultant, on the day of the 17-week visit) are S. The consultant recommends what the record then shows: intravenous iron that day and four weeks later (given at 17 and 21 weeks, with anemia corrected by 27 weeks), an anatomy scan with placental location (normal, no previa or accreta signs, so no repeat scan), aspirin, glucose screening (normal at 27 weeks), two-weekly visits from 28 weeks (missed during travel and recorded as a deviation), a growth scan (done at 34 weeks), birth in a hospital with a blood bank and induction at 39 weeks (chosen at 34 weeks), the hemorrhage plan with oxytocin, quantitative blood loss, and tranexamic acid within 3 hours (agreed at 34 weeks), travel advice, and contraception counseling (done at 34 weeks).
  16. Delivery as a judgment call. The assignment allowed a safe delivery with managed hemorrhage as a counterfactual. It is not modeled, because the episode must end before her true-frame date of death and a delivery near that date would compress the plan; the 34-week visit documents the full prevention and treatment plan instead.
