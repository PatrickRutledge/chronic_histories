# Teaching Note — Mumtaz Mahal pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** She was born Arjumand Banu Begum in Agra in April 1593; an encyclopedia, citing a seventeenth-century court history, gives 27 April 1593. Her father, Abu'l-Hasan Asaf Khan, was a Persian noble; his sister was the Empress Nur Jahan, wife of the emperor Jahangir. Her mother's family came from Qazvin in Persia. She was betrothed to Prince Khurram, later the emperor Shah Jahan, in 1607, and married him on 10 May 1612; he gave her the title Mumtaz Mahal, rendered as 'Chosen One of the Palace' or 'the Exalted One of the Palace'. She was a Shi'a Muslim. Despite her frequent pregnancies, she travelled with her husband's entourage on his military campaigns. Her first thirteen children were born between 1613 and 1630: Hur-ul-Nisa (1613), Jahanara (1614), Dara Shikoh (1615), Shah Shuja (1616), Roshanara (1617), Aurangzeb (1618), Izad Bakhsh (1619), Surayya Banu (1621), a son who died soon after birth (1622), Murad Bakhsh (1624), Lutf Allah (1626), Daulat Afza (1628), and Husn Ara (1630). Six of her children died in infancy or early childhood before 1631: Hur-ul-Nisa at three and Surayya Banu at seven, both of smallpox; Izad Bakhsh in infancy; a son soon after birth in 1622; Lutf Allah at about eighteen months; and Daulat Afza in infancy. Her thirteenth child, Husn Ara, was born on 23 April 1630. In the year 1040 AH (from August 1630) the emperor was in the Deccan with the court at Burhanpur, during a severe famine in the Deccan and Gujarat, when he ordered soup kitchens and weekly alms for as long as he remained at Burhanpur. The court chronicler Abdul Hamid Lahori recorded that on 17 Zi-l ka'da 1040 AH she died, in the fortieth year of her age, to the great grief of her husband, having borne him eight sons and six daughters; the translators note that she died in childbirth. The date corresponds to 17 June 1631 in the Gregorian calendar, at Burhanpur, giving birth to a daughter, Gauhar Ara. Later accounts state that she died after a prolonged labor of about 30 hours with her fourteenth child. The state tourism page for the Taj Mahal calls Gauhar Ara her thirteenth child and lists thirteen children, while the court chronicle counts fourteen (eight sons and six daughters). Her fourteenth child, Gauhar Ara, was born on 17 June 1631, about fourteen months after Husn Ara. She was first buried at Burhanpur in the walled Zainabad garden on the Tapti River; her body was moved to Agra in December 1631, and the Taj Mahal was built as her tomb over more than twenty years.
- **R:** Her death is attributed to postpartum hemorrhage.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
