# Teaching Note — Billie Holiday pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Billie Holiday was born Eleanora Fagan in Philadelphia on April 7, 1915, and was raised in Baltimore by her mother, Sarah Julia "Sadie" Fagan. Her mother died in October 1945; afterwards Holiday drank more heavily and her drug use increased as she grieved. Holiday began using heroin in the early 1940s, and her addiction became severe in the late 1940s; she was arrested repeatedly over her heroin use in that period. In May 1947 Holiday was arrested for narcotics possession; she pleaded guilty, was sentenced to a year and a day, and was held at the federal facility for women at Alderson, West Virginia, until her early release on March 16, 1948. Holiday sought treatment for her addiction, at one point checking herself into an institution in the hope of breaking her habit. Her conviction cost her the New York City cabaret card she needed to perform in clubs that served alcohol. Her autobiography, Lady Sings the Blues, written in collaboration with William Dufty, was published in 1956. In 1959 Holiday was admitted to Metropolitan Hospital in New York with liver and heart problems and was diagnosed with cirrhosis. While she was in the hospital she was arrested for narcotics possession and kept under police guard. She died there on July 17, 1959, aged 44; the cause of death is given as cirrhosis of the liver with pulmonary edema, or pulmonary edema and heart failure. After 1950 she slipped deeper into alcohol use over the following seven years.
- **R:** The Federal Bureau of Narcotics pursued Holiday for years, and later writers have described that pursuit as driven by racism and linked it to her performance of "Strange Fruit," a song against lynching.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
