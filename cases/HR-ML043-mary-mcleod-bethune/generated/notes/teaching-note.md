# Teaching Note — Mary McLeod Bethune pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Mary Jane McLeod was born on July 10, 1875, near Mayesville, South Carolina, the fifteenth of seventeen children of Samuel and Patsy McLeod. Her parents, and eight of her older siblings, had been enslaved; after emancipation the family bought five acres of land and farmed cotton. She was the only McLeod child to get an education, first at a Presbyterian mission school near Mayesville and then at Scotia Seminary. In 1898 she married Albertus Bethune; they separated after about nine years without divorcing, and he died in 1918. In 1904 she founded a school for Black girls in Daytona Beach, Florida. She opened McLeod Hospital there in 1911, which the National Park Service says played a critical role in containing the influenza pandemic of 1918, and she and her students faced down the Ku Klux Klan at the school. The school merged with the Cookman Institute (dated 1923 by the National Park Service; named Bethune-Cookman College in 1929 according to another reference), and she was its president until 1942. She was president of the National Association of Colored Women from 1924, received the NAACP's Spingarn Medal in 1935, and founded the National Council of Negro Women on December 5, 1935. She directed the Division of Negro Affairs of the National Youth Administration from the mid-1930s until it closed in 1943 and led the informal group of Black federal advisers known as the Black Cabinet. She said of her place in Washington: "When they see me, they know that the Negro is present." For many years she suffered from chronic asthma, which increasingly restricted her ability to travel, and after a particularly severe attack in 1942 she gave up the presidency of Bethune-Cookman College. The guide to her foundation's papers lists "Bethune asthma attacks" as a major topic of her administrative correspondence in the first half of 1943, and describes her health problems as a recurrent subject of her correspondence in those years. From 1935 she lived in a series of apartments in Washington, D.C., while keeping her home, known as the Retreat, on the campus in Daytona Beach. In December 1943 she and the National Council of Negro Women bought 1318 Vermont Avenue, which became the council's first national headquarters and her Washington residence. She retired to Florida in the late 1940s while still president of the National Council of Negro Women, and on her 75th birthday in 1950 left public life for her small home on the Bethune-Cookman campus. In her last years her papers record a description of an asthma clinic in Biloxi, Mississippi (1950), and her failing health and a hospitalization (1952); in 1952 she travelled to Liberia as a U.S. delegate. She died of a heart attack in Daytona Beach, Florida, in May 1955, at 79. Two reference works give May 18, 1955; the National Park Service gives May 20 and says she died at home. Noting that her death was not far off, she had written "My Last Will and Testament," published by Ebony after her death.
- **R:** Retrospective interpretation: a fatal heart attack at 79 is most consistent with coronary heart disease, but no source found describes heart symptoms, a cardiac diagnosis or blood pressure readings during her life.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
