Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Mary Jane McLeod was born on July 10, 1875, near Mayesville, South Carolina, the fifteenth of seventeen children of Samuel and Patsy McLeod. Her parents, and eight of her older siblings, had been enslaved; after emancipation the family bought five acres of land and farmed cotton. [D]
  • She was the only McLeod child to get an education, first at a Presbyterian mission school near Mayesville and then at Scotia Seminary. In 1898 she married Albertus Bethune; they separated after about nine years without divorcing, and he died in 1918. [D]
  • In 1904 she founded a school for Black girls in Daytona Beach, Florida. She opened McLeod Hospital there in 1911, which the National Park Service says played a critical role in containing the influenza pandemic of 1918, and she and her students faced down the Ku Klux Klan at the school. The school merged with the Cookman Institute (dated 1923 by the National Park Service; named Bethune-Cookman College in 1929 according to another reference), and she was its president until 1942. [D]
  • She was president of the National Association of Colored Women from 1924, received the NAACP's Spingarn Medal in 1935, and founded the National Council of Negro Women on December 5, 1935. She directed the Division of Negro Affairs of the National Youth Administration from the mid-1930s until it closed in 1943 and led the informal group of Black federal advisers known as the Black Cabinet. She said of her place in Washington: "When they see me, they know that the Negro is present." [D]
  • For many years she suffered from chronic asthma, which increasingly restricted her ability to travel, and after a particularly severe attack in 1942 she gave up the presidency of Bethune-Cookman College. [D]
  • The guide to her foundation's papers lists "Bethune asthma attacks" as a major topic of her administrative correspondence in the first half of 1943, and describes her health problems as a recurrent subject of her correspondence in those years. [D]
  • From 1935 she lived in a series of apartments in Washington, D.C., while keeping her home, known as the Retreat, on the campus in Daytona Beach. In December 1943 she and the National Council of Negro Women bought 1318 Vermont Avenue, which became the council's first national headquarters and her Washington residence. [D]

Assessment

  • Asthma, confirmed, uncontrolled, with type 2 inflammation (J45.40). Question 1: this is asthma. The 270 mL, 21 percent bronchodilator response confirms variable airflow obstruction, and the post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC of 0.72 with a normal DLCO argues against COPD, which in any case would be unusual in a never smoker. Question 2: read with race-neutral GLI Global equations, as the American Thoracic Society now recommends, her FEV1 is 64 percent of predicted; race-based equations would have set a lower predicted value for her and made the impairment look milder, which is one way asthma in Black patients has been under-recognized. Question 4: FeNO 46 ppb, eosinophils 0.42, total IgE 210 and dust mite sensitization show type 2 (allergic and eosinophilic) inflammation. She does not have severe asthma, because she has never been treated with an inhaled corticosteroid, so biologic therapy is not indicated now; if she remained uncontrolled on optimized step 4 to 5 treatment, or had repeated oral corticosteroid courses, she would qualify for assessment, and anti-IL-5 or anti-IL-4 receptor therapy would fit her phenotype.
