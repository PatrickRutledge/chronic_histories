# Teaching Note — Gwen Ifill pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Gwendolyn Ifill was born in Queens, New York, on September 29, 1955, the fifth of six children. Her father, a Panamanian immigrant, became a pastor in the African Methodist Episcopal Church; her mother was from Barbados. The family moved around the Northeast for her father's work and for a time lived in federally subsidized housing. She graduated from Simmons College in Boston in 1977. As a college intern at the Boston Herald American she found a racist note left for her; the paper's editors hired her after she graduated. She went on to report for the Baltimore Evening Sun, The Washington Post, The New York Times and NBC News. In 1999 she became moderator of PBS's Washington Week in Review, the first African American woman to host a major national political talk show, and a senior correspondent for the NewsHour. In 2013 she and Judy Woodruff were named co-anchors of the PBS NewsHour, the first women to jointly lead a national nightly news broadcast. She moderated the vice-presidential debates of 2004 and 2008. In the 2004 debate she asked the candidates about AIDS in the United States, citing the much higher death rate among Black women aged 25 to 44. She wrote The Breakthrough: Politics and Race in the Age of Obama (2009). On April 7, 2016, she told viewers she would be off the air for a few weeks; PBS described the reason as health issues it did not specify, and in May said she was doing well and expected to return around Memorial Day. She was back on the air by the start of June, when she interviewed President Obama. She kept her illness private. After her death a close friend said she had been diagnosed with cancer at the end of 2015 or early in 2016, had told family and friends, and that the April leave was due to complications of treatment; another friend described her keeping up her work through chemotherapy. PBS said she died after several months of cancer treatment. She took a second leave shortly before her death and died at a hospice in Washington, D.C., on November 14, 2016, at 61. Her family gave the cause as endometrial cancer.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
