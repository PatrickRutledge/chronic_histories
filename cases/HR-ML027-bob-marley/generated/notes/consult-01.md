## Assessment

1. **Acral lentiginous melanoma of the right great toe nail unit, clinical stage IB (cT1b N0 M0)** (C43.71). A thin invasive acral melanoma of the nail unit, T1b by the AJCC 8th edition because it is 0.8 mm thick, with no clinical sign of spread: clinical stage IB. No scans or blood tests are needed at this stage. The chance that a sentinel node contains melanoma is about 5 to 10 percent, so a sentinel lymph node biopsy is offered; it gives the most accurate stage, which decides whether treatment after surgery would be considered. There are two surgical choices. Amputation at the interphalangeal joint has the lowest chance of the melanoma returning in the toe. Digit-sparing en bloc excision of the whole nail unit down to the bone, with a 5 to 10 mm margin and a full-thickness skin graft, keeps the toe and its function; for melanomas this thin, published series show similar survival, with a higher chance (in some series about one in five) of a local recurrence that would then be treated by amputation. Either is oncologically appropriate for him; it is his decision, and his wish to keep the toe for his faith, his music and his football is a sound reason to choose the digit-sparing operation. If the excision showed a thicker melanoma or involved margins, I would recommend amputation. Adjuvant immunotherapy is considered only from stage IIB, so it is not expected to be needed unless the sentinel node is positive. **S**

## Recommendations

1. Lymphoscintigraphy and right inguinal sentinel lymph node biopsy, with the definitive operation, in two weeks.
2. Definitive surgery by his choice: digit-sparing en bloc excision of the nail unit down to the bone with a 5 to 10 mm margin and a full-thickness skin graft from the inner upper arm; amputation at the interphalangeal joint if the margins are involved or the melanoma proves thicker.
3. No staging CT, PET, or LDH for clinical stage IB without symptoms; no blood tests needed before surgery.
4. No adjuvant therapy if the sentinel node is negative; surveillance with history and examination of the skin, nails, graft and nodes every 6 to 12 months for 5 years and then yearly, with a yearly full skin examination by dermatology.
5. Monthly skin self-examination, including the palms, soles, between the toes and all nails, the graft, and both groins, taught after surgery.
6. A stiff-soled shoe until the graft heals, then a graded return to running and football.
