# Teaching Note — Bob Marley pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Robert Nesta Marley was born on February 6, 1945, at Nine Mile in Saint Ann Parish, Jamaica, the son of a Jamaican mother and a white English father. He was a Jamaican singer, songwriter and musician, considered one of the pioneers of reggae, who toured the world with his band, the Wailers. He was raised a Roman Catholic and converted to Rastafari in the late 1960s; he became known as a Rastafarian icon who infused his music with spirituality. In 1977 he noticed a pigmented lesion on the nail bed of his right great toe and thought it was the result of a football injury. The first physician he saw thought that a reasonable explanation; because the lesion was growing, he saw a second physician, who performed a biopsy. In July 1977 he was diagnosed with acral lentiginous melanoma of the right great toe, presenting as a pigmented lesion under the nail. His doctors advised amputation of the toe, which he declined. Instead the nail and nail bed and the cancerous tissue were removed, and the area was covered with skin from his thigh. The sources give his reasons for declining amputation in different words: one reports that he said it would be against his Rastafari faith, another that his Rastafarian beliefs prohibited it, and an encyclopedia article that he cited religious beliefs and that amputation would have hindered his performing career. He continued to tour. In September 1980 he collapsed while jogging in Central Park, New York, and was found to have cancer that had spread to his brain, lungs and liver; his last concert was in Pittsburgh two days later. He was treated at cancer centers including Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, where he received radiation therapy, and then at the clinic of Josef Issels in Bavaria, Germany. On a flight home to Jamaica his condition worsened, the flight was diverted to Miami, and he died at Cedars of Lebanon Hospital in Miami on May 11, 1981, at 36. He has long served as the posthumous public face of skin cancer in people with skin of color; acral lentiginous melanoma is the most common melanoma in people of color, and awareness campaigns in his memory urge everyone to check their palms, soles, and nails.
- **R:** Whether the football injury caused the lesion is disputed: an encyclopedia article states that the lesion was not caused by the injury but was a sign of a cancer already present, and clinicians note that many people with acral melanoma recall an injury to the site, while the role of trauma is not established.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
