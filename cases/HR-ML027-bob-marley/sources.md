## Historical evidence

1. Fischer H. “Dying young: Bob Marley (1945–1981).” *Hektoen International*, April 2024. A physician’s medical-humanities essay. Supports that he was the son of a Jamaican mother and a white English father; was raised Roman Catholic and converted to Rastafari in the late 1960s; in 1977 noticed a pigmented lesion on the nail bed of his right great toe that he thought was a football injury; that the first physician thought this reasonable and a second, because the lesion was growing, performed a biopsy showing acral lentiginous melanoma; that he refused amputation, “which might have been curative,” and “declined to have the toe amputated because he said it would be against his Rastafari faith”; that the tumor later spread to his lungs, brain and liver; his treatment at the clinic of Josef Issels in Bavaria; and his death in Miami after the flight home was diverted. https://hekint.org/2024/04/18/dying-young-bob-marley-1945-1981/
2. Kyriakou G, et al. “Don’t worry about a thing … every little thing gonna be all right (except for acral lentiginous melanoma).” *Clin Dermatol* 2022;40(5):613–616 (PMID 36509509). Abstract only. Supports the diagnosis in July 1977 of acral lentiginous melanoma of the right great toe presenting as a pigmented subungual lesion; that he was advised amputation, refused, and opted for less invasive solutions; his death from metastatic disease in May 1981 at 36; and that he has served as the posthumous “poster child” for skin cancer in people with skin of color. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36509509/
3. Piccotti T. “How Did Bob Marley Die? What to Know About the Singer’s Cancer Diagnosis and Death.” Biography.com, updated April 17, 2024. Supports the diagnosis in July 1977 after he sought care for what he believed was a soccer injury; that doctors recommended amputation, which he refused “because his Rastafarian beliefs prohibited it”; that skin and tissue around the nail bed were removed and replaced with skin from his thigh; his collapse while jogging in Central Park in the fall of 1980, with spread to the brain, lungs and liver; radiation therapy and care at Memorial Sloan Kettering; the Issels clinic; his last concert in Pittsburgh on September 23, 1980; and his death on May 11, 1981, at Cedars of Lebanon Hospital in Miami. It also quotes a dermatologist that many patients with acral melanoma describe trauma to the site. https://www.biography.com/musicians/a46803099/how-did-bob-marley-die
4. The Skin Cancer Foundation. “Bob Marley Should Not Have Died from Melanoma,” March 21, 2024. Supports his birth on February 6, 1945; a dark spot under his toenail attributed to a soccer injury; that he chose excisional surgery removing the nail bed and cancerous tissue instead of the recommended amputation; that acral lentiginous melanoma is the most common form of melanoma in people of color; and the advice to check the palms, soles, and the nails every month. https://www.skincancer.org/blog/bob-marley-should-not-have-died-from-melanoma/

## Editorial resolutions

- **Recent-death rule.** He died in 1981, so the rule does not apply. Even so, the historical claims are limited to what the fetched sources state.
- **His reasons for declining amputation.** The reliable sources agree that he was advised amputation and declined it (CLM-0006), but they put his reasons in different words: Hektoen International reports that he said it would be against his Rastafari faith, Biography.com that his Rastafarian beliefs prohibited it, and Wikipedia that he cited religious beliefs and that amputation would have hindered his performing career. CLM-0007 records all three, with certainty “probable,” without choosing between them. Other reasons found only on low-quality sites (for example, fear of appearing weak) are not claimed. The record makes no general statement about what the Rastafari faith permits or forbids, and it never presents his faith as the cause of his death. Whether amputation would have cured him cannot be known; Hektoen’s “might have been curative” is not repeated as a claim.
- **The football injury.** That he first thought the lesion was a football injury, and that the first physician agreed, is **D** (CLM-0004). Whether the injury caused it is **R**, disputed (CLM-0008): Wikipedia says it did not, and clinicians say only that many patients recall trauma. The modern episode uses the injury only as the reason the band was first called a bruise, and teaches that a bruise under the nail grows out while a melanoma band does not.
- **Missed follow-up.** Biography.com states that he did not attend routine follow-up appointments after the diagnosis. This single-source statement is not claimed, because it adds nothing to the teaching and could be read as blame for his outcome.
- **Cannabis.** No fetched reliable source about his illness documents his cannabis use in relation to his health. The encyclopedia mentions a conviction for possession of a small quantity and his support for legalization, but these are not health facts and are not used. No cannabis use is recorded, no substance-use disorder is coded, and the preventive visit records only a negative single-question screen for unhealthy drug use.
- **Faith in the modern episode.** His Rastafari faith appears as he and the sources describe it (CLM-0003, CLM-0007): in the modern episode he says that his faith, his music and his football matter to him in the decision, and asks to be understood before he decides. No dietary, grooming, or other practice is attributed to him, and no clinician questions his faith.
- **The shared decision.** The modern episode sets out amputation at the interphalangeal joint and digit-sparing excision of the whole nail unit as two oncologically appropriate choices for a 0.8 mm (T1b) nail melanoma, with honest figures for local recurrence. He chooses the digit-sparing operation and agrees in advance to amputation if the pathology requires it. The thickness was chosen at the upper limit of “thin” melanoma so that a sentinel node biopsy is offered and the digit-sparing choice remains appropriate. Judgment call for the owner: the systematic review supporting digit-sparing surgery mainly covers in situ and minimally invasive melanoma; a reviewer may prefer a thinner synthetic lesion.
- **Index age and dates.** The synthetic date of birth is February 6, 1994 (shift 49 years). The index visit on June 8, 2026 maps to June 8, 1977, when he was 32, in the year he noticed the lesion and before his documented diagnosis in July 1977. The other visits (June 17, July 15, August 24, September 21, and October 19, 2026) and the surgery (July 1, 2026) map to the same days of 1977, within his lifetime; they are not a claim about where he was, and he was touring in Europe for part of that year.
- **Presenting problem.** The pigmented band with a Hutchinson sign (L60.8) is the presenting problem at intake, resolved at the consultation on June 17, 2026, when the biopsy result led to the diagnosis of acral lentiginous melanoma (C43.71). The intake documents state only what was known then.
- **Synthetic clinical picture.** All visits, the dermoscopy and pathology, the Breslow thickness and stage, the operation (with a graft from the inner upper arm, where his documented graft came from the thigh), the sentinel node result, the pain and mood scores, his height (170 cm) and weight, and the earlier urgent care visit are synthetic. Qualitative results (HIV, hepatitis B and C tests) appear only in the note text.
- **Sick visit.** A lateral ankle sprain at football seven and a half weeks after surgery (S93.401A), chosen because it fits his documented love of football, interacts sensibly with the case (the graft must be checked, and the sprain is unrelated to the melanoma), and insinuates no undocumented real diagnosis. Ottawa rules negative; ibuprofen for five days; healed by the next visits.
- **Preventive visit.** A periodic adult preventive visit at 32 (CPT 99395 and 99497 in plain text): depression, alcohol, drug, and tobacco screens; one-time HIV and hepatitis C screening and hepatitis B screening with vaccination (two-dose HepB-CpG, completed at the next visit); influenza and COVID-19 vaccines; hearing protection on stage; a full skin examination as part of melanoma surveillance; and advance care planning with an unnamed agent.
- **Focused visits.** The three chronic-care follow-ups (diagnosis and consultation, two weeks after surgery, and the first surveillance visit) are documented as focused visits on the NCCN cutaneous melanoma guideline, the AAD primary melanoma guideline, the AJCC 8th edition, the ASCO-SSO sentinel node guideline, and the nail-unit excision review. One protocol deviation is recorded instead of an invented result: the full skin examination at diagnosis was not recorded at the dermatology biopsy visit; Dr. Clarke examined the whole skin at the consultation visit and found no other lesion (no harm), and a yearly dermatology examination was booked.
- **Consultation.** The surgical oncology referral and the consultation by Naledi Mbeki-Rowe, MD (a fictional consultant), on the diagnosis day, are **S**. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: clinical stage IB with no scans or blood tests; sentinel node biopsy offered and done (negative); digit-sparing excision by his choice, with clear margins, so amputation was not needed; no adjuvant therapy at pathologic stage IA, with surveillance every 6 to 12 months; and monthly self-examination of the palms, soles, toes, and nails, taught two weeks after surgery.
- **Real people.** His parents, Josef Issels, and the clinicians who treated him in his lifetime appear only in the claims, without names except Issels. No relative is named or given a role in the modern episode; his health care agent is unnamed. No other figure with a record in the dataset is named.
