# Teaching Note — Fannie Lou Hamer pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Fannie Lou Hamer was born on October 6, 1917, in Montgomery County, Mississippi, the twentieth child of Lou Ella and James Lee Townsend, sharecroppers. She joined her family in the cotton fields at six and left school after the sixth grade, at 12, to work on the cotton plantations of the Delta. She worked for eighteen years as a sharecropper and timekeeper on a Delta plantation, made timekeeper because she could read and write. In 1964 she gave her home as Ruleville, in Sunflower County, Mississippi. In 1961, hospitalized for abdominal pain, she was found to have a uterine tumor; during the operation to remove it, a white surgeon performed a hysterectomy without her knowledge or informed consent. She later called this a "Mississippi appendectomy," a name for the sterilizations done without consent to Black women in the South. On August 31, 1962, she tried to register to vote in Indianola; when she refused to withdraw her application, the plantation owner ordered her off his land and she had to leave that same night. On June 9, 1963, returning from a voter-registration workshop, she was arrested with other activists in Winona, Mississippi, and in the jail two Black prisoners were ordered by officers to beat her with a blackjack, as she testified the next year. The beating left her with lifelong injuries to her eyes, legs and kidneys: permanent kidney damage, and a blood clot in her left eye with loss of vision in one eye. On August 22, 1964, she testified before the Credentials Committee of the Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City about her eviction and the Winona beating. In her late fifties she found a lump in her left breast and did not seek care for it at first. Appeals were made to help with her mounting medical bills. In early 1977 she was admitted for the last time to the hospital in Mound Bayou, where she was treated for complications of heart disease, diabetes and breast cancer. She died on March 14, 1977, in Mound Bayou, Mississippi, at 59. Accounts of the cause differ: complications of hypertension and breast cancer, complications of heart disease and cancer, or breast cancer.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
