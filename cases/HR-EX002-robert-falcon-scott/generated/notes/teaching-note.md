# Teaching Note — Robert Falcon Scott pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Robert Falcon Scott was born on June 6, 1868, at Plymouth, Devon, into a seafaring family, and joined his first Royal Navy ship when he was 13. He led the Discovery expedition to the Antarctic from 1901 to 1904. In October 1902, at the Discovery's winter quarters, scurvy was found after a sledge party returned from the western journey; the expedition's surgeon then examined everyone on board and found signs in a good many men, in most only the merest indication, and the outbreak was over by about October 20. Scott arranged to replace part of the sledging pemmican with cooked seal meat. On the southern sledge journey (November 1902 to February 1903) the party examined gums and legs every Sunday. When one companion developed angry-looking gums in late December 1902, they stopped eating bacon and increased the seal-meat ration, and Scott recorded that the scurvy symptoms lessened. At a thorough medical examination on the southern journey on January 14, 1903, Scott recorded that he himself had "distinctly red gums, and a very slight swelling in the ankles." Two days later, on a double ration of seal meat, his gums were much cleaner and his leg vastly improved; by the last days of the journey he wrote that their scurvy had been advancing again and that his ankles were considerably swollen. After the southern party returned to the ship in February 1903, Scott wrote that he had both legs much swollen and very uncomfortable gums, and an extraordinary lassitude, physical and mental, that lasted many weeks before he returned to a normally vigorous condition. On the southern journey, on December 20, 1902, he wrote that his tobacco supply was so low that he had been trying tea leaves, which were "nothing less than horrid." After the Discovery expedition the needs of the naval service called him to the command of battleships and to confidential work at the Admiralty, and five years passed before he resumed Antarctic work. In 1908 he married the sculptor Kathleen Bruce; they had one son. On November 24, 1909, he wrote from the offices of the British Antarctic Expedition at 36 and 38 Victoria Street, London, that the Pole was at the forefront of the campaign for raising funds but that scientific work would not be neglected. The Terra Nova left the Roath Basin at Cardiff on June 15, 1910, with Scott and members of the British Antarctic Expedition on board. He reached the South Pole on January 17 to 18, 1912, and found that a rival expedition had already been there. On the return march, on March 18, 1912, he wrote that his right foot had "gone, nearly all the toes," and on March 19 that amputation was "the least I can hope for now." All five men of the polar party died of cold and hunger. Two died earlier on the return march; Scott and the two remaining companions died in their tent about 11 miles short of a depot of food and fuel. His last journal entry, dated March 29, 1912, records a continuous gale since March 21, fuel and food exhausted, and ends "For God's sake look after our people." A search party found the tent on November 12, 1912. His original diaries of the second expedition, from October 18, 1910 to March 29, 1912, are held by the British Library as Add MS 51024-51042.
- **R:** In his account of the Discovery expedition he wrote that scurvy was then believed to be ptomaine poisoning from tainted meat. A modern historian of medicine notes that many British doctors of the time accepted this view and overlooked the experiments of Holst and Frølich on dietary deficiency, so medical advice given to him before his second expedition would probably not have helped him prevent scurvy. A historian of medicine concludes that scurvy may have contributed to the general ill health of the polar party in 1912, but that their deaths were more likely caused by a combination of frostbite, malnutrition and hypothermia. Physiologists reviewing the expedition conclude that rations of a different composition, giving more energy for the same weight, might have saved some of the party, and that more vitamin C, though hard to achieve at the time, could have improved their chances. Atmospheric scientists comparing the party's temperature records with modern automatic weather stations found that daily minimum temperatures from late February to March 19, 1912 were on average 10 to 20 °F below those measured in the same region and season since 1985, and that this cold likely contributed substantially to the party's exhaustion and frostbite.
