## Assessment

1. **Fitness for a long polar expedition**. He is fit to take part, including in long sledge journeys. He has no symptoms and a low ten-year cardiovascular risk, so an exercise test or echocardiogram would add little and is not needed; a graded training program before departure will prepare him better. Dental, hearing and eye checks are worth completing, because none can be fixed in the field. **S**
2. **Stage 1 hypertension** (I10). Confirmed by home readings, with no sign of target-organ damage on the tests so far; the urine albumin test is still needed. At his low risk the guideline advises three to six months of lifestyle measures first, which fits his timetable. If the home average stays at or above 130/80 at about three months, an angiotensin receptor blocker such as losartan is the best choice for the expedition: a thiazide adds to salt and water loss with cold diuresis and long marches, and a beta-blocker can worsen cold extremities and blunt the response to exercise. It should start at least four weeks before departure, so that electrolytes and creatinine can be checked two to four weeks after starting, with written sick-day rules for vomiting, diarrhea and dehydration. **S**
3. **Personal history of scurvy; nutrition for man-hauling** (Z86.39). His earlier scurvy came from months without fresh food, and his recovery on seal meat fits vitamin C deficiency. Scurvy can appear after one to three months of very low intake, so a reliable daily source is essential on preserved rations. Man-hauling in extreme cold needs roughly 5,000 to 6,500 kcal a day, more than earlier sledging rations gave, and energy-dense fat is the lightest way to carry it. **S**
4. **Cold injury and altitude**. Frostbite and hypothermia are the main environmental threats on the journeys. Prevention, early recognition, and correct field treatment (rewarming only when refreezing can be prevented) matter more than any medicine. The polar plateau is high, and the cold makes its effect on the body greater than its height suggests, so altitude illness is possible on the ascent. **S**
5. **Mental health and sleep in isolation**. PHQ-9 3 and GAD-7 4 are minimal. The polar winter, constant daylight on the journeys, crowding, and months of separation from his family are known strains; a plan made now is more useful than a crisis plan later. **S**

## Recommendations

1. No exercise test or echocardiogram; a graded training program with weighted-pack walks and endurance work before departure.
2. Blood pressure: lifestyle measures now; home readings before each visit; urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio; if the home average is still 130/80 or higher at about three months, start losartan, at least four weeks before departure, with electrolytes and creatinine in two to four weeks and written sick-day rules; avoid thiazides and beta-blockers.
3. Stop tobacco completely, with varenicline and counseling, because nicotine narrows skin blood vessels and adds to frostbite risk; no nicotine products in the field.
4. Nutrition: about 5,000 to 6,500 kcal a day on man-hauling journeys with a high fat share; weigh weekly in the hut; fresh seal meat when available; vitamin C 250 mg daily from tablets packed in every sledging ration when no fresh food is available, well above the recommended 90 mg and far below the 2,000 mg upper limit; a weekly check of gums and legs.
5. Cold injury: a wilderness first aid course before departure for him and the sledging parties, covering frostbite and hypothermia prevention, rewarming in water at 37 to 39 °C only when refreezing can be prevented, ibuprofen for frostbite, gentle handling and insulation of hypothermic casualties, and casualty packaging in a sledge.
6. Altitude: gradual ascent to the plateau, with acetazolamide in the medical kit to treat acute mountain sickness.
7. Mental health and sleep: repeat PHQ-9 and GAD-7 before departure; a fixed routine, bright light in the polar winter and eye masks in constant daylight, a monthly self-check, and a named person in the party to talk to.
8. Evacuation and communication: a written plan with satellite messaging where it reaches, the seasonal evacuation windows, and a medical kit, medicine supply and medical summary sized for a year without resupply.
9. Dental examination and cleaning, audiometry, and an eye examination with spare glasses and glacier glasses before departure.
