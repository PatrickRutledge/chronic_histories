Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Robert Falcon Scott was born on June 6, 1868, at Plymouth, Devon, into a seafaring family, and joined his first Royal Navy ship when he was 13. [D]
  • He led the Discovery expedition to the Antarctic from 1901 to 1904. [D]
  • In October 1902, at the Discovery's winter quarters, scurvy was found after a sledge party returned from the western journey; the expedition's surgeon then examined everyone on board and found signs in a good many men, in most only the merest indication, and the outbreak was over by about October 20. Scott arranged to replace part of the sledging pemmican with cooked seal meat. [D]
  • On the southern sledge journey (November 1902 to February 1903) the party examined gums and legs every Sunday. When one companion developed angry-looking gums in late December 1902, they stopped eating bacon and increased the seal-meat ration, and Scott recorded that the scurvy symptoms lessened. [D]
  • At a thorough medical examination on the southern journey on January 14, 1903, Scott recorded that he himself had "distinctly red gums, and a very slight swelling in the ankles." Two days later, on a double ration of seal meat, his gums were much cleaner and his leg vastly improved; by the last days of the journey he wrote that their scurvy had been advancing again and that his ankles were considerably swollen. [D]
  • After the southern party returned to the ship in February 1903, Scott wrote that he had both legs much swollen and very uncomfortable gums, and an extraordinary lassitude, physical and mental, that lasted many weeks before he returned to a normally vigorous condition. [D]
  • In his account of the Discovery expedition he wrote that scurvy was then believed to be ptomaine poisoning from tainted meat. A modern historian of medicine notes that many British doctors of the time accepted this view and overlooked the experiments of Holst and Frølich on dietary deficiency, so medical advice given to him before his second expedition would probably not have helped him prevent scurvy. [R]
  • On the southern journey, on December 20, 1902, he wrote that his tobacco supply was so low that he had been trying tea leaves, which were "nothing less than horrid." [D]
  • After the Discovery expedition the needs of the naval service called him to the command of battleships and to confidential work at the Admiralty, and five years passed before he resumed Antarctic work. In 1908 he married the sculptor Kathleen Bruce; they had one son. [D]
  • On November 24, 1909, he wrote from the offices of the British Antarctic Expedition at 36 and 38 Victoria Street, London, that the Pole was at the forefront of the campaign for raising funds but that scientific work would not be neglected. [D]
