# Teaching Note — Emily Dickinson pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Emily Dickinson was born on December 10, 1830, in the family Homestead on Main Street in Amherst, Massachusetts. In the late winter of 1848 she was removed from Mount Holyoke Female Seminary for about a month because of lung congestion and a cough. In 1851 she had symptoms then thought to be consumptive, consulted local and Boston doctors, and for two years took a glycerine prescription from a Boston tuberculosis specialist until the symptoms apparently subsided. By her own account an eye affliction began in the fall of 1863; in February 1864 she consulted the Boston ophthalmologist Henry Willard Williams, lived in Cambridgeport from April to December 1864 to be near him for treatment, and repeated the treatment from April to October 1865, after which she was apparently cured. The treatment required confinement in dim light, no reading, and writing only in pencil; she called her first stay in Cambridge eight months of Siberia. Her withdrawal from normal social situations began in her mid-twenties; in later years she rarely went beyond the family Homestead and spoke with some visitors from behind a darkened door rather than face to face. Her death came after two and a half years of ill health that followed a death in her family in October 1883 and a consequent nervous prostration; she later had two blackouts and was confined to bed for the seven months before her death. Her letters mention severe headache and nausea, and her last illness ended in a coma with raspy, difficult breathing. Her reclusiveness hampered her Amherst physician, because she would not admit him to her bedside to take a pulse. She died in Amherst on May 15, 1886, aged 55; her physician entered Bright's disease as the cause on her death certificate, at the time a general diagnosis that included hypertensive symptoms as well as nephritis.
- **R:** No record of the eye diagnosis survives. Her main symptoms were sensitivity to light, aching eyes, and a restricted ability to do close work; iritis is considered the most likely explanation, and exotropia, a psychiatric cause, and hysterical hyperaesthesia of the retina have also been proposed. Scholars disagree whether her withdrawal was a deliberate artistic choice or had a medical origin, such as an anxiety condition. A 2013 review by Hirschhorn and Longsworth rejects a proposed diagnosis of epilepsy on scientific, clinical, and biographical grounds. Researchers have concluded that she died of heart failure caused by severe hypertension; no documentary evidence records the yellow pallor and odor associated with nephritis.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
