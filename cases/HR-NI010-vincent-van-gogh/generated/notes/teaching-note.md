# Teaching Note — Vincent van Gogh pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Vincent van Gogh was born on March 30, 1853, and decided to become an artist at the age of 27. In October 1888 the painter Paul Gauguin joined Van Gogh at the Yellow House in Arles, in the south of France, where Van Gogh hoped to found an artists' community; their discussions about art became more frequent and heated, and the situation deteriorated. On December 23, 1888, when he was 35, after a heated argument, Van Gogh cut off his left ear in a state of utter confusion. The police found him at home the next morning and took him to hospital. He could later recall nothing of what had happened. In the hospital in Arles he was treated by the assistant physician Dr. Felix Rey. His condition worsened and he spent several days in isolation; he then recovered slowly and went home after about two weeks, on January 7, 1889. New attacks followed about a month later. He was admitted to the hospital in Arles again on February 7, 1889, with delirium and hallucinations, and again on February 27, 1889, after neighbours petitioned for him to be confined. On May 8, 1889, he had himself admitted voluntarily to the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole psychiatric hospital in Saint-Remy-de-Provence, where he stayed for a year. The director, Dr. Peyron, entered his diagnosis in the admissions register; Nolen and colleagues report that Peyron described attacks of epilepsy separated by long intervals. In a letter to his brother Theo written at the asylum about May 23, 1889, he wrote that after the shock nothing would have been so agreeable to him as never to wake up again, that this horror of life was already less pronounced and the melancholy less acute, and that other patients, like him, had heard sounds and strange voices during their crises. Further crises began in July 1889, while he was painting a quarry, and the following months alternated between crises and recovery; Nolen and colleagues count four episodes at Saint-Remy between July 1889 and April 1890. He drank heavily in Paris from 1886, probably both absinthe and wine, and later wrote to his brother that he had become almost an alcoholic from overdoing it; in Arles he seems to have cut down. He left Saint-Remy in May 1890 for Auvers-sur-Oise, near Paris, where Dr. Paul Gachet was asked to keep an eye on him. He was exceptionally productive there, but felt lonely and depressed. On July 27, 1890, he shot himself in the chest; he died in Auvers-sur-Oise on July 29, 1890, at the age of 37.
- **R:** Nolen and colleagues judged that he had an alcohol use disorder, with dependence shown by failed attempts to cut down in Arles, made worse by malnutrition and poor sleep. Nolen and colleagues concluded that he probably had a bipolar mood disorder, more likely bipolar II than bipolar I, with recurrent depressive episodes, some of them psychotic, and probably hypomanic symptoms. The Van Gogh Museum states that no all-embracing diagnosis has been found. Nolen and colleagues also found strong indications of borderline personality traits, present as an underlying vulnerability from young adulthood. Nolen and colleagues proposed that the brief psychosis after the ear incident was a delirium from alcohol withdrawal, because he was forced to stop drinking suddenly when he was admitted to hospital. The writer Martin Bailey questioned part of their reasoning, arguing that they underestimated how much he went on drinking afterwards. Nolen and colleagues concluded that focal (temporal lobe) epilepsy, which Dr. Rey had suggested in Arles, cannot be excluded as an additional condition, although its probability cannot be judged without an EEG or brain imaging. Schizophrenia, Meniere's disease, syphilis, acute intermittent porphyria and thujone poisoning from absinthe have also been proposed; Nolen and colleagues judged schizophrenia and the others unlikely or highly unlikely, because he had no psychotic symptoms between episodes and no negative symptoms.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
