History of Present Illness

Mr. van Gogh, a 35-year-old painter, is seen at the request of Dr. Verhoeven four weeks after aripiprazole 10 mg was started in hospital. He has taken every dose. The confusion of the acute episode has cleared, and he hears nothing by day, but on some nights he hears his name called as he falls asleep and feels briefly that people in the street are talking about him. In the weeks before the episode he slept three or four hours a night, painted almost continuously, and felt full of energy and plans; he was drinking five or six drinks a day, and the confusion began soon after he stopped drinking abruptly. Since discharge his mood has been low and he feels ashamed of what he cannot remember; passive thoughts that it would be easier not to wake up are becoming less frequent, and he has no intent or plan. He drinks two or three glasses of wine most evenings. He has never had a seizure, lost time, or had spells of odd smells or sudden fear. He had his brain MRI and EEG this morning. He wants to understand what happened and to paint again. [S]

Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Vincent van Gogh was born on March 30, 1853, and decided to become an artist at the age of 27. [D]
  • In October 1888 the painter Paul Gauguin joined Van Gogh at the Yellow House in Arles, in the south of France, where Van Gogh hoped to found an artists' community; their discussions about art became more frequent and heated, and the situation deteriorated. [D]
  • On December 23, 1888, when he was 35, after a heated argument, Van Gogh cut off his left ear in a state of utter confusion. The police found him at home the next morning and took him to hospital. He could later recall nothing of what had happened. [D]
  • In the hospital in Arles he was treated by the assistant physician Dr. Felix Rey. His condition worsened and he spent several days in isolation; he then recovered slowly and went home after about two weeks, on January 7, 1889. [D]
  • He drank heavily in Paris from 1886, probably both absinthe and wine, and later wrote to his brother that he had become almost an alcoholic from overdoing it; in Arles he seems to have cut down. [D]
  • Nolen and colleagues judged that he had an alcohol use disorder, with dependence shown by failed attempts to cut down in Arles, made worse by malnutrition and poor sleep. [R]
  • Nolen and colleagues concluded that he probably had a bipolar mood disorder, more likely bipolar II than bipolar I, with recurrent depressive episodes, some of them psychotic, and probably hypomanic symptoms. The Van Gogh Museum states that no all-embracing diagnosis has been found. [R]
  • Nolen and colleagues also found strong indications of borderline personality traits, present as an underlying vulnerability from young adulthood. [R]
  • Nolen and colleagues proposed that the brief psychosis after the ear incident was a delirium from alcohol withdrawal, because he was forced to stop drinking suddenly when he was admitted to hospital. The writer Martin Bailey questioned part of their reasoning, arguing that they underestimated how much he went on drinking afterwards. [R]
  • Nolen and colleagues concluded that focal (temporal lobe) epilepsy, which Dr. Rey had suggested in Arles, cannot be excluded as an additional condition, although its probability cannot be judged without an EEG or brain imaging. [R]
  • Schizophrenia, Meniere's disease, syphilis, acute intermittent porphyria and thujone poisoning from absinthe have also been proposed; Nolen and colleagues judged schizophrenia and the others unlikely or highly unlikely, because he had no psychotic symptoms between episodes and no negative symptoms. [R]

Assessment

  • First episode of psychosis with mood features, provisional diagnosis (F29). The leading possibilities are bipolar I disorder with psychotic features, suggested by weeks of reduced need for sleep and increased activity before the episode and low mood after it, and a psychosis caused by heavy drinking and its abrupt withdrawal, suggested by the timing; the two may have combined. A brief psychotic or schizophreniform disorder remains possible; schizophrenia is less likely with this course. Focal epilepsy is unlikely without seizure-like events but is reasonable to look for once. The diagnosis should stay provisional and be settled by the course: a later manic or depressive episode would point to bipolar disorder, and psychosis without mood change or alcohol to a schizophrenia-spectrum disorder. Aripiprazole is a sound choice for its low metabolic and prolactin effects; the residual night-time symptoms justify a modest increase. A mood stabilizer is not needed now, because aripiprazole also treats mania; lithium would be the first choice if a mood episode occurs. [S]
  • Alcohol use disorder, moderate (F10.20). Drinking is the most changeable risk for relapse and for suicide. He takes no opioids, and his liver tests are well below three times the upper limit, so naltrexone is suitable. [S]
  • Self-injury during confusion (Z91.52). The injury happened in confusion and without suicidal intent; his current passive thoughts of not waking up are a separate and treatable part of his low mood. His risk is raised in the months after a first episode, and the safety plan is appropriate. [S]

Plan of Treatment

  • Increase aripiprazole to 15 mg once daily; if akathisia appears, go back to 10 mg rather than add more medicines, and treat it with propranolol if needed. [S]
  • No mood stabilizer now; consider lithium if a manic or depressive episode occurs. [S]
  • Complete the brain MRI and sleep-deprived EEG; if both are normal and there are no seizure-like events, no further epilepsy testing is needed. [S]
  • Start naltrexone 50 mg daily, continue thiamine, follow drinking with PEth, and treat alcohol and psychosis together in the same team. [S]
  • Enroll him in coordinated specialty care now: care coordination, individual resilience-focused therapy, education for him and the people he chooses, supported return to work, and peer support. [S]
  • Monitor weight at every visit for six months, blood pressure, fasting glucose, A1c and lipids at about 12 weeks and then yearly, akathisia and parkinsonism at each visit early on, and the AIMS every six months. [S]
