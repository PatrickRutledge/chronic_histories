Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Recent-death rule. Van Gogh died in 1890, so the rule does not apply. Every historical claim is limited to what the fetched sources state; mental-health, self-harm, and alcohol claims are marked sensitive.
  2. Index age and dates. The index visit on January 19, 2026 maps to January 19, 1889, when he was 35, twelve days after his discharge from the hospital in Arles on January 7, 1889. The synthetic date of birth is March 30, 1990 (shift 137 years). The chronic-care visits on February 2, March 2, April 20, and July 20, 2026, the sick visit on February 16, 2026, and the preventive visit on June 15, 2026 map to the same days of 1889. The antipsychotic and thiamine start on January 5, 2026 (true frame January 5, 1889, during the Arles admission) stands for the inpatient start. In the true frame these outpatient dates overlap his readmissions in Arles in February and his stay at Saint-Rémy from May 1889; the synthetic visits are a modern teaching episode, not a claim about where he was, and the true-date distribution is labeled as anachronistic. Past medical history includes only events before the index date.
  3. Letters edition. The Van Gogh Museum’s letters edition (vangoghletters.org) renders its letters only with JavaScript, so the text of letter 776 could not be fetched or read. His letter of about May 23, 1889 is therefore quoted only as the Van Gogh Museum quotes it (SRC-0001), and his “almost an alcoholic” letter only as The Art Newspaper quotes it (SRC-0006). No other words of his are quoted.
  4. Diagnosis. The asylum physician’s diagnosis is recorded as a documented fact (CLM-0006, D); whether he had epilepsy is a retrospective question (CLM-0014, R, possible). Bipolar disorder (possible), borderline personality traits (possible), alcohol use disorder (probable), alcohol withdrawal delirium as the cause of the first psychosis (disputed, with The Art Newspaper’s critique linked as disputing), and schizophrenia and the other proposed causes (disputed) are all R and stay off the problem list. The roster’s “probable bipolar I” and “absinthe toxicity” are not used: Nolen and colleagues favor bipolar II and reject thujone poisoning.
  5. Problems. The modern first episode is coded F29 (unspecified psychosis not due to a substance or known physiological condition) with verification provisional for the whole episode, and its display states the differential: bipolar I disorder with psychotic features, brief psychotic or schizophreniform disorder, alcohol withdrawal delirium or alcohol-induced psychosis, and focal epilepsy. At six months the record explains how the label would be settled (a mood episode would point to bipolar disorder; psychosis without mood change or alcohol to a schizophrenia-spectrum disorder) rather than resolving it early. Alcohol use disorder is F10.20, and the ear injury is recorded as Z91.52 (personal history of nonsuicidal self-harm) because it happened in confusion. Akathisia (G25.71) is added at the sick visit and resolved at the six-week visit. The F-codes and the PHQ-9 of 13 trigger the structured safety assessment.
  6. Self-harm and death. The ear injury is stated once, as the Van Gogh Museum states it, in CLM-0003; the modern record calls it self-injury to the left ear during confusion and gives no further detail. His death by a gunshot wound is recorded in CLM-0017 as history only, without detail, and is not used in the modern episode. The modern passive death wish (PHQ-9 item 9 scored 1, without intent or plan, gone by six weeks) is anchored to his own letter (CLM-0007). The safety plan gives the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline and lethal-means counseling (no firearm access, medicines dispensed two weeks at a time, alcohol out of the house while low), and safety is reviewed at every later visit, including the sick visit, because akathisia raises distress and suicide risk.
  7. Real people. Paul Gauguin, Dr. Félix Rey, Dr. Peyron, Dr. Paul Gachet, his brother Theo, and Martin Bailey appear only in the claims. No modern decision, statement, or health fact is attributed to any real person: the modern episode has a friend he paints with and a fellow painter who left after an argument, neither named, and no relative takes any part. No family history is recorded because no fetched source gives his relatives’ health before the index date. His roster nationality is recorded in the identity as “Netherlands,” because the dataset’s cross-case name check reserves the word for another record’s alias.
  8. Synthetic content. All visits, laboratory values, vital signs, PHQ-9 and AUDIT-C scores, AIMS and Barnes ratings, waist measurements, the MRI and EEG results, the medicines and doses, his pipe smoking, his friend and the coordinated specialty care team, and his height (170 cm) are synthetic. Qualitative results (urine drug test, HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis C tests, MRI, and EEG) appear only in the note text.
  9. Preventive visit. A periodic adult preventive visit (CPT 99395 with 99497 in plain text) was added at age 36. Judgment calls: PCV20 because of the alcohol use disorder; hepatitis B vaccination for an adult under 60 with no record; tobacco cessation recorded as declined; sun protection and studio solvent ventilation advised for an outdoor painter, with no test for any exposure; and advance care planning as a psychiatric advance directive recording only his own general preferences, with an unnamed agent.
  10. Sick visit. A same-day visit for moderate akathisia two weeks after aripiprazole was raised from 10 to 15 mg. It teaches recognizing akathisia, distinguishing it from relapse, returning to the lower dose, short-term propranolol (no asthma, adequate pulse), avoiding a benzodiazepine because of his alcohol use, and reviewing safety. It implies no real diagnosis.
  11. Focused visits. The four chronic-care follow-ups (two weeks with the consultation, six weeks, three months, and six months) are documented as focused visits on the APA 2020 schizophrenia guideline, the ADA/APA consensus, the CMID depression and bipolar monograph, the CANMAT and ISBD guidance for sleep and the mood features, the VA/DoD alcohol guideline, and the VA/DoD suicide-risk guideline. Deviations recorded instead of invented results: the baseline waist was measured at two weeks instead of at intake; the 12-week weight and metabolic check fell at 15 weeks (within the APA’s four months, after the CMID’s 12 weeks); and no PHQ-9 was taken in the seven weeks between the six-week and three-month visits, longer than the monthly measure advised in acute treatment, with no harm (scores fell from 8 to 6) and monthly care-coordinator PHQ-9 calls added. The baseline AIMS was done with the consultation at two weeks.
  12. Consultation. The psychiatry referral and the consultation by Jonah Feld, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the two-week visit, are S. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: aripiprazole raised to 15 mg with advice to return to 10 mg if akathisia appeared (which happened), no mood stabilizer unless a mood episode occurs, MRI and EEG sufficient if normal (they were), naltrexone started that day, coordinated specialty care, the monitoring schedule (including the PHQ-9 cadence the record then missed once), and continued treatment with a review near one year.
  13. Not used. The letters themselves (see above), the asylum register, Peyron’s and Rey’s own writings, biographies, and accounts of the ear incident beyond the museum’s are not used. Accounts of his death beyond the museum’s are not used.

CLM-0003DOn December 23, 1888, when he was 35, after a heated argument, Van Gogh cut off his left ear in a state of utter confusion. The police found him at home the next morning and took him to hospital. He could later recall nothing of what had happened.
CLM-0004DIn the hospital in Arles he was treated by the assistant physician Dr. Felix Rey. His condition worsened and he spent several days in isolation; he then recovered slowly and went home after about two weeks, on January 7, 1889.
CLM-0005DNew attacks followed about a month later. He was admitted to the hospital in Arles again on February 7, 1889, with delirium and hallucinations, and again on February 27, 1889, after neighbours petitioned for him to be confined.
CLM-0006DOn May 8, 1889, he had himself admitted voluntarily to the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole psychiatric hospital in Saint-Remy-de-Provence, where he stayed for a year. The director, Dr. Peyron, entered his diagnosis in the admissions register; Nolen and colleagues report that Peyron described attacks of epilepsy separated by long intervals.
CLM-0007DIn a letter to his brother Theo written at the asylum about May 23, 1889, he wrote that after the shock nothing would have been so agreeable to him as never to wake up again, that this horror of life was already less pronounced and the melancholy less acute, and that other patients, like him, had heard sounds and strange voices during their crises.
CLM-0008DFurther crises began in July 1889, while he was painting a quarry, and the following months alternated between crises and recovery; Nolen and colleagues count four episodes at Saint-Remy between July 1889 and April 1890.
CLM-0009DHe drank heavily in Paris from 1886, probably both absinthe and wine, and later wrote to his brother that he had become almost an alcoholic from overdoing it; in Arles he seems to have cut down.
