Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Thomas Jefferson was born at Shadwell, in what was then Goochland County, Virginia, on April 13, 1743 (April 2, 1743, in the Old Style calendar then in use). [D]
  • At 20, in a letter of March 20, 1764, written at Williamsburg late at night, he wrote of "a violent head ach, with which I have been afflicted these two days." [D]
  • In 1776 an illness kept him at home in Virginia for about six weeks longer than he intended; a correspondent wrote that his time at home had been interrupted by "an inveterate head ach." [D]
  • His wife died on September 6, 1782, of complications after childbirth. In November 1782 he wrote that he was only "a little emerging from that stupor of mind which had rendered me as dead to the world as she was whose loss occasioned it." [D]
  • In March 1784, at Annapolis as a delegate to Congress, he wrote that he had "lately added an attack of my periodical headach" to his habitual ill health and had to avoid reading, writing and almost thinking; his letters thereafter call the attacks his "periodical head-ach." [D]
  • In September 1786, in Paris, he dislocated his right wrist in a fall; the bones were set, but not well. He wrote with his left hand for a time, and in December 1788 wrote that the joint "had never been replaced," that he had lost the use of the hand "except that I can write," and that "a withered hand and swelled and crooked fingers" remained. [D]
  • After five years in France as a diplomat, he took office as the first Secretary of State in New York City on March 21, 1790. [D]
  • In New York, a periodical headache "came on me the 1st. of May" 1790, and the first week was violent; his account books record purchases of Peruvian bark (cinchona) on May 1 and May 3. [D]

12 later historical claims are omitted because they describe events after the index age. See the teaching note.
