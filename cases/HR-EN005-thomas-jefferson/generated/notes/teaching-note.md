# Teaching Note — Thomas Jefferson pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Thomas Jefferson was born at Shadwell, in what was then Goochland County, Virginia, on April 13, 1743 (April 2, 1743, in the Old Style calendar then in use). He died at Monticello on July 4, 1826, at 83. At 20, in a letter of March 20, 1764, written at Williamsburg late at night, he wrote of "a violent head ach, with which I have been afflicted these two days." In 1776 an illness kept him at home in Virginia for about six weeks longer than he intended; a correspondent wrote that his time at home had been interrupted by "an inveterate head ach." His wife died on September 6, 1782, of complications after childbirth. In November 1782 he wrote that he was only "a little emerging from that stupor of mind which had rendered me as dead to the world as she was whose loss occasioned it." In March 1784, at Annapolis as a delegate to Congress, he wrote that he had "lately added an attack of my periodical headach" to his habitual ill health and had to avoid reading, writing and almost thinking; his letters thereafter call the attacks his "periodical head-ach." In September 1786, in Paris, he dislocated his right wrist in a fall; the bones were set, but not well. He wrote with his left hand for a time, and in December 1788 wrote that the joint "had never been replaced," that he had lost the use of the hand "except that I can write," and that "a withered hand and swelled and crooked fingers" remained. After five years in France as a diplomat, he took office as the first Secretary of State in New York City on March 21, 1790. In New York, a periodical headache "came on me the 1st. of May" 1790, and the first week was violent; his account books record purchases of Peruvian bark (cinchona) on May 1 and May 3. The 1790 attack eased after the first week but lingered: on May 23 "a lingering head-ach" still kept him from business, and on June 13 he wrote that he had been able to do business for the past ten days and that it was "the first time the bark had ever failed to remove my complaint very speedily." During the 1790 attack a Baltimore physician wrote to him recommending a remedy containing opium for use when the bark failed; on June 13 he replied that the letter found him "so far recovered as to have no further occasion for medecine." In June 1791 he wrote that a journey had freed him "from the almost constant headach with which I had been persecuted thro the whole winter and spring," and that being clear of it while travelling proved it had been "occasioned by the drudgery of business." In March 1807, as President, he described daily "fits" of periodical headache lasting from about nine or ten in the morning until dark, shortening from nine hours to five, during which he was "shut up in a dark room"; the last attack recorded in his letters was in March and April 1808. In 1819 he wrote that "a periodical head ach has afflicted me occasionally, once perhaps in 6. or 8. years, for 2 or 3 weeks at a time, which seems now to have left me," and that "a stiff wrist, the consequence of an early dislocation, makes writing both slow and painful." In retirement he was increasingly troubled by rheumatism, and diarrhea troubled him through the last 25 years of his life; in 1825 he had "a difficulty of making water," which his physician attributed to an enlarged prostate obstructing the flow of urine. In his final years his physician prescribed laudanum, a tincture of opium, for diarrhea, and he accepted its regular use because it allowed him to resume his activities. He enslaved more than 600 people over the course of his life; Monticello gives the number as more than 610, of whom about 400 lived in bondage at Monticello.
- **R:** The nature of the headaches is disputed. Biographers since about 1930 have usually called them migraine, and his principal biographer called them "probably migraine"; a 1984 review argued for tension-type headaches; a 2006 review in Headache concluded that they probably were migraines, although not all current diagnostic criteria can be met; cluster headache has also been proposed. A 2008 medical biography states that he intermittently experienced anxiety, depression and insomnia. His exact cause of death was never conclusively determined; a Monticello account suggests a combination of exhaustion from severe diarrhea, kidney infection and damage, and pneumonia.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
