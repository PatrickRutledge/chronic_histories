Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Sources. The case relies on his own letters on Founders Online, the Thomas Jefferson Foundation's encyclopedia, slavery and timeline pages, Encyclopedia Virginia, and the full text of Battle (1984). For the Headache 2006 review and the 2008 medical biography only the PubMed abstracts were read. A 2003 Cephalalgia note and a 2006 letter in Headache on his headaches were found in PubMed without abstracts and are not cited. Popular health sites found in searches were not used.
  2. Birth date and calendar. He was born on April 2, 1743, in the Old Style (Julian) calendar then used in Virginia; the date was later adjusted to April 13, the same day in the Gregorian calendar. The preferred date is April 13, 1743, and the Old Style date is recorded as a superseded alternative. The synthetic date of birth keeps April 13.
  3. Index age and dates. The index visit on May 7, 2026 maps to May 7, 1790, the seventh day of the headache that “came on me the 1st. of May,” when he was 47 and had been Secretary of State in New York since March 21. The synthetic date of birth is April 13, 1979 (shift 236 years). The visits on May 21, June 8, June 18, July 16 and August 13, 2026 map to the same days of 1790; he lived in New York from late March to early September 1790 (less than six months in residence), so every visit falls there. The synthetic bout eases over the same weeks as the documented one, which he described as violent for a week and lingering into June. Past medical history includes only events up to September 1786; historical claims about later events are linked to later timeline events and are left out of the intake documents.
  4. Migraine (CLM-0013, R, disputed). He called the attacks his “periodical head-ach” and never used the word migraine. Biographers have usually called them migraine; Battle (1984) argued for tension-type headache; Cohen and Rolak (2006) concluded they were probably migraines though not all current criteria can be met; cluster headache has also been proposed. The modern migraine diagnosis is synthetic and is made from a synthetic diary; it is not a claim about his real diagnosis. His letters record no aura, vomiting or one-sided pain, and the synthetic diary gives none of these either, beyond nausea.
  5. Presenting problem. Recurrent disabling headaches (R51.9) is the intake problem; it ends at the two-week visit, when the consultant confirms migraine without aura (G43.009), recorded on that date. Intake documents name migraine only as a possibility to be confirmed.
  6. Wrist. Contemporary letters, including his own, call the 1786 injury a dislocation; his daughter's later recollection calls it a compound fracture. The case follows the contemporary word, dislocation, and records the conflict here. The roster's “broken right wrist” is therefore described as a dislocation that healed out of place. The 1822 injury to the left wrist is not used.
  7. Laudanum and opium. The roster's laudanum lead is recorded only as documented: in 1790 a physician sent an opium remedy after he had recovered, and he did not need it (CLM-0009); in his final years laudanum was prescribed for diarrhea and he accepted regular use (CLM-0016). Both are marked sensitive. No opioid use is recorded in the modern episode, which uses the 1790 letter for the teaching point that opioids are not used for migraine.
  8. Depression and grief. His grief after his wife's death in 1782 is documented in his own words (CLM-0005, D, sensitive). The roster's depression lead rests on a 2008 medical biography's statement of intermittent anxiety, depression and insomnia (CLM-0014, R, possible, sensitive); no mood diagnosis is made. The modern PHQ-9 and GAD-7 are low, so no safety assessment is required.
  9. Slavery. He enslaved more than 600 people over his lifetime; Monticello gives more than 610, about 400 of them at Monticello (CLM-0018, D). This is recorded plainly as a documented historical fact. It has no clinical role in the modern episode, no enslaved person is named, and no health fact about any enslaved person is recorded. No member of the Hemings family is named or given any role. Because the figure covers his whole life, the claim is linked to the timeline at his death and is not shown in the intake documents.
  10. Roster leads not used. The roster lists dental disease, “probable late-onset diabetes” and financial ruin in old age. No source read documents dental disease or diabetes beyond a single word in one abstract, so neither is a claim or a modern diagnosis; financial ruin is not a health matter for this episode and is not used.
  11. Relatives. No relative is named in the modern episode or given a decision, statement or health fact. His wife's death appears only as a dated historical fact and as the word widower in the social history; his daughters appear only as two daughters in Virginia. No family history is recorded, since the only headache history in a relative comes from family letters about his daughter and would be a health fact about a real relative.
  12. Instruments. The MIDAS has no LOINC total score code on the terminology server; it is recorded as its five items with the PhenX LOINC codes for the same questions (unit days), and the total is given in the notes. The HIT-6 has no LOINC code found and is recorded as narrative. The SNNOOP10 red-flag screen is narrative.
  13. Acute treatment guideline. The AAN/AHS 2019 guideline on acute migraine treatment is for children and adolescents. For adults the case uses the AHS 2019 position statement, the 2021 AHS consensus statement and the 2025 ACP guideline on acute treatment.
  14. Synthetic clinical picture. Everything in the modern episode is synthetic: the diary, the attack counts, every score (MIDAS items, HIT-6, PHQ-9, PHQ-2, GAD-7, AUDIT-C), the lipid panel, the vital signs, the medicines and the outcomes. Height (189 cm) and weights are synthetic. The over-the-counter combination analgesic used before intake is mentioned in the notes and the referral but not coded as a medication.
  15. Clinical judgment calls. (1) No neuroimaging: a pattern stable for more than twenty years, a normal examination and no red flags. (2) Naproxen and metoclopramide at intake, before the diagnosis was confirmed; sumatriptan added at the consultation after a cardiovascular screen. (3) Prevention started even though his attacks come in bouts, because the bout rate in the synthetic history gives 13 migraine days in four weeks and MIDAS grade IV. (4) Propranolol was chosen over topiramate and amitriptyline because of their effects on concentration and morning alertness, and over a CGRP monoclonal antibody by his own choice after the AHS 2024 and ACP 2025 positions were explained; the antibody is the documented next step. (5) Propranolol raised to 120 mg at four weeks. (6) Hand therapy, a splint and dictation for the wrist, written as an occupational therapy visit so that the headache consultation is the case's single referral step.

  18. Focused visits. The three chronic-care follow-ups (two weeks with the consultation, six weeks, three months) are documented as focused visits on ICHD-3, the 2021 AHS consensus and its 2024 position statement, the AHS 2019 position statement, the 2025 ACP acute and preventive guidelines, the AHS Choosing Wisely list and the CMID depression monograph.
