## Historical evidence

1. National Park Service, Clara Barton National Historic Site. “Clara Barton Chronology,” 1821–1860, 1861–1869, and 1870–1912 (chron1, chron2, and chron3). Supports her birth as Clarissa Harlowe Barton on December 25, 1821, in North Oxford, Massachusetts, the youngest of five children; her mother's death on July 18, 1851, and her father's on March 21, 1862; her battlefield work from 1861, including Antietam in September 1862, after which “Miss Barton was weakened by typhoid fever”; the office for missing soldiers from March 1865 to 1869 (63,182 letters, 22,000 men identified); the loss of her voice in December 1868 “from fatigue and mental prostration”; her travel to Europe in September 1869 “on the advice of her doctor … to regain her health”; Franco-Prussian War relief in 1870 and 1871; nervous exhaustion and temporary loss of eyesight in 1872 and 1873; “nervous strain” after her return in October 1873, worsened by her sister's death on May 24, 1874; her move to Dansville in 1876, “first to a sanitarium and later to her own home”; the founding of the American Association of the Red Cross on May 21, 1881; her resignation on May 14, 1904; and her death at her Glen Echo home on April 12, 1912, at 90. https://www.nps.gov/clba/learn/kidsyouth/chron1.htm, https://www.nps.gov/clba/learn/kidsyouth/chron2.htm, and https://www.nps.gov/clba/learn/kidsyouth/chron3.htm
2. HISTMED Master Roster v3. Internal project source. Its clinical claims were provisional and were not treated as independent corroboration.
3. Barton WE. *The Life of Clara Barton, Founder of the American Red Cross*, Volume 1. Boston: Houghton Mifflin; 1922. Project Gutenberg eBook 67505. Quotes her diary for “a brief period of depression” in the spring of 1852 while she was teaching in New Jersey, which the author judged did not last long, and her own account of the eve of Antietam: “I was faint, but could not eat; weary, but could not sleep; depressed, but could not weep.” https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/67505
4. Barton WE. *The Life of Clara Barton*, Volume 2 (1922). Project Gutenberg eBook 67954. Quotes her lecture on the voice loss of 1868 (“Nervous prostration had declared itself. I went to my home in Washington, lay helpless all winter”); her letters describing the cough and eight months in bed in England from October 1872, the breakdown “with ‘prostration of the nervous system’” after her return, deepened by her sister's illness and death, and that she “averaged less than two hours' sleep in twenty-four for almost a year” and “could not write my name for over four months”; her description of Dansville as “the largest and most noted water and rest cure in the country”; the author's statement that “she had periods of depression and times of weakness, but she gained strength”; and her double pneumonia in the winter of 1910 to 1911, from which she recovered, and again in the winter before her death. https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/67954
5. Spiegel AD. “The role of gender, phrenology, discrimination and nervous prostration in Clara Barton's career.” *J Community Health* 1995;20(6):501–526. PMID 8568024. The abstract describes therapy for her long-term nervous prostration that changed her behavioral approach to illness and to life, and her death at 90 in Glen Echo on April 12, 1912. Only the abstract was read. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8568024/
6. Henneberger M. “Clara Barton's inner war.” *Bangor Daily News*, April 7, 2012. Quotes her biographer Elizabeth Brown Pryor on a crippling low mood “lifted finally by her only true remedy — a need for her services.” https://www.bangordailynews.com/2012/04/07/living/clara-bartons-inner-war/

## Editorial resolutions

- **Index age.** The episode presents her at 52, after her documented return home in October 1873 from war relief abroad, when she broke down with what she called prostration of the nervous system. The synthetic date of birth is December 25, 1973, so the whole-year shift is 152 years. The index visit is January 20, 2026 (true frame January 20, 1874). The chronic-care visits follow on February 3, March 3, April 14, and June 16, 2026; the sick visit is July 27, 2026; the preventive visit is October 13, 2026 (true frame February 3, March 3, April 14, June 16, July 27, and October 13, 1874). She lived to 1912, so all fall within her lifetime. The visit type is `preventive_adult` because she is under 65. Past medical history and family history include only events before January 1874; her sister's death in May 1874 stays in the claims and timeline and is not in the family history.
- **Diagnosis.** Her periods of depression and nervous prostration are documented in her own diary and letters (**D**). Calling them major depressive disorder is a retrospective interpretation (**R**, possible); no diagnosis was made in her lifetime. The synthetic diagnosis, recurrent major depressive disorder (F33.1), is placed both_with_link to those claims. Burnout (Z73.0) is added as a synthetic contributing factor linked to her documented exhaustion after wartime relief; it is described as an occupational phenomenon, not a mental disorder. The F33 code requires the safety assessment, which is present.
- **Rest cures.** Her stays abroad on medical advice and at the Dansville sanitarium, which she called a water and rest cure, are recorded as history only (**D**). Claim CLM-0011 states that her recovery cannot be credited to the rest cure and that rest cures are not an effective treatment for depression. In the modern episode she asks whether she simply needs a long rest; the clinician and the consultant explain that prolonged rest or withdrawal from activity does not treat depression and that structured activity, psychotherapy, an antidepressant, sleep regularity, and social contact do. The two weeks of medical leave are a work-load measure inside an active treatment plan, not a rest cure.
- **Safe messaging.** The safety assessment is synthetic and records no current ideation, intent, or plan. PHQ-9 item 9 is scored 0 at every visit, and the safety plan and the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline are reviewed at every later visit, including the sick visit and the preventive visit. Diary passages in which she wrote of wishing for death, and a 2012 newspaper report that her diaries show she considered suicide, are not quoted, are not used as claims, and are not echoed in the synthetic episode. The 1852 diary episode is recorded only as “a brief period of depression.” Her death is stated without detail.
- **Family and other people.** The family history records only her parents' deaths, with causes not recorded. No statement or health fact is attributed to any relative, and the physicians who advised her are not named or quoted. The 1922 biography's author was her cousin; only his factual narration and his quotations of her own words are used. Her biographer Pryor is cited only for an interpretation (**R**).
- **Not used.** The Library of Congress collection pages and finding aid could not be fetched (bot challenge), so the diaries are cited through the 1922 biography. The Dansville library and historical society pages, which give conflicting dates for her arrival, were not used; the 1876 date follows the NPS chronology and her own letter. Pryor's biography was not read. Her replies to physicians' questionnaires about headaches, colds, back weakness, and digestive trouble, quoted in the biography, are not used. Her early caregiving for an injured brother and the later deaths of siblings are not used.
- **Sick and preventive visits.** The sick visit is an acute low back strain after lifting supply crates, chosen to teach the added bleeding risk of an NSAID with sertraline, staying active rather than resting, and a safety review during early remission. It does not echo a documented injury. The preventive visit adds negative screens, a lipid panel and HbA1c with a low cardiovascular risk, an ordered mammogram and colonoscopy (no results invented), influenza, PCV20, and zoster vaccines, and advance care planning that names no one.
- **Focused visits.** The four chronic-care follow-ups (two, six, twelve, and twenty-one weeks after starting sertraline) are documented as focused visits on the CMID depression monograph with the VA/DoD MDD and suicide-risk guidelines: PHQ-9 at every visit (18, 16, 12, 7, 4), suicide-risk review with the safety plan at every visit, the SSRI check at two weeks and after the dose increase (by nurse telephone call, reported at the twelve-week visit), adherence and adverse effects, the bipolar screen by psychiatric interview, psychotherapy, sleep, work hours, and social contact. Mood-stabilizer and antipsychotic monitoring is not indicated. One protocol deviation is recorded as `deferred`, with its outcome and corrective action, and no result was invented: the care-manager telephone PHQ-9 planned about four weeks after the twelve-week review was not completed, leaving nine weeks unmeasured before remission was confirmed. No Mood Disorder Questionnaire score is recorded; the psychiatric interview covers the bipolar screen.
- **Consultation.** The psychiatry referral and consultation (Rafael Mendes, MD, a fictional consultant, on the day of the two-week visit) are **S**. The consultant confirms recurrent major depression without bipolar, psychotic, or PTSD features, agrees that acute risk is low, and recommends what the record then shows: sertraline 50 mg increased to 100 mg at six weeks for a partial response, weekly CBT for about 16 sessions, a monthly PHQ-9 until remission (missed once, as recorded), safety review at every visit, continuation for at least twelve months after remission, a phased return from 25 to 45 hours with no deployment for six months and a peer support group, and the explanation that rest alone does not treat depression. The return criteria for psychiatry were not met.
- Barton died in 1912, so the recent-death review rule does not apply. Ordinary clinical, provenance, and safe-messaging reviews remain required.
