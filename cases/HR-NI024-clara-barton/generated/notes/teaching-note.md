# Teaching Note — Clara Barton pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Clarissa Harlowe Barton was born on December 25, 1821, in North Oxford, Massachusetts, the youngest of five children. Her diary records a brief period of depression in the spring of 1852, while she was teaching in New Jersey; her biographer judged that the mood did not last long. From 1861 she supplied and tended wounded soldiers at battlefields and field hospitals through the Civil War; after working for several days following the Battle of Antietam in September 1862 she was weakened by typhoid fever. In her account of the eve of the Battle of Antietam she wrote that she was faint but could not eat, weary but could not sleep, and depressed but could not weep. From March 1865 to 1869 she ran an office that answered 63,182 letters and identified 22,000 missing soldiers. In December 1868 she lost her voice while giving a lecture, from fatigue and mental prostration; in her own later account, nervous prostration had declared itself and she lay helpless all winter in Washington. In September 1869, on her doctor's advice, she travelled to Europe to regain her health. During the Franco-Prussian War of 1870 and 1871 she organized civilian relief in Strasbourg, Paris, and other French cities. In 1872 and 1873 she suffered nervous exhaustion and temporarily lost her eyesight; in a letter she wrote that she broke down with a cough in England in October 1872 and was confined to bed for eight months. After her return in October 1873 she broke down again with what she called prostration of the nervous system, deepened by her only sister's death in May 1874; she wrote that for almost a year she averaged less than two hours of sleep a day and could not write her name for over four months. In 1876 she moved to Dansville, New York, first living at a sanitarium that she called the largest and most noted water and rest cure in the country and later in her own home; she regained her strength gradually over several years, with periods of depression and weakness. The American Association of the Red Cross was formed on May 21, 1881, with her as president; she resigned the presidency on May 14, 1904. She died at her home in Glen Echo, Maryland, on April 12, 1912, aged 90, during a second illness with double pneumonia; she had recovered from an earlier attack the winter before. Her mother died on July 18, 1851, and her father on March 21, 1862.
- **R:** A 1995 review by Spiegel describes her long-term nervous prostration and argues that the therapy she received changed her behavioral approach to illness and to life. Her biographer Elizabeth Brown Pryor, as quoted in a 2012 newspaper article, describes a long struggle with depression that was lifted by a need for her services.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
