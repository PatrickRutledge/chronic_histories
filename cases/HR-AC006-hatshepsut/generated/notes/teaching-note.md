# Teaching Note — Hatshepsut pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Hatshepsut was a daughter of the king Thutmose I and became the chief queen of Thutmose II. After the death of Thutmose II she acted as guardian and regent for the young Thutmose III, her nephew and stepson; by his seventh regnal year she had taken the public role of a king, while still referred to with feminine forms. She claimed to have been born of a union between her mother and the god Amun-Ra, and looked to her father Thutmose I as the source of her kingship. Her dates follow different chronologies: The Metropolitan Museum of Art dates objects of her joint reign with Thutmose III to about 1473 to 1458 BCE, and the British Museum gives her dates as 1479 to 1457 BC. She died in the twenty-second regnal year of Thutmose III. No earlier than the forty-second year of Thutmose III, at least twenty years after her death, a program began to efface and recarve her monuments. In 2007 Zahi Hawass announced that KV60A, the mummy of an obese woman left on the floor of tomb KV60 in the Valley of the Kings, was Hatshepsut. The identification rested on CT scans, above all on a molar found in a small chest inscribed with her name from the royal cache DB320, which fitted a gap in KV60A's jaw where only one root of a molar remained.
- **R:** Her birth year is not recorded. An age of about 50 at death, suggested by study of the mummy identified as hers, would place her birth about 1507 BC; this is a rough estimate. The identification is debated: any link between these mummies and the inscribed chest is uncertain, the consensus cited is that the tooth probably broke after death rather than being extracted in life, the DNA results were preliminary, and identifications of royal mummies of this dynasty are often unclear. The CT scans of KV60A were reported to show a woman of about 50, about five feet tall and greatly overweight, with severe dental disease (missing teeth, root remnants, bone eroded at root tips by infection, and severe gum disease), and osteoarthritis and osteoporosis; a burst abscess with spreading infection was suggested as a cause of death. A lesion in the left iliac bone of KV60A was read by the radiologist Ashraf Selim as more likely a metastatic deposit from another cancer than a primary bone tumour, which could not be totally excluded. Diabetes has been proposed for KV60A on circumstantial grounds; the radiologist called it a theory that cannot be confirmed. In 2011 the University of Bonn reported that a flask in its Egyptian Museum inscribed with her name held the dried residue of a skin lotion or medication (palm oil, nutmeg apple oil, unsaturated fatty acids and hydrocarbons from creosote and asphalt), including benzo(a)pyrene, a strong carcinogen, and suggested that she may have exposed herself to it over years.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
