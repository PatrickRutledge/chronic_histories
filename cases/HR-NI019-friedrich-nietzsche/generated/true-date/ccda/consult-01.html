Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Friedrich Nietzsche was born on October 15, 1844, in Röcken, near Leipzig, where his father was a Lutheran minister. [D]
  • His father, a Lutheran pastor, died in 1849, when his son was four, after about a year of seizures and progressive neurological deficits; an autopsy described "softening" of the brain. Nietzsche later wrote that his father died "in his six-and-thirtieth year"; medical accounts give his age as 36. The cause was not established. [D]
  • He had severe myopia, worse in the right eye, and a right pupil larger than the left, both noted in early childhood; at about five an eye examination found that the right eye needed a very strong (six-dioptre) lens. By about 30 the right eye was functionally blind, and in 1878 an ophthalmologist found fluid in the right retina and diagnosed retinitis (central chorioretinitis). [D]
  • Severe headaches began in his school years; at nine he was already missing school because of them. They were mainly right-sided, frontal and above the eye, came with sensitivity to light and with nausea and vomiting, and grew more severe in adult life. [D]
  • In 1869, at 24, he was appointed to the chair of classical philology at Basel. Ill health led to sick leave in 1871 and again in 1876 to 1877, and in 1879 he resigned his professorship and received a small pension; he never held another post. [D]
  • Looking back in 1888, he wrote that in 1879, in his "six-and-thirtieth year," he reached "the lowest point in my vitality," when "my eyes could distinguish nothing that lay three paces away from me"; he resigned at Basel, and the next winter, his "first winter in Genoa," he wrote Dawn "in the midst of the agony of a headache which lasted three days, accompanied by violent nausea." [D]
  • After resigning he settled into a pattern of spending winters near the Mediterranean, usually in Italy, and summers in Sils Maria. After an unsuccessful spring taking the waters at Recoaro, he went to the Upper Engadine at the beginning of July 1881 and rented a modest room in Sils Maria, hoping the dry, sunny climate would relieve his headaches; he kept a strict daily schedule with five to seven hours of walking, filling notebooks as he went. [D]
  • By his own admission he had gonorrhea twice in his twenties; whether either infection was in fact syphilis could not have been told apart reliably at the time. [D]

Assessment

  • Chronic migraine without aura (G43.709). ICHD-3 criteria for chronic migraine are met: headache on 15 or more days a month for more than three months, with features of migraine without aura (throbbing, moderate to severe, worse with activity, with nausea, vomiting and light and noise sensitivity) on at least 8 days a month. The milder pressure days are part of the same disorder. Cluster headache and hemicrania continua do not fit: there are no tearing, redness or other autonomic signs, and the pain is not continuous. [S]
  • Medication overuse headache (G44.40). Present: a combination analgesic on 10 or more days a month for more than three months in a person with 15 or more headache days a month. Butalbital is a barbiturate and should be tapered rather than stopped at once; ibuprofen can be stopped now. [S]
  • Secondary causes (G43.709). The strongly right-sided pattern and the rising frequency justified imaging, and the MRI with contrast shows no structural lesion, including none behind the right eye. Nonreactive treponemal and nontreponemal tests exclude syphilis as a cause of the headaches or the pupils, and no lumbar puncture is needed. The unequal pupils, both briskly reactive and present since early childhood, are long-standing and benign, and the poor right eye is explained by myopic macular atrophy. His father's illness cannot be diagnosed from the record; nothing in his own history, examination or MRI suggests an inherited neurological disease, and no genetic testing is indicated. [S]
  • Preventive treatment (G43.709). Prevention is indicated for chronic migraine with severe disability. A CGRP monoclonal antibody is a first-line option under the 1879 American Headache Society position statement and the European Headache Federation guideline, which also supports starting it with withdrawal in medication overuse headache. Galcanezumab suits him: monthly self-injection travels well between his summer and winter homes, and it causes constipation less often than erenumab. Topiramate is a poor fit because it can slow word-finding and concentration and, rarely, cause acute myopia with angle-closure glaucoma. OnabotulinumtoxinA is effective in chronic migraine but needs injections every 12 weeks at a center; it is the next option if the antibody is not enough. [S]

Plan of Treatment

  • Stop ibuprofen now. Replace as-needed butalbital combination tablets with a scheduled taper: one tablet twice a day for 7 days, once a day for 7 days, every other day for 7 days, then stop. Expect worse headache for one to two weeks; seek same-day care for tremor, sweating, agitation, confusion or a seizure. A nurse phone check at the end of the first week. [S]
  • Galcanezumab 240 mg subcutaneously today as a loading dose, then 120 mg every four weeks; judge the effect at about three months, seeking at least half as many migraine days and fewer than 15 headache days a month. [S]
