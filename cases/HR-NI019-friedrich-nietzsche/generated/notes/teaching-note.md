# Teaching Note — Friedrich Nietzsche pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Friedrich Nietzsche was born on October 15, 1844, in Röcken, near Leipzig, where his father was a Lutheran minister. His father, a Lutheran pastor, died in 1849, when his son was four, after about a year of seizures and progressive neurological deficits; an autopsy described "softening" of the brain. Nietzsche later wrote that his father died "in his six-and-thirtieth year"; medical accounts give his age as 36. The cause was not established. He had severe myopia, worse in the right eye, and a right pupil larger than the left, both noted in early childhood; at about five an eye examination found that the right eye needed a very strong (six-dioptre) lens. By about 30 the right eye was functionally blind, and in 1878 an ophthalmologist found fluid in the right retina and diagnosed retinitis (central chorioretinitis). Severe headaches began in his school years; at nine he was already missing school because of them. They were mainly right-sided, frontal and above the eye, came with sensitivity to light and with nausea and vomiting, and grew more severe in adult life. In 1869, at 24, he was appointed to the chair of classical philology at Basel. Ill health led to sick leave in 1871 and again in 1876 to 1877, and in 1879 he resigned his professorship and received a small pension; he never held another post. Looking back in 1888, he wrote that in 1879, in his "six-and-thirtieth year," he reached "the lowest point in my vitality," when "my eyes could distinguish nothing that lay three paces away from me"; he resigned at Basel, and the next winter, his "first winter in Genoa," he wrote Dawn "in the midst of the agony of a headache which lasted three days, accompanied by violent nausea." After resigning he settled into a pattern of spending winters near the Mediterranean, usually in Italy, and summers in Sils Maria. After an unsuccessful spring taking the waters at Recoaro, he went to the Upper Engadine at the beginning of July 1881 and rented a modest room in Sils Maria, hoping the dry, sunny climate would relieve his headaches; he kept a strict daily schedule with five to seven hours of walking, filling notebooks as he went. He dated the idea of the eternal recurrence, the basic conception of Thus Spoke Zarathustra, to August 1881, on a walk beside the Lake of Silvaplana near Surlej. In the 1880s he took a variety of medicines for stomach cramps, constipation, retching, headaches and severe insomnia, which was treated with chloral hydrate, apparently in high doses. By his own admission he had gonorrhea twice in his twenties; whether either infection was in fact syphilis could not have been told apart reliably at the time. In early January 1889, in Turin, he had a mental breakdown: on January 3 he was stopped by police after a public disturbance (the story that he embraced a beaten horse is apocryphal), and he wrote bizarre, grandiose letters. His friend Franz Overbeck brought him to the asylum in Basel, and he was moved to the asylum in Jena; at both he was diagnosed with general paresis of the insane (paretic syphilis). He never recovered. From March 1890 his mother cared for him at home; after her death in 1897 his sister cared for him at the Villa Silberblick in Weimar. Strokes followed, and by 1899 he was bedridden with left-sided weakness. He died in the house on August 25, 1900, of pneumonia; no autopsy was performed. His sister founded the Nietzsche Archive in Naumburg in 1894 and moved it to Weimar in 1896, took control of his literary legacy during his illness, and assembled notebook material as The Will to Power; scholars regard her editing as not well founded in his surviving plans and marred by her strong anti-Semitic commitments.
- **R:** The cause of his final illness is disputed. His physicians diagnosed general paresis of the insane (neurosyphilis), long the standard account; later authors have argued instead for a slowly growing right-sided retro-orbital or sphenoid wing meningioma, frontotemporal dementia, CADASIL or MELAS, or a psychiatric illness; a 2015 review judged that the question will possibly remain open, while still regarding an atypical general paresis as the most probable diagnosis. Medical historians have interpreted his lifelong headaches as migraine without aura beginning in childhood; others have attributed them, wholly or partly, to a slowly growing tumor behind the right eye. One review attributes the blindness of his right eye to high progressive myopia with retinal degeneration, and his unequal pupils, present since childhood, to a unilateral tonic pupil rather than to syphilis. Based on his letters, after 1880 he had periods of elation and depressive episodes, notably in 1882 and in 1886 to 1887.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
