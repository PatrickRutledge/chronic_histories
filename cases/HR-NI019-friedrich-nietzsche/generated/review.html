Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Sources. The case relies on the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, the Klassik Stiftung Weimar (which holds the Nietzsche-Archiv and, in the Goethe- und Schiller-Archiv, his estate), the Nietzsche-Haus in Sils Maria, his own Ecce Homo in a public-domain translation, and the full texts of Sax (2003) and André and Rios (2015). For Hemelsoet et al. (2008), Orth and Trimble (2006), Danesh-Meyer and Young (2010) and Owen et al. (2007) only the PubMed abstracts were read. Britannica could not be fetched (access refused) and is not cited. His letters in the digital critical edition (nietzschesource.org) could not be read without a browser and are not cited; no letter is quoted except through the sources above.
  2. Index age and dates. The index visit on July 7, 2026 maps to July 7, 1881, a few days after he reached Sils Maria “at the beginning of July 1881” for his first summer there; he was 36. The synthetic date of birth is October 15, 1989 (shift 145 years). The visits on July 21, August 4, August 18, September 1 and September 15, 2026 map to the same days of 1881. The Nietzsche-Haus places him in Sils Maria for that summer and his own account places the eternal-recurrence idea there in August 1881; the exact day he left in the autumn was not verified, so the last visit was kept in mid-September and the three-month review is left as a goal date without a visit. Past medical history includes only events up to 1879; historical claims about later events are linked to later timeline events and are left out of the intake documents.
  3. Father (CLM-0002, family history). His own account says his father died “in his six-and-thirtieth year” (at 35); André and Rios and Hemelsoet et al. give 36. The case records “35 or 36.” The autopsy finding of “softening” of the brain is recorded as written, and the cause is “not established”; no modern diagnosis is given to his father. Statements in one source about his father's own headaches and mood are left out, as health facts about a relative that the episode does not need. His fear of a similar illness is a synthetic concern of the patient; no statement or decision is attributed to any relative.
  4. Disputed final illness (CLM-0013, R, disputed). His physicians diagnosed general paresis of the insane, the traditional neurosyphilis account. Sax (2003) argued for a right-sided retro-orbital meningioma, Owen et al. (2007) for a sphenoid wing meningioma, Orth and Trimble (2006) for frontotemporal dementia, and Hemelsoet et al. (2008) for CADASIL; Danesh-Meyer and Young (2010) found neither general paresis nor a meningioma convincing; André and Rios (2015) judge the question open while regarding an atypical general paresis as the most probable. No cause of the dementia is treated as established, and none is diagnosed in the modern episode. The roster's “probable neurosyphilis” and “probable syphilitic dementia” are therefore recorded only as the disputed historical diagnosis.
  5. Migraine (CLM-0014, R, disputed). Migraine is a retrospective interpretation; the modern chronic migraine diagnosis is synthetic and rests on a synthetic diary.
  6. Synthetic MRI and syphilis serology. The modern MRI with and without contrast is normal apart from elongated globes and a right posterior staphyloma of high myopia: no structural lesion is invented, and the normal result is not a claim about the historical meningioma debate. Imaging is justified in the modern episode by a strongly one-sided pattern, a rising frequency, long-standing unequal pupils and a poor eye. Syphilis serology (treponemal immunoassay with an index of 0.12 and RPR, both nonreactive) is ordered because of past gonorrhea, the unequal pupils and the poor vision; the result is nonreactive because the modern episode diagnoses migraine and medication overuse, and a positive result would read as a claim about the historical dispute. The RPR is narrative, since the record's observations must be numeric. Serology was ordered at intake and drawn on the morning of the consultation with the MRI, so its result first appears at the consultation.
  7. Gonorrhea (CLM-0010, D, probable, sensitive). André and Rios report that he admitted to gonorrhea twice in his twenties. It is recorded plainly in past medical history because it bears on the historical debate and on the modern serology decision; it is not a claim that he had syphilis.
  8. Sedatives (CLM-0009, D, probable, sensitive). His use of chloral hydrate “apparently in high doses” and many other medicines in the 1880s anchors the modern sedative-analgesic overuse. Chloral hydrate is not used in modern practice; the synthetic overused medicine is a butalbital, acetaminophen and caffeine combination (a barbiturate with an analgesic), which shows both medication overuse headache and the need to taper a barbiturate. It is not a claim about his real medicines.
  9. Eyes (CLM-0003, D; CLM-0015, R). His severe myopia, worse in the right eye, the right pupil larger since childhood, and near blindness of the right eye are documented. The modern pathologic myopia of the right eye with macular atrophy, the refraction (about -13.50 and -6.25 dioptres), the vision (20/100 and 20/20), the pressures and the contact lenses are synthetic, chosen to fit the documented history and the reading of Danesh-Meyer and Young. The unequal pupils are recorded as long-standing and both reactive; no tonic pupil or other pupil diagnosis is made.
  10. Sister and mother. His sister's founding of the Nietzsche Archive and her editing of The Will to Power, criticized in the Stanford Encyclopedia, are recorded as documented history (CLM-0017) and linked to a timeline event after his mother's death; his mother's and sister's care in his last years is history (CLM-0012). Neither has any role, statement or decision in the modern episode, and no health fact about either is recorded.
  11. Mood. The depressive episodes in his letters after 1880 (CLM-0016, R, possible, sensitive) are after the index date and are not used as a diagnosis. The modern PHQ-9 is 7 at intake and 4 at ten weeks, with item 9 scored 0, and no F-code problem is recorded, so no safety assessment is required; direct questions about hopelessness and self-harm are documented at each focused visit.
  12. Name. “Friedrich Wilhelm Nietzsche,” his full name, is the alias; no other record uses it. No other figure with a record in the dataset is named.
  13. Roster leads. The roster's migraines, dementia, mental collapse at 44 and severe vision problems are all documented and used as history; “probable neurosyphilis” is recorded only as disputed (see above).
  14. Instruments. The MIDAS is recorded as its five PhenX LOINC items (unit days), with the total in the notes; the HIT-6, the SNNOOP10 screen, the diary, sleep hours and the RPR are narrative. Intraocular pressures are imaging-category observations.
  15. Clinical judgment calls. (1) MRI with and without contrast rather than no imaging, for the reasons above; normal result. (2) Syphilis serology nonreactive; no lumbar puncture. (3) No medicine change at intake beyond education and a cap on the tablets, so that the diary records his baseline before the consultation. (4) A three-week scheduled taper of the butalbital combination, because barbiturates are tapered (EAN 2020), with prevention started the same day (EHF 2022); ibuprofen stopped at once. (5) Galcanezumab over topiramate (cognitive effects and the rare acute myopia with angle-closure glaucoma, a poor fit for a writer with high myopia), over erenumab (constipation), and over onabotulinumtoxinA as first choice (injections every 12 weeks at a center while he travels), with onabotulinumtoxinA as the next option. (6) Naproxen with metoclopramide during the taper and sumatriptan added after it, with limits of two days a week and eight triptan days a month. (7) Contact lenses for an anisometropia of about seven dioptres. (8) CBT-I without sedative sleeping tablets. (9) Medication overuse headache resolved at eight weeks; chronic migraine left active, with efficacy to be judged formally at three months.
  16. Preventive visit. A periodic adult preventive visit at 36 (CPT 99395 with 99497 in plain text). Judgment calls: no lipid testing before 40 without risk factors; no diabetes screening at a BMI of 22.1; one-time HIV, hepatitis C and hepatitis B screening (negative, reported at eight weeks); syphilis up to date; Tdap, the first hepatitis B vaccine dose and the influenza vaccine given; COVID-19 vaccine declined as an individual decision; advance care planning records an unnamed agent, a friend with whom he corresponds.
  17. Sick visit. An acute posterior vitreous detachment of the right eye without a retinal break, chosen because it tests the plan: high myopia raises the risk of a retinal tear, flashes and floaters in one eye must be told apart from migraine aura, and the eye needs a same-day dilated examination and a repeat within six weeks (done at the eight-week review). It is not a historical claim.
  18. Focused visits. The three chronic-care follow-ups (two weeks with the consultation, four weeks, eight weeks) are documented as focused visits on ICHD-3, the 2021 AHS consensus and its 2024 position statement, the 2020 EAN guideline on medication-overuse headache, the 2022 EHF CGRP antibody guideline and the 2018 EHF onabotulinumtoxinA guideline, the AHS Choosing Wisely list, the CDC STI guideline, the AAO refractive errors, comprehensive eye evaluation and posterior vitreous detachment Preferred Practice Patterns, the AASM insomnia guideline and the CMID depression monograph. One deviation is recorded instead of an invented result: the nurse phone check planned for the end of the first taper week did not take place; no harm resulted (rebound headache only, no withdrawal symptoms, taper completed as written), and the corrective action is to book phone checks at the visit that starts any taper. Synthetic findings introduced: rebound headache in the first ten days of the taper, mild redness at the first injection site, and the posterior vitreous detachment.
  19. Consultation. The headache medicine referral and the consultation by Ingeborg Achterberg-Sato, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the two-week visit, are S. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: chronic migraine and medication overuse headache by ICHD-3 criteria; no secondary cause on MRI, serology or the eye examination, and no genetic testing for his father's unexplained illness; the three-week taper with naproxen and metoclopramide and sumatriptan after it; galcanezumab with onabotulinumtoxinA as the next option; and a written plan before he leaves for the winter. The phone check the consultant recommended is the recorded deviation.
