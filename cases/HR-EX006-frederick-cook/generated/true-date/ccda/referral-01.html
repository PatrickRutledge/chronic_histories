Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Frederick Albert Cook was born on June 10, 1865, in Hortonville, New York, the fifth of six children of Theodor and Magdalena Koch (later Cook). [D]
  • His father, a physician, died of pneumonia when Frederick was five, leaving the family poor; he worked at odd jobs while at school, entered the College of Physicians and Surgeons at Columbia University in 1887, transferred to New York University, passed his medical examinations, and opened a medical office in Manhattan. [D]
  • He served as surgeon, ethnologist and photographer on Robert Peary's North Greenland expedition of 1891 to 1892. [D]
  • As physician of the Belgian Antarctic Expedition (1897 to 1899), whose ship Belgica wintered in the pack ice, he recognized the signs of scurvy in the men and recommended that they eat fresh seal meat. On this expedition he met Roald Amundsen, who remained his supporter through the later controversies. [D]
  • He claimed to have reached the summit of Mount McKinley (Denali) on the morning of September 16, 1906, with one companion. The claim has been disputed ever since and is widely regarded as untrue. [D]
  • He claimed to have reached the North Pole on April 21, 1908, with two Inuit companions, Ahwelah and Etukishook, after which they wintered in a cave at Cape Sparbo on Devon Island before returning in 1909. A commission of the University of Copenhagen, given his surviving records, declared the claim not proven, and Robert Peary, who claimed the Pole for April 6, 1909, disputed it publicly. [D]
  • He organized the Petroleum Producers' Association in Texas in 1922. In 1923 he was convicted of mail fraud, fined $12,000 and sentenced to 14 years and 9 months. [D]
  • The Columbia Encyclopedia states that he served five years (1925 to 1930) of the sentence. [D]
  • He called it 'the greatest tragedy of my life' to have been a federal prisoner for six years and three months: sixteen months in the county jail at Fort Worth, Texas, and four years and eleven months at Leavenworth. [D]
  • At Leavenworth he served as a physician in the prison hospital and edited the prison newspaper, the New Era. [D]
  • He was paroled from Leavenworth in March 1930, at 64. The parole required that he 'write and speak nothing but the truth', the government was to take the first $12,000 he earned toward his fine, and he met reporters wearing a faded old suit before leaving for Chicago. [D]
  • After his release he worked on his memoirs and led a relatively quiet life. [D]
