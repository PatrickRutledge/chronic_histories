## Historical evidence

1. Kildare Library and Arts Service (Kildare County Council), Kildare eHistory Journal. "Death of Ernest Shackleton" (2022), quoting the *Freeman's Journal* of January 30, 1922 and other newspapers. Supports his birth at Kilkea, County Kildare, in 1874; that he was the son of Dr. Henry Shackleton, of a well-known Quaker family; and his death after a heart attack at South Georgia, marked on its centenary on January 5, 2022. https://kildarelibraries.ie/ehistory/?p=4914
2. Library of Congress. Name authority record n50002512, "Shackleton, Ernest Henry, Sir, 1874-1922." Its biographical note gives the dates of birth and death, February 15, 1874 and January 5, 1922. https://id.loc.gov/authorities/names/n50002512.html
3. Wild F, with Macklin AH. *Shackleton's Last Voyage: The Story of the Quest.* London, 1923. Project Gutenberg eBook 58973. His return to London after the war; John Quiller Rowett's financial support; the change from an Arctic to a southern expedition; organization within three months; sailing postponed beyond August 20; departure from St. Katharine's Dock on September 17, 1921; the sudden faintness ashore at Rio de Janeiro in December 1921 ("this attack had a greater significance than was appreciated at the time"); Macklin's note that he looked tired and ill at the end of December; Macklin's account of his death at about 2.50 a.m. on January 5, 1922, after facial neuralgia treated with aspirin; and Macklin's medical appendix: "The cause was atheroma of the coronary arteries," long-standing and "due to overstrain during a period of debility," with the scurvy of the Discovery southern journey as a possible lasting cause. https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/58973
4. Calder I, Till J. "Shackleton's heart." *J R Soc Med* 2016;109(3):106–108. PMID 26759361. Read in full text. The Discovery breakdown (breathlessness and weakness, attributed to scurvy and "a sort of asthma"; invalided home in 1903); Marshall's Nimrod diary of January 20, 1908 ("very unwell after pulling on a rope") and the pulmonary systolic murmur he heard twice; McIlroy's recollection that Shackleton "wouldn't undress and let me listen to his heart"; "a heavy cigarette smoker for most of his life"; Macklin's view at embalming (angina pectoris from "pretty extensive atheroma of the coronary arteries"; no further pathological examination feasible); and the authors' argument for a congenital atrial septal defect. The interpretation is graded **R**. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4794964/
5. Firth PG, Benavidez OJ, Fiechtner L. "The signs and symptoms of Ernest Shackleton." *J Med Biogr* 2023;31(1):10–15. PMID 33896263. Abstract only: inflamed gums, severe dyspnea, and exercise intolerance on the Discovery expedition; a pulmonary systolic murmur later; thiamine deficiency with cardiomyopathy proposed. Graded **R**. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33896263/
6. Shackleton EH. *South: The Story of Shackleton's Last Expedition, 1914–1917.* London, 1919. Project Gutenberg eBook 5199. "Of the Ross Sea Party, Mackintosh, Hayward, and Spencer-Smith died"; "out of fifty-six men three died in the Antarctic." https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5199
7. South Georgia Museum. "Death of Shackleton." The slight faintness at Rio in December 1921; the fatal heart attack at 47; burial at Grytviken on March 5, 1922, at Lady Shackleton's wish. https://sgmuseum.gs/chapter/death-of-shackleton/
8. HISTMED Master Roster v3. Internal project source. Its clinical claims were provisional and were not treated as independent corroboration.

## Editorial resolutions

- **Index age and whereabouts.** The modern episode presents him at 47. The synthetic date of birth is 1979-02-15 (the true month and day), so the shift is 105 years. The index visit is May 12, 2026 (true frame May 12, 1921) and the last visit is September 8, 2026 (true frame September 8, 1921), nine days before the Quest left London on September 17, 1921. Wild's account places him in London organizing the expedition through 1921; every visit, including the preventive and sick visits, falls before the sailing. Exact day-by-day whereabouts in London were not verified.
- **Presenting problem and modern diagnosis.** Exertional chest tightness and breathlessness is the presenting problem (PRB-001, R07.89), resolved at the cardiology consultation, when chronic coronary disease with stable angina (PRB-003) is recorded. The diagnosis is synthetic. It is anchored to the documented exertional illnesses (CLM-0002, CLM-0003), the faintness at Rio (CLM-0008), and Macklin's documented wording, "atheroma of the coronary arteries" (CLM-0009). Macklin's opinion is recorded as he wrote it; his examination at embalming was not a full autopsy, and the cause of death is recorded as his opinion, not as a modern diagnosis. The record does not decide what caused his earlier breakdowns.
- **Alternative theories.** The congenital atrial septal defect (Calder and Till 2016) and thiamine-deficiency cardiomyopathy (Firth et al.) are recorded as **R**, possible. The modern echocardiogram includes an agitated-saline study and shows no shunt, so the episode teaches how the question would be settled today without making a claim about his real heart.
- **Refusal of examination.** McIlroy's recollection that he would not undress to have his heart listened to is recorded (CLM-0005); its year was not confirmed and is not used. At Rio, Wild's account says he had only "felt a slight faintness"; a refusal of examination at Rio, which some popular sources describe, was not found in a fetched source and is not recorded. In the modern episode his reluctance, and his fear that a diagnosis would stop the expedition, are worked through with shared decision-making; no statement or decision is attributed to Macklin, McIlroy, Wild, his wife, or any other real person.
- **Smoking and drinking.** Heavy cigarette smoking is documented (CLM-0006, **D**, probable) and anchors the synthetic smoking history. Heavy drinking in his last years, named in the roster, was not confirmed in a fetched reputable source and is not recorded as a claim; the modern hazardous drinking (AUDIT 12) is synthetic, marked as not a claim about his real drinking, and is not coded as a disorder, so no safety assessment is required.
- **Revascularization.** The stent is a judgment call: in chronic coronary disease with normal heart function and no left main disease, a stent improves angina but has not been shown to lower death or heart attack. It is placed because angina persisted on an optimized beta-blocker, after a shared decision in which he chose symptom relief for the expedition and accepted six months of dual antiplatelet therapy at sea. The procedure date (July 1, 2026) is not a clinic visit; the decision is dated to the six-week visit.
- **Lipid target.** The record uses the ESC 2024 target (LDL cholesterol below 55 mg/dL with at least a 50% reduction), which the CMID monograph also cites, and adds ezetimibe at an LDL of 62 mg/dL; the 2023 AHA/ACC guideline would also allow it at that level in a very-high-risk patient.
- **Nimrod and Endurance.** The Nimrod expedition is recorded only through Marshall's diary as cited by Calder and Till. The Endurance claim records that three men of the Ross Sea party died and that, of fifty-six men, three died in the Antarctic. The roster's phrase "led 27 men to safety across 800 miles" was not verified in the fetched sources and is not used.
- **Family.** No family medical history is recorded, because none was found in the fetched sources. His father appears in the birth claim and Lady Shackleton only in the burial claim. His children were not confirmed in a fetched source and are not named. The modern episode records his health care agents without naming them.
- **Aliases.** "Ernest Henry Shackleton" and "Sir Ernest Shackleton" are recorded as aliases; neither appears in any other record. "Ernest" alone is the first name of another record's subject, which the whole-name check does not block.
- **Sick and preventive visits.** A periodic adult preventive visit (age 47) on August 11, 2026 (true frame August 11, 1921) and a same-day sick visit on August 25, 2026 (true frame August 25, 1921) were added. The sick visit is an acute low back strain from lifting stores, chosen because it interacts with dual antiplatelet therapy (no NSAIDs) and the statin (deciding against a CK test), and teaches imaging restraint. It does not echo a documented illness.
- **Focused visits.** The four chronic-care follow-ups (two weeks, six weeks, ten weeks, and four months) are documented as focused visits on the CMID ischemic heart disease and hyperlipidemia monographs, the 2023 AHA/ACC and 2024 ESC guidelines, and the USPSTF tobacco and alcohol recommendations. All results, the CT angiogram, the echocardiogram, the stent, and the exercise test are synthetic. The coronary calcium score, CT-FFR, METs, and exercise test findings are recorded in the notes only, because no suitable numeric code was used. One protocol deviation is recorded: the cardiac rehabilitation referral due at the diagnosis of stable angina was deferred for eight weeks until after the stent, with no harm evident and a corrective action.
- **Consultation.** The cardiology consultation by the fictional Dr. Solberg-Adebayo on the second visit is synthetic. Its recommendations (a higher beta-blocker dose, ezetimibe if LDL stays at 55 mg/dL or higher, a stent by shared decision if angina persists, six months of dual antiplatelet therapy, cardiac rehabilitation, a pre-departure exercise test, the expedition precautions, and no surveillance imaging) are those the later visits carry out; the late rehabilitation referral is recorded as a deviation.
- Shackleton died in 1922, so the recent-death review rule does not apply. Ordinary clinical and provenance reviews remain required.
