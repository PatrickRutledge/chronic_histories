# Teaching Note — Ernest Shackleton pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Ernest Henry Shackleton was born on February 15, 1874, at Kilkea, County Kildare, Ireland, the son of Dr. Henry Shackleton, of a well-known Quaker family. On the southern sledge journey of the Discovery expedition he became unable to pull the sledge because of breathlessness and weakness; the collapse was attributed to scurvy and "a sort of asthma," and he was invalided home against his will in 1903. On the Nimrod expedition (1907 to 1909), its medical officer Eric Marshall recorded in his diary on January 20, 1908, that Shackleton was very unwell after pulling on a rope, and twice heard a pulmonary systolic murmur. On the Imperial Trans-Antarctic (Endurance) expedition of 1914 to 1917, of fifty-six men three died in the Antarctic: Mackintosh, Hayward, and Spencer-Smith of the Ross Sea party. No member of the Endurance's own party died. His colleague Dr. James McIlroy later recalled that on one occasion Shackleton would not undress and let him listen to his heart. He was a heavy cigarette smoker for most of his life. After the war he returned to London. In 1921, with John Quiller Rowett taking on practically the whole financial responsibility, he turned his planned Arctic expedition into the Shackleton-Rowett expedition to the South; it was organized and equipped within three months, its sailing was postponed beyond August 20 by a strike of ships' joiners and other delays, and the Quest left St. Katharine's Dock in London on September 17, 1921. In Rio de Janeiro, on the morning the Quest sailed in December 1921, an urgent message called Dr. Alexander Macklin to Shackleton, who had been taken suddenly ill ashore; Macklin found him fully recovered, saying he had merely felt a slight faintness. Frank Wild wrote that "this attack had a greater significance than was appreciated at the time." At the end of December, Macklin noticed that he was looking tired and ill. At South Georgia, early on January 5, 1922, aboard the Quest at Grytviken, he called Macklin to his cabin, complaining of severe facial neuralgia for which he had taken aspirin; while Macklin was with him he had an attack and died suddenly, at about 2.50 a.m. Macklin wrote that the cause was "atheroma of the coronary arteries," a long-standing condition that in his opinion was due to overstrain during a period of debility. At embalming Macklin could see the heart, but no further pathological examination was feasible. At the wish of Lady Shackleton he was buried at Grytviken, South Georgia, on March 5, 1922.
- **R:** Two authors argue that his breakdowns, beginning with the Discovery journey, were caused by a congenital atrial septal defect, citing the pulmonary systolic murmur and his episodes of breathlessness and weakness. Other authors propose that the illness on the Discovery expedition was thiamine-deficiency (beriberi) cardiomyopathy, alone or worsened by scurvy.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
