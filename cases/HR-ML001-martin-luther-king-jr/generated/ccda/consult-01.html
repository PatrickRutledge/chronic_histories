History of Present Illness

Dr. King, 36, is seen at the request of Dr. Ashby-Nwankwo. For about two months, after a winter of continuous campaigning and an admission for exhaustion six months ago, he has had low mood most days, loss of pleasure in reading and preaching, guilt that he cannot do more, poor concentration, fatigue and broken sleep of about five hours, with an hour to fall asleep and waking before five. His energy returns on the platform but drains afterwards. He has lived for nearly ten years with threats against his life and his family, including the bombing of his home, a stabbing that required chest surgery, jailings, and anonymous threats, one of which urged him to take his own life; he believes his calls are monitored. He is watchful in crowds and at home, but has no nightmares, flashbacks, avoidance or emotional numbing. He has never had a period of elevated mood, high energy or little need for sleep. At twelve, grieving his grandmother, he impulsively injured himself; he has not had thoughts of suicide since and has none now. He speaks calmly of the likelihood that he will be killed. His faith, his family and his congregation sustain him. He fears that a psychiatric record could be used against him and his work. [S]

  • In 1934 his father, the Reverend Michael King, attended the Baptist World Alliance meeting in Berlin and afterwards changed his own name and his son's from Michael to Martin Luther, after the German Protestant reformer; the son's birth certificate was altered to read Martin Luther King, Jr. on July 23, 1957.   • His grandmother, Jennie Celeste Williams, died on May 18, 1941, when he was twelve, while he was at a parade without his parents' permission. The editors of his papers note that neither he nor his father later described this as a suicide attempt; his father wrote that he cried off and on for several days and could not sleep at night. [D]
  • He entered Morehouse College in 1944, was ordained in 1948, graduated from Crozer Theological Seminary in 1951, and received his doctorate in systematic theology from Boston University on June 5, 1955. [D]
  • He married Coretta Scott on June 18, 1953, at the Scott home near Marion, Alabama. [D]
  • As president of the Montgomery Improvement Association during the bus boycott, his home was bombed on the evening of January 30, 1956, while he spoke at a mass meeting; his wife and daughter were not hurt, and he pleaded with an angry crowd for nonviolence. [D]
  • On September 20, 1958, at a book signing at Blumstein's Department Store in Harlem, he was stabbed in the upper left side of the chest with a seven-inch steel letter opener. At Harlem Hospital he had more than two hours of surgery; the blade lay so close to the aorta that, he was told, a sneeze could have punctured it, and he wrote that his whole chest had to be opened to remove it. He left the hospital on October 3 and convalesced until late October. [D]
  • He was arrested and jailed repeatedly: at a sit-in at Rich's department store in Atlanta in October 1960, when he was sentenced to four months of hard labor for violating probation; in Albany, Georgia, in December 1961 and in July 1962, when he spent two weeks in jail; and in Birmingham from April 12 to 19, 1963. [D]
  • The FBI monitored him from December 1955; wiretaps on his home and the SCLC offices were authorized in October 1963, and he was subjected to surveillance under the counterintelligence program. In about November 1964 the FBI anonymously sent him a recording with an anonymous letter that SCLC staff read as urging him to take his own life. [D]
  • In October 1964, exhausted by a heavy speaking schedule and with a respiratory infection, he was admitted to St. Joseph's Infirmary in Atlanta for several days of rest; he was asleep there on October 14, 1964, when his wife telephoned to tell him he had been awarded the Nobel Peace Prize. [D]
  • He received the Nobel Peace Prize in Oslo on December 10, 1964, at 35 the youngest person to have won it. [D]
  • After voting rights marchers were beaten at the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma on March 7, 1965, he led the march from Selma to Montgomery from March 17 to 25, 1965. [D]
  • A man who drove for the King family in the mid-1960s described him as a chain smoker who kept his cigarettes hidden at home. [D]
  • Biographers and historians describe depression in his adult life: David Garrow wrote that, partly as a result of the FBI's intimidation, he often suffered from morbid depression, a contention others have disputed; a psychologist writing in 2024 states that, although never diagnosed, he had serious bouts of depression that he kept secret for fear it could be used against him. Severity is moderate. There is no history of hypomania and the MDQ is negative, so an antidepressant is appropriate.
