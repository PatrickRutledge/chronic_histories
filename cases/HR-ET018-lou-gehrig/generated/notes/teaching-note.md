# Teaching Note — Lou Gehrig pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Henry Louis Gehrig was born on June 19, 1903, in the Yorkville section of Manhattan, New York, to Heinrich and Christina Gehrig, German immigrants. Three of his siblings died young: his sister Anna in September 1902 at about three months, his sister Sophie in the winter of 1906 of a combination of measles, diphtheria and bronchopneumonia, and a brother shortly after birth. He enrolled at Columbia University on a football scholarship in February 1921. He played first base for the New York Yankees and, on May 2, 1939, in Detroit, took himself out of the lineup after a then-record 2,130 consecutive games. In 1938 he batted .295, the first time under .300 since 1925, and his hitting fell off sharply in midsummer; he had an attack of lumbago and a badly jammed thumb that season. In the winter of 1938 to 1939 he lost his strength and collapsed while ice skating, in spring training 1939 he collapsed while putting on his uniform pants and his reflexes had slowed alarmingly, and at 35 he batted .143 in the first games of the 1939 season. His first neurological symptoms began in 1938, around the time of his 35th birthday. He had repeated head injuries, including concussions, during his playing career. He was examined at the Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota, from June 13 to June 19, 1939, and on his 36th birthday his internist, Harold C. Habein, certified the diagnosis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis; the clinic's statement added that he could continue in some executive activity. He retired from baseball on July 4, 1939, when the Yankees held Lou Gehrig Appreciation Day before nearly 42,000 fans; in his speech he said that he considered himself "the luckiest man on the face of the earth." In October 1939 he accepted Mayor La Guardia's appointment to a ten-year term as a New York City Parole Commissioner, was sworn in on January 2, 1940, and resigned quietly about a month before his death because his condition made the work impossible. He died at his home on Delafield Avenue in the Fieldston section of the Bronx on June 2, 1941, at 37.
- **R:** Researchers at the Boston University center for the study of traumatic encephalopathy have suggested that repetitive head trauma can cause a syndrome that mimics ALS, and that his repeated head injuries may have contributed to his decline; the Mayo Clinic has sealed his records, and it is not known whether an autopsy was performed.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
