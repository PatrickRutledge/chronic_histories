# Teaching Note — Frances Burney pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Frances Burney was born in King's Lynn, Norfolk, on June 13, 1752. Burney's mother died in 1762, when Burney was about ten. Burney married Alexandre d'Arblay, whom she met in 1793, and her only child, Alexander, was born in December 1794, when she was 42. In April 1802 she and her son joined her husband in France, where they lived for about ten years. Burney was a novelist, diarist, and playwright. Her first novel, Evelina, was published anonymously in January 1778, and she published four novels between 1778 and 1814. In her letter Burney wrote that about August 1810 she began to be troubled by "a small pain in my breast," heavy rather than acute, which increased week by week. She resisted examination for some months; physicians and surgeons then treated her for months without success, the hardness of the affected spot increased, and her right arm was kept from use for many months. She noted that the breast was not discolored and not much larger than the other. On the last day of September 1811, at her home in Paris, Burney underwent removal of the breast without anesthesia. The operation was performed by the surgeon Larrey, surgeon of the Imperial Guard, with other physicians and surgeons present. She was given a wine cordial beforehand, and she wrote that the operation, including the dressing, lasted 20 minutes and that she believed she fainted twice. Burney described the operation in a journal letter to her sister Esther, begun in March 1812 and finished in June 1812, nine months after the operation; she wrote that she dared not revise or read it, "the recollection is still so painful." The manuscript is in the Berg Collection of the New York Public Library. Burney died on January 6, 1840, at 87.
- **R:** Whether Burney's breast disease was cancer is debated. Her letter and a modern biographical source call it breast cancer, but no tissue was examined; a 2024 article argues that it was tuberculosis rather than carcinoma. She lived for more than 28 years after the operation.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
