Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Recent-death rule and living relatives. She died in 1987, before the rule’s threshold, so it does not apply. Her husband and her siblings, however, are living or recently living public people. None of them is named anywhere in the record; her husband appears only as “her husband, a pianist and conductor,” in the documented history of her marriage (CLM-0004) and in the modern household, with no role, statement or health fact. The family memoir and the claims drawn from it, an account attributed to her husband of how her first symptoms began, and later disputed claims about her death are not used at all.
  2. Sources not used. The Jacqueline du Pré Charitable Trust, the MS Society UK and the Royal Academy of Music pages on her were not found; the New York Times obituary could not be fetched; the Guardian obituary was not found; Britannica and the Jewish Women’s Archive could not be fetched (403). Elizabeth Wilson’s biography was not read, and no reliable quotation of its medical account was found; it is not cited. The 2018 review cites Carol Easton’s biography, which was not read.
  3. Name. The display name is Jacqueline du Pré. Splitting at the last space would make “Jacqueline du” her given names. The record uses identity.name_parts with given names Jacqueline Mary and the family name “du Pré” (lower-case particle), so the C-CDA and FHIR names are correct; the notes call her Ms. du Pré. Aliases are Jacqueline Mary du Pré and the unaccented Jacqueline du Pre; neither appears in another record. Her family nickname is not used as an alias, because it is part of another figure’s name in this batch.
  4. Death date. 19 October 1987 (Wikipedia, the 2018 review); Women in World History gives 20 October, the date of the New York Times obituary, recorded as a conflicting alternative.
  5. Index age and dates. The synthetic date of birth is 26 January 1998 (shift 53 years). The index visit on 9 March 2026 maps to 9 March 1973, when she was 28: after her last public concerts in London and New York in February 1973 and before the documented diagnosis in London in October 1973. All visits map to March to October 1973, within her lifetime; they are not a claim about where she was. The modern diagnosis comes two weeks after the first visit rather than four years after the first symptoms, which is the intended contrast.
  6. Intake claims. The 1971 loss of sensation and fatigue (CLM-0005), the teen-age visual episodes (CLM-0006, R) and the last concerts (CLM-0007) appear in the intake documents; the diagnosis (CLM-0008), the course (CLM-0010, R) and later events are linked to later timeline events.
  7. Presenting problem. Numbness and clumsiness of the hands with Lhermitte’s sign (R20.2) is resolved at the diagnosis visit on 23 March 2026, when relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (G35.A, both_with_link) and vitamin D deficiency (E55.9) are recorded. G35.A is the FY2026 ICD-10-CM code for relapsing-remitting MS. The intake documents state only what was known then.
  8. Earlier episode. The synthetic history of a month of finger tingling 18 months before the first visit is anchored to her documented loss of sensation in her fingers in 1971. The teen-age blurred vision noted by the Mayo authors (CLM-0006) is R and is not used in the modern history; nor is the account of a thigh numbness after anesthesia, which rests on her husband’s recollection.
  9. Diagnostic choices. Aquaporin-4 antibody was tested because the presentation is a myelitis; MOG antibody was not, because nothing in the picture suggests MOG antibody disease. The 2024 McDonald revision was published (Lancet Neurology, September 2025) and is cited alongside the 2017 criteria; she meets both.
  10. Disease-modifying therapy. Ocrelizumab was chosen as early high-efficacy therapy for active disease: natalizumab was set aside because her JCV antibody is positive (index 1.4), and ofatumumab, equally reasonable, was declined at her preference for twice-yearly infusions given her travel. This is a teaching choice for review.
  11. Relapse treatment. Intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days was used; NICE NG220 prefers oral methylprednisolone 500 mg daily for 5 days, which the consultant names as an equivalent alternative. This is a judgment call for review.
  12. Vaccination timing. PCV20, the first recombinant zoster dose and the first hepatitis B dose were given three weeks before the first infusion; the second hepatitis B dose came two weeks after B-cell depletion began and the second zoster dose three months later. Starting treatment promptly for active disease was judged more important than completing every series first; the resulting non-protective hepatitis B antibody (6 mIU/mL) is recorded and a booster deferred while B cells are depleted.
  13. Synthetic clinical picture. All visits, results, imaging, CSF findings, the JCV, hepatitis B and varicella results, nine-hole peg times (coded as NIH Toolbox nine-hole pegboard times, in seconds), EDSS scores, treatments, her height (173 cm) and weights are synthetic. Qualitative results (CSF, antibodies, serology, pregnancy and urine tests) are in note text only.
  14. Sick visit. Acute uncomplicated cystitis (N30.00) with a pseudo-relapse, seven weeks after the first ocrelizumab course, chosen because infections are more common on anti-CD20 therapy and because telling a pseudo-relapse from a relapse (and not giving steroids for it) is a core teaching point; it insinuates no undocumented real diagnosis.
