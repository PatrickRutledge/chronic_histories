# Teaching Note — Jacqueline du Pré pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Jacqueline Mary du Pré was born in Oxford, England, on 26 January 1945. Her mother was a pianist who had studied at the Royal Academy of Music, and her father an accountant. She began the cello as a small child, studied with William Pleeth at the Guildhall School of Music from the mid-1950s, won the Guilhermina Suggia Award at 11 in 1956 and the Queen's Prize at 15, attended a Pablo Casals masterclass in Zermatt in 1960, and made her formal debut at Wigmore Hall in London in March 1961, at 16. In 1965, at 20, she recorded Elgar's Cello Concerto with the London Symphony Orchestra under Sir John Barbirolli; she played chiefly on the Davidov Stradivarius of 1712. She converted to Judaism and married her husband, a pianist and conductor, in Jerusalem in June 1967. In 1971 her playing declined as she began to lose sensitivity in her fingers and other parts of her body; she returned to England exhausted, took a sabbatical from 1971 to 1972 and performed only rarely. When she resumed in 1972 the numbness in her fingers worsened, with fatigue. Her last public concerts were in February 1973, in London, where she played the Elgar Concerto, and in New York. She was diagnosed with multiple sclerosis in London in October 1973, at 28, about four years after her first symptoms; the diagnosis ended her career as a performer. She went on teaching and giving master classes, singing or whistling the musical lines and explaining the fingering when she could no longer play, and remained a teacher and mentor until her death. She was appointed an Officer of the Order of the British Empire in the 1976 New Year Honours. After her diagnosis and retirement she raised funds for multiple sclerosis research; fellowships in her name later funded travel grants for early-career MS researchers. She died of multiple sclerosis in London on 19 October 1987, at 42, and was buried in Golders Green Jewish Cemetery. One reference work gives 20 October.
- **R:** Two physicians writing about her illness note episodes of blurred vision in her teens, which they read as possibly an early sign of multiple sclerosis. Corticosteroids were the treatment available to her; physicians writing later judge that her illness followed a relapsing-remitting course that became secondary progressive.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
