Assessment

  • Relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis, active (G35.A). Multiple sclerosis by the 1964 McDonald criteria: a current attack of partial cervical myelitis, with dissemination in space in four regions and dissemination in time shown by enhancing and non-enhancing lesions on the same scan; CSF-specific oligoclonal bands add certainty. The 1971 revision is also met, and central veins support the diagnosis. The earlier episode of finger tingling was probably a first attack. Mimics were considered and excluded: normal B12 and thyroid function, negative aquaporin-4 antibody (a cell-based assay is needed for any myelitis), negative HIV and syphilis serology. MOG antibody testing is not indicated: there is no optic neuritis, long cord lesion or ADEM-like illness, and the lesions are typical of MS. With a disabling relapse, an enhancing cord lesion and multiple lesions at 28, her disease is active, and early high-efficacy treatment gives the best long-term outcome. [S]
  • Vitamin D deficiency (E55.9). Correct the deficiency; vitamin D is not a treatment for MS. [S]

Data reviewed: Dr. Thackeray-Osei's intake note and results. MRI of the brain with gadolinium: eleven ovoid T2 lesions, eight periventricular (several perpendicular to the ventricles), two juxtacortical and one in the right middle cerebellar peduncle; one periventricular lesion enhances; central veins in most lesions. MRI of the cervical and thoracic spine: short dorsal lesion at C3 to C4 with patchy enhancement, non-enhancing lesion at T7, no swelling. Lumbar puncture: white cells 6, protein 42 mg/dL, six CSF-specific oligoclonal bands, IgG index 0.86. Serum aquaporin-4 IgG (cell-based) negative; HIV and syphilis negative; JCV antibody positive, index 1.4. Vitamin B12 468 pg/mL and TSH 1.6 mIU/L at the first visit. Drawn with the lumbar puncture: hepatitis B surface antigen and core antibody negative, surface antibody negative; varicella IgG positive. Today: 25-hydroxyvitamin D 16 ng/mL; IgG 1080 and IgM 124 mg/dL; urine pregnancy test negative. [S]

Plan of Treatment

  • Treat the relapse, which limits her hand function and her work: methylprednisolone 1 g intravenously daily for 3 days after a negative urine dipstick (oral methylprednisolone 500 mg daily for 5 days is an equivalent alternative). [S]
  • Early high-efficacy disease-modifying therapy with ocrelizumab: 300 mg intravenously on day 1 and day 15, then 600 mg every six months, with methylprednisolone 100 mg, an antihistamine and acetaminophen before each infusion. Natalizumab is not advised because her JCV antibody is positive, which raises the risk of PML with longer use; ofatumumab is an equally effective option that she declined in favor of twice-yearly infusions. [S]
  • Before the first infusion: hepatitis B screening (done; not immune), immunoglobulins, varicella and measles immunity, and a pregnancy test; inactivated vaccines at least two weeks before (PCV20, recombinant zoster and hepatitis B started today), and no live vaccines once treatment starts. Check hepatitis B surface antibody one to two months after the series. [S]
  • Re-baseline MRI of the brain three to six months after starting, then yearly; blood count and immunoglobulins before each dose; teach her that infections, especially urinary infections, are more common and can cause a pseudo-relapse that does not need steroids. [S]
  • Occupational therapy for hand function and energy management, and a graded return to practice; aerobic exercise and pacing for fatigue after excluding other causes. [S]
  • Cholecalciferol 4000 units daily for the deficiency, with a level in three months. [S]
  • Pregnancy: contraception for now; when she is ready, a preconception visit, with conception planned a few months after an ocrelizumab dose. [S]
