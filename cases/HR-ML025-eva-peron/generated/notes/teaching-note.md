# Teaching Note — Eva Perón pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** She was born Eva María Ibarguren on May 7, 1919, in Los Toldos, a village in the Province of Buenos Aires, Argentina; her marriage contract later gave her birth date as May 7, 1922. She married Juan Perón on October 22, 1945, and became Argentina's first lady after his election in 1946. Her work for the poor made her a powerful political figure, and she is best known as Evita. Her medical ordeal began in January 1950, when she was 30: she fainted and underwent an appendectomy. Despite persistent weakness and anemia from vaginal bleeding, she delayed further tests. In August 1951, much weaker, she developed increasingly severe abdominal pain and fainted again. Examination showed advanced cancer of the cervix, and Argentine doctors treated her with radium, then a standard therapy. In October 1951 the New York cancer surgeon George Pack examined her under anesthesia to confirm the cancer, and in November 1951 he performed extensive surgery, a hysterectomy, because the cancer had spread to adjacent pelvic organs. He entered the operating room after she was anesthetized and left before she awoke. She underwent both procedures believing she had vague female problems, and was not told she had cancer; the secrecy kept the diagnosis from her and from the public during a presidential campaign. When abdominal pain returned in February 1952, her doctors confirmed, again without telling her, that the cancer had recurred. It shrank for a time with more radiotherapy, but the pain returned in May 1952, and chemotherapy failed. She died on July 26, 1952, at 33, never knowing that she had cervical cancer. The Pap smear, which can find cervical cancer at its earliest stage, was developed in 1942, but in 1950 it was not yet standard medical care in the United States or Argentina.
- **R:** A study by Nijensohn and colleagues, published online in 2011, reported evidence that she underwent a prefrontal lobotomy in Buenos Aires in the last months of her illness, for pain, anxiety and agitation from metastatic cancer; the evidence includes a neurosurgeon's memoirs and press interviews, accounts of people close to her, and skull radiographs. A later paper by the same author argued that the operation was also meant to change her behavior. The medical historian Barron Lerner, who reported the case in The Lancet in 2000, said it showed how far medical practice and ethics have changed: patients with cancer were then rarely told what they had, whereas most patients in the United States are now told their diagnosis.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
