Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. A thin chart. Almost nothing about his health is documented. The record holds what is documented (his baptism, family deaths whose causes are unrecorded, the will and its signatures, his death and burial, the cause unknown) and says plainly what is not. Every health interpretation is R: the shaky signatures, the vicar's story of a fever after drinking, the pipe residues, and the syphilis and mercury hypothesis. Past medical history is empty because no illness before the index date is documented.
  2. Calendar and dates. England used the Julian (Old Style) calendar throughout his life, ten days behind the Gregorian in his lifetime. Baptism 26 April 1564 (Julian) is 6 May 1564 (Gregorian); death 23 April 1616 (Julian) is 3 May 1616 (Gregorian); burial 25 April is 5 May; the will of 25 March 1616 (Julian, the first day of the year as then reckoned in England) is 4 April 1616 (Gregorian). The conversions were checked by computing Julian day numbers in the build script. The preferred death date is Gregorian, with the Julian date as a superseded alternative.
  3. Birth date not documented. The exact birth date is not recorded; 23 April is a tradition. The preferred birth is recorded as approximate (late April 1564, Julian), with the baptism and the traditional day as alternatives. The synthetic date of birth, May 3, 1974, uses the Gregorian equivalent of the traditional 23 April as a placeholder, a shift of 410 years; the true-date variant therefore shows a birth on 3 May 1564 (Gregorian), a modeling artifact, not a claim.
  4. Index age and dates. He is 51 at the index visit on January 12, 2026 (true frame 12 January 1616, Gregorian; 2 January, Julian), at Stratford, about when the will is thought to have been first drafted. The follow-ups are January 26 (consultation; true 16 January, Julian), February 23 (13 February, Julian) and March 23 (13 March, Julian), with the sick visit on February 2 and the preventive visit on February 16. Every visit falls before the will was revised and signed on 25 March (Julian) and before the illness that the National Archives essay places in late March; none contradicts a documented event. Goal targets are set for April 20, 2026, before his true death date. The window was long enough, so no earlier period was needed.
  5. Name. “William Shakespeare” splits simply into given and family names, so `identity.name_parts` is not used. The alias “William Shakspere” is the spelling of his baptism entry (“Gulielmus … Shakspere”) and of the burial entry (“Will Shakspere”); grep found it in no other record. Two existing records mention his surname in passing; they do not use his display name.
  6. Presenting problem and diagnosis. The intake problem is a tremor of the writing hand (R25.1), resolved on January 26, 2026, when essential tremor (G25.0) is recorded. The tremor is classified against the MDS 2018 consensus as essential tremor: bilateral upper-limb action tremor for at least three years, without other signs. Enhanced physiological tremor from alcohol rebound and caffeine is recorded as a contributing component, not the diagnosis; hyperthyroidism is excluded by TSH; early Parkinson disease is excluded clinically (no rest or re-emergent tremor, bradykinesia, or rigidity), so no MRI or dopamine transporter imaging is done. G25.0 is placed both_with_link to the shaky signatures (CLM-0006), which remain R; no tremor is documented in his lifetime.
  7. Alcohol. The vicar's story that he “drank too hard” is hearsay written down over forty years after his death (R, sensitive) and is used only as history. The modern episode gives him synthetic hazardous drinking (AUDIT 12, about 24 drinks a week) without an alcohol use disorder, coded Z72.89 (other problems related to lifestyle) rather than an F10 code because DSM-5-TR criteria are not met, so no safety assessment is required. A brief intervention (USPSTF, NIAAA) brings him within the limits of 14 a week and 4 a day, and GGT falls from 84 to 49 U/L. The teaching point is that alcohol transiently steadies essential tremor and should not be used to treat it.
  8. Roster leads left out or downgraded. The roster's “probable lead and mercury exposure” and “possible syphilis treatment (mercury traces)” are not supported by any documentary or physical evidence found; the only source is a 2005 hypothesis drawn from his writings (CLM-0010, R, disputed, sensitive), and no mercury test, problem, or exposure history is used. The pipe-residue study (CLM-0009, R, disputed, sensitive) found cannabis-like residues in pipes from his garden and cocaine only in pipes from elsewhere, and its authors say it does not show that he smoked them; the cocaine lead is therefore not attached to him, and the modern episode records no drug use. “Financial stress” and “retired at 49, possibly for health reasons” were not found in any source read and are left out.
  9. Treatment. Propranolol extended-release 60 mg, increased to 80 mg after a partial response at four weeks (AAN 2011, reaffirmed 2025; MDS 2019), with pulse, blood pressure, and beta-blocker safety at each visit; primidone is the documented alternative and is not used. Occupational therapy teaches weighted pens, forearm support, larger script, and dictation. TETRAS scores, the Archimedes spiral, and handwriting samples are kept in note text because they are not LOINC-coded here; AUDIT (75624-7) and PHQ-2 are coded surveys.
  10. Will and advance care planning. At the preventive visit he mentions that he is preparing a will and asks to discuss his medical wishes. The discussion is recorded in clinical terms only: a health care agent (an unnamed friend), a written directive, his values, and goals of care, with reassurance that a shaky signature does not reflect capacity.
  11. Sick and preventive visits. The sick visit is a common cold with a low-grade fever a week after starting propranolol, teaching no antibiotics, acetaminophen, and avoiding oral decongestants (which raise blood pressure and can worsen tremor) and alcohol-containing night syrups. It is not linked to the fever in the vicar's story. The preventive visit is `preventive_adult` at 51, with PCV20 and the first zoster dose, a fecal immunochemical test, lipid and HbA1c screening, alcohol follow-up, and fever and infection precautions.
  12. Focused visits. The three chronic-care follow-ups (two, six, and ten weeks after intake) are documented as focused visits on the AAN essential tremor guideline, the MDS classification and treatment review, and USPSTF and NIAAA alcohol guidance: TETRAS, spiral and handwriting at each visit, signs beyond tremor at diagnosis, TSH and metabolic tests, a pre-treatment cardiovascular baseline and beta-blocker safety, drinking reviews with GGT at ten weeks, and occupational therapy; primidone, imaging, and surgery are recorded as not indicated with reasons. One deviation is recorded as `deferred`, with its outcome and corrective action, and no result was invented: the occupational therapy evaluation due within two to four weeks of referral had not happened by the six-week visit because of a waiting list; there was no harm, another therapist was found, and two sessions were completed by the last visit.
  13. Consultation. The movement disorders neurology referral and consultation (Benedikt Osei-Carrow, MD, a fictional consultant, on the day of the second visit) are S. The consultant confirms essential tremor, finds no parkinsonism and no need for imaging, advises propranolol with titration, primidone as the alternative, occupational therapy, and keeping alcohol within limits; the record then shows propranolol increased to 80 mg, occupational therapy, and no imaging.
  14. Real people. His parents, wife, children, the vicar John Ward, and the poets named in Ward's note appear only in documented history claims. The modern episode attributes no decision, statement, or health fact to them; his health care agent is an unnamed, synthetic friend. Living scholars, archivists, and study authors are not named; their work is cited by title and publisher.
  15. Primary clinician. Rowena Achterberg-Sule, MD, is cast as his primary care physician.
  16. William Shakespeare died in 1616, so the recent-death review rule does not apply. Ordinary clinical, provenance, and safe-messaging reviews remain required.

CLM-0006The Folger Shakespeare Library describes the three signatures on the will as written in shaky strokes of the pen, suggestive of someone who had trouble holding a writing implement because of illness, and notes that if he was ill and signing from bed at an awkward angle the shakiness would not be remarkable; his other three signatures show similar slight variation. An archivist writing for The National Archives argued in 2016 that he was not seriously or chronically ill in January 1616 but probably fell seriously ill quite suddenly in late March and deteriorated over the next four weeks.possible

The researchers stressed that the results do not show that he smoked any of these pipes.disputed
