# Teaching Note — William Shakespeare pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** William Shakespeare was born in Stratford-upon-Avon in April 1564; the exact date of his birth is not recorded, although it is most often celebrated on 23 April. His baptism is recorded in the parish register of Holy Trinity Church on Wednesday 26 April 1564 (Julian; 6 May 1564, Gregorian), in Latin, as 'Gulielmus filius Johannes Shakspere'. Baptisms typically took place within three days of birth. His father, John Shakespeare, was a glover who also traded in wool and lent money, served on the town council, and became high bailiff in 1568. Before William was born his parents had lost two infant daughters, Joan within the first few weeks of her life and Margaret a year after she was born; a younger sister, Anne, died at the age of eight. At eighteen he married Anne Hathaway of Shottery; their daughter Susanna was born six months after the marriage, followed by the twins Judith and Hamnet in 1585. Hamnet died at the age of eleven. His wife and children remained in Stratford while he worked in London, and in 1597 he bought New Place, a large house in the centre of the town, which was his family home. His will, made on 25 March 1616 (Julian; 4 April, Gregorian) and proved on 22 June 1616, opens with the words 'in perfect health and memory, God be praised', a typical phrase of the time. It is written on three sheets, each bearing his signature; these are three of the six surviving examples of his signature. Its first leaf has 'January' struck out and replaced with 'March', and scholars have suggested that it was drafted in January and revised on 25 March. He died at Stratford on 23 April 1616 (Julian; 3 May 1616, Gregorian), the date given on his monument in Holy Trinity Church, and his burial is recorded in the parish register on 25 April 1616 (Julian) as 'Will Shakspere gent.' The Shakespeare Birthplace Trust states that the cause of his death is not known.
- **R:** The Folger Shakespeare Library describes the three signatures on the will as written in shaky strokes of the pen, suggestive of someone who had trouble holding a writing implement because of illness, and notes that if he was ill and signing from bed at an awkward angle the shakiness would not be remarkable; his other three signatures show similar slight variation. An archivist writing for The National Archives argued in 2016 that he was not seriously or chronically ill in January 1616 but probably fell seriously ill quite suddenly in late March and deteriorated over the next four weeks. John Ward, vicar of Stratford-upon-Avon and a physician, wrote in his notebook of 1662 to 1663 that 'Shakespeare, Drayton, and Ben Jonson had a merry meeting and it seems drank too hard for Shakespeare died of a fever there contracted'; the Folger Shakespeare Library calls it the only known account of his death. A 2001 chemical study of 24 fragments of seventeenth-century clay pipes from Stratford-upon-Avon and its surroundings reported residues suggestive of cannabis in eight, four of them from the garden of his property, and of Peruvian cocaine in two, neither of which came from his property. The researchers stressed that the results do not show that he smoked any of these pipes. A 2005 article in Clinical Infectious Diseases proposed, from his writings, the sonnets, and contemporary gossip, that he had syphilis and that a late-life decline in output, tremor, social withdrawal, and hair loss were due to mercury poisoning from its treatment.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
