D William Shakespeare was born in Stratford-upon-Avon in April 1564; the exact date of his birth is not recorded, although it is most often celebrated on 23 April. His baptism is recorded in the parish register of Holy Trinity Church on Wednesday 26 April 1564 (Julian; 6 May 1564, Gregorian), in Latin, as 'Gulielmus filius Johannes Shakspere'. Baptisms typically took place within three days of birth.
The birth date is undocumented; 23 April is a tradition.

D His will, made on 25 March 1616 (Julian; 4 April, Gregorian) and proved on 22 June 1616, opens with the words 'in perfect health and memory, God be praised', a typical phrase of the time. It is written on three sheets, each bearing his signature; these are three of the six surviving examples of his signature. Its first leaf has 'January' struck out and replaced with 'March', and scholars have suggested that it was drafted in January and revised on 25 March.
The formula does not establish his state of health.

R The Folger Shakespeare Library describes the three signatures on the will as written in shaky strokes of the pen, suggestive of someone who had trouble holding a writing implement because of illness, and notes that if he was ill and signing from bed at an awkward angle the shakiness would not be remarkable; his other three signatures show similar slight variation. An archivist writing for The National Archives argued in 2016 that he was not seriously or chronically ill in January 1616 but probably fell seriously ill quite suddenly in late March and deteriorated over the next four weeks.
Interpretations of handwriting and of the will's revisions; no symptom is recorded, and other explanations (posture, age, the act of signing at the foot of a sheet) are offered by the same source.

Calendars from around the world context
R John Ward, vicar of Stratford-upon-Avon and a physician, wrote in his notebook of 1662 to 1663 that 'Shakespeare, Drayton, and Ben Jonson had a merry meeting and it seems drank too hard for Shakespeare died of a fever there contracted'; the Folger Shakespeare Library calls it the only known account of his death.
Hearsay written down more than forty years after the death; often dismissed, though The National Archives' 2016 essay suggests it may deserve reconsideration. It is not evidence of his drinking habits.
 possible John Ward's notebook: only known account of Shakespeare's death and other references (Folger Shakespeare Library V.a.292, 1662-1663) supports
Shakespeare's will: a new interpretation (2016) context
R A 2001 chemical study of 24 fragments of seventeenth-century clay pipes from Stratford-upon-Avon and its surroundings reported residues suggestive of cannabis in eight, four of them from the garden of his property, and of Peruvian cocaine in two, neither of which came from his property. The researchers stressed that the results do not show that he smoked any of these pipes.
The pipes cannot be linked to him or dated to his household; the cocaine-bearing pipes came from elsewhere.
 disputed Doobee or not doobee? (1 January 2024), on the 2001 South African Journal of Science study of clay pipe residues from Stratford-upon-Avon supports
R A 2005 article in Clinical Infectious Diseases proposed, from his writings, the sonnets, and contemporary gossip, that he had syphilis and that a late-life decline in output, tremor, social withdrawal, and hair loss were due to mercury poisoning from its treatment.
A speculative hypothesis drawn from literary evidence; it was answered by letters in the same journal, and no documentary record of the disease, its treatment, or a tremor exists.
