# Teaching Note — Alexander the Great pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Plutarch, writing about four centuries later, says he was born early in the month Hecatombaeon (the Macedonian Lous), on the sixth day, the day the temple of Artemis at Ephesus burned; the Loeb editor dates this to 356 BC and notes that the month corresponds nearly to July, and that the date may have been moved to fit the coincidence. He was born at Pella in Macedonia, the son of King Philip II and Olympias of Epirus. Ancient historians record many battle wounds: a sword wound to the thigh at Issus, a catapult bolt through shield and breastplate into the shoulder at Gaza, from which he 'was not easily cured', and a blow to the head at the Granicus. In a speech that Arrian writes for him, he says that no part of the front of his body is free from wounds. Storming a town of the Malli in the Indus valley (326 or 325 BC), he was shot with an arrow under the breast through his breastplate into the chest; Arrian cites Ptolemy that air was breathed out from the wound with the blood. He fainted from loss of blood, the arrowhead lodged at a rib was cut out, he swooned again at its removal, and Plutarch says it brought him to death's door, but he recovered after a long time under regimen and treatment. In Cilicia in 333 BC he fell gravely ill, according to Aristobulus from his toils, and according to other writers after bathing in the cold river Cydnus, with convulsions, high fever and sleeplessness; his physician, Philip the Acarnanian, treated him and he recovered. The ancient historians describe long drinking parties with his Companions, especially in his later years, and disagreed about how much he drank: Aristobulus, quoted by Arrian, held that he 'was not a great wine-drinker' and sat long over wine for companionship, and Plutarch that he was 'less addicted to wine than was generally believed'; Plutarch also tells of a contest in drinking unmixed wine after which the winner and many others died. During a drinking party in Sogdiana (328 BC) he killed his officer Clitus. In October 324 BC at Ecbatana, during games and drinking parties, his closest companion Hephaestion fell sick with a fever and died on about the seventh day. The historians agree that his grief was great: until the third day he neither ate nor cared for his appearance, and the mourning lasted many days. Accounts that he hanged or crucified the physician differ. After the mourning he campaigned against the Cossaeans in the mountains in winter, and in the spring of 323 BC marched back to Babylon, meeting embassies on the road. At Babylon, after banquets at the house of Medius, he developed a fever that the court journal, as summarized by Arrian and Plutarch, followed day by day as it worsened until he could no longer speak; he died at about 32 years and 8 months. A Babylonian astronomical diary records 'The king died' on 29 Aiaru, which corresponds to June 11, 323 BC; Plutarch gives the 28th of Daesius, toward evening, which his editor dates to June 13.
- **R:** Physicians writing since have read the Malli wound as a tension pneumothorax with injury to the upper lobe of the left lung; others have argued that his recovery and return to the saddle within days make a pneumothorax unlikely. How he recovered is scarcely recorded. Whether he had what would now be called an alcohol use disorder is disputed. A psychiatric review of the ancient historians concluded that he periodically drank large quantities of undiluted wine to the point of pathological intoxication, but that the data do not give convincing evidence of abuse or dependence by DSM-IV or ICD-10 criteria, and that alcohol was unlikely to have caused his death; a medical historian has questioned retrospective diagnoses of this kind. The cause of his death is unknown and much argued. Modern proposals include typhoid fever (an NEJM clinicopathological discussion and a review that found the court journal's account most characteristic of typhoid), malaria, West Nile virus encephalitis, poisoning (Veratrum album thought the most plausible poison if he was poisoned), alcohol, and Guillain-Barre syndrome with a false diagnosis of death; each has been disputed. Plutarch thought the story of poisoning a fabrication.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
