# Teaching Note — Hubert H. Humphrey pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Hubert Horatio Humphrey Jr. was born on 27 May 1911 in Wallace, South Dakota. Humphrey served in the U.S. Senate from Minnesota from 1949 to 1964, as vice president from 1965 to 1969, and again in the Senate from 1971 until his death. Humphrey sought medical advice in 1967 for blood in his urine; some doctors suspected bladder cancer, others disagreed, and he was not treated. In 1968 doctors found and removed several pinhead-size nonmalignant growths from his bladder. In 1994 researchers reported a p53 gene mutation in bladder cells from a 1967 urine sample of his. In 1973 doctors found possibly cancerous tissue in his bladder and treated it with thiotepa and X-ray therapy, given five minutes a day for five weeks. After three years free of cancer, an examination at the National Naval Medical Center in Bethesda for symptoms of a urinary tract infection found a low-grade malignancy, and in October 1976 a six-hour radical cystectomy at Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer Center removed his bladder, prostate, lymph nodes, the fatty tissue around the bladder, and part of the urethra; the ureters were joined to a conduit made from a segment of small intestine, brought out to an opening on the skin to the right of the navel and drained into a bag. The nodes were to be examined for spread, and an experimental two-drug chemotherapy might be offered. He resumed his Senate duties in January 1977, serving as deputy president pro tempore from January 5, 1977. In August 1977 doctors found a malignant tumor on his pelvic bone during a four-hour operation in Minneapolis to relieve a bowel obstruction; the cancer was described as inoperable and terminal, and chemotherapy was planned. He returned to the Senate eleven weeks later, with further chemotherapy scheduled. Humphrey died on 13 January 1978 in Waverly, Minnesota.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
