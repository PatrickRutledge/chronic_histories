# Teaching Note — Hippocrates pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Plato names him in the Protagoras as 'Hippocrates of Cos, the Asclepiad', a physician who took pupils for a fee, and in the Phaedrus cites his method; Aristotle calls him 'the Great Hippocrates'. These are the earliest witnesses to him. The three ancient lives (by Soranus, a late writer, and in the Suda and Tzetzes, all written many centuries after him) say that he was born on Cos in 460 BC, that his father Heraclides was a physician of the Asclepiad guild and taught him, and that he travelled widely in Greece; the Loeb editor finds 'a certain amount of fable' in them but nothing improbable in their broad outline. According to the Suda he died in Thessaly and was buried at Larissa. The Hippocratic Corpus is not the work of one man. The Loeb editor describes it as the remains of a library of works 'of various dates and of different schools', a great part of which is 'neither by Hippocrates himself nor by his school'; Aristotle ascribed one of its treatises to Polybus, and Hippocrates is 'a name without writings'. Which treatises, if any, he wrote is still debated. Modern medicine regards him as its founder: reviews call him 'the father of modern medicine' and 'the founding father of clinical medicine', for medicine based on observation of clinical signs rather than on religious or magical beliefs.
- **R:** How long he lived is not known: the traditions make him live 85, 90, 104 or 109 years, and the Suda says about 104. Some stories in the lives are legend; the Loeb editor notes that the tale of his staying the plague at Athens contradicts Thucydides. Nothing about his own health is documented in the sources read: no illness, injury or cause of death is recorded. The roster's 'longevity; natural aging' is a lead only, resting on the disputed traditions of his age.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
