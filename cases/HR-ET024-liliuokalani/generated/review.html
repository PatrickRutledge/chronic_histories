Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Sources. The case relies on the Liliʻuokalani Trust (the trust she founded), the State of Hawaiʻi's Washington Place timeline, Public Law 103-150, her own 1898 memoir and a Rutgers University Libraries article. ʻIolani Palace's website, the Hawaiʻi State Archives' digital collections, the Library of Congress and its Chronicling America newspapers (including the Honolulu Star-Bulletin report of her death) could not be fetched (blocked). Wikipedia and popular sites found in searches were not used.
  2. Recent-death rule. She died in 1917, so the rule does not apply. A sick visit is included.
  3. Final illness. The Liliʻuokalani Trust says she “dies from a stroke at the age of 79,” and a Rutgers University Libraries article that she “suffered a stroke and died in 1917”; Washington Place gives the date and place. No source read gives the date of the stroke, whether there was more than one, or a decline over months; secondary accounts that describe failing health in the weeks before her death were not used because their sources could not be read. The claim (CLM-0012) is D with certainty “probable” and states what is not documented; no claim about strokes earlier in 1917 or a months-long decline is made. The modern minor stroke (I63.412) is linked to this claim (placement both_with_link); nothing in the record says she had atrial fibrillation or a stroke in March 1917.
  4. Index age and dates. The index visit on March 16, 2026 maps to March 16, 1917, when she was 78 and living at Washington Place in Honolulu, her home for 55 years. The visits on March 17, April 20, May 18, June 15, July 13 and August 24, 2026 map to the same days of 1917, before her 79th birthday and well before her death on November 11, 1917. Her day-to-day whereabouts in 1917 are not documented in the sources read; nothing places her away from Honolulu. The synthetic date of birth is 1947-09-02, a shift of 109 years. No medical history before 1917 is documented in the sources read (her memoir mentions periods of poor health without diagnoses), so the past medical history is empty.
  5. Name. She is known by a single name, Liliʻuokalani, written throughout with the ʻokina (U+02BB) in the display name, aliases and notes. identity.name_parts is used, as for other people known by a single name: her single name is in the family-name field with no given name, so the C-CDA and FHIR names are not split. The notes call her Liliʻuokalani, as she asked in the modern episode, not “Mrs. Dominis.” The aliases are her birth name in the Liliʻuokalani Trust's form, Lydia Liliʻu Loloku Walania Wewehi Kamakaʻeha (the roster brief omitted “Wewehi”), and her baptismal name, Lydia Kamakaʻeha Pākī (the Trust and Washington Place). Neither appears in any other record. The married name “Lydia K. Dominis” was not used because no source read gives it in that form; English-language sources of the period often omit the ʻokina and kahakō, as in Public Law 103-150 and her memoir, and those quotations are left as written.
  6. Overthrow and imprisonment. The overthrow, imprisonment and forced abdication are documented (CLM-0007, CLM-0008) and described in the words of the 1993 Apology Resolution where possible. They are recorded as history and in a trauma-informed social history, not as symptoms; no trauma diagnosis is made. The modern clinicians acknowledge this history when she raises it and do not probe it.
  7. Native Hawaiian healing. Lomilomi through Ke Ola Mamo, a real Native Hawaiian Health Care System that lists lomilomi among its services, and hoʻoponopono with her ʻohana as they choose, are offered alongside clinic care. No practice or ceremony is described. Whether Ke Ola Mamo offers hoʻoponopono was not checked, so the record does not say it does. The one clinical interaction recorded is that a bodywork practitioner should be told she takes an anticoagulant and use gentle pressure.
  8. ʻOhana and other people. Her ʻohana members, the member of her household who drives her, her health care agent (whom she raised as hānai) and her minister are synthetic and unnamed, and no relative or historical person is given a modern statement, decision or health fact. Her parents, hānai parents, husband, brother and the officials of 1893 appear only in claims. The decisions in the modern episode are hers.
  9. Care setting. The project facility name (St. Posthumous Health Center) is kept; in the notes it is a clinic in Honolulu with an affiliated emergency department and a rapid-access stroke clinic. Her modern home is Washington Place, following the documented history, although it is now the Governor's residence; this is a modeling choice, not a claim.
  10. Presenting problem and diagnosis. The presenting problem is transient right hand weakness and word-finding difficulty with a suspected TIA (G45.9, verification provisional), resolved at the stroke clinic the next day when diffusion MRI showed an infarct: a minor ischemic stroke by the tissue-based definition (I63.412, embolic pattern in the left middle cerebral artery territory). Paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (I48.0) was recorded at five weeks from the 30-day monitor. Hypertension (I10) was recorded at intake from her history of untreated borderline readings.
  11. Atrial fibrillation decision. Atrial fibrillation is found (six episodes, burden about 3%, none felt) and anticoagulation is started: apixaban 5 mg twice daily (CHA2DS2-VASc 6, HAS-BLED 2), full dose because she meets only one dose-reduction criterion (weight 58 kg). Clopidogrel was stopped the same day; aspirin had stopped at day 21.
  12. Clinical judgment calls. (1) Same-day emergency evaluation for an ABCD2 score of 5 with presentation about 18 hours after onset, and dual antiplatelet loading within 24 hours after CT. (2) MRI and echocardiogram at the stroke clinic the next morning rather than the same day. (3) 21 days of aspirin and clopidogrel (CHANCE), then clopidogrel alone until anticoagulation. (4) Atorvastatin 40 mg rather than 80 mg in a 78-year-old woman weighing 58 kg; LDL 132 to 64 mg/dL. (5) Losartan 25 mg started one week after the event, when home readings averaged 138/82. (6) No carotid procedure or follow-up imaging for a plaque under 50%. (7) Apixaban started at five weeks, when atrial fibrillation was found. (8) The nosebleed managed without stopping apixaban, with cautery offered and declined in favor of moisturizing.
