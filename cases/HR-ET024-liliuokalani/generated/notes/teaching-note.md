# Teaching Note — Liliʻuokalani pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Liliʻuokalani was born on September 2, 1838, in Honolulu, at her family's home at Pūowaina, to Analeʻa Keohokālole and Caesar Kapaʻakea; in her memoir she wrote that she was born near the site of the later Queen's Hospital, on Sept. 2, 1838. Her birth name was Lydia Liliʻu Loloku Walania Wewehi Kamakaʻeha. She became the hānai (adopted) daughter of High Chief Abner Pākī and Chiefess Laura Kōnia, and was baptized as a Christian with the name Lydia Kamakaʻeha Pākī. From 1842 she was educated at the Chiefs' Children's School, founded by Kamehameha III for young royals. In 1862 she married John Owen Dominis and moved to Washington Place in Honolulu, where she lived for 55 years, until her death; her husband died in 1891. She was a musician and composer of more than 150 songs, including "Aloha ʻOe" (1878) and He Mele Lāhui Hawaiʻi, the kingdom's national anthem from 1866; she played the piano and ukulele and wrote musical notation. She became queen in 1891, on the death of her brother King Kalākaua, the last reigning monarch of the Hawaiian Kingdom. On January 16, 1893, the United States Minister and naval representatives caused armed naval forces of the United States to invade the sovereign Hawaiian nation and position themselves near ʻIolani Palace to intimidate the Queen and her government; on January 17 the kingdom was overthrown, and to avoid bloodshed she yielded her authority, under protest, to the United States rather than to the Provisional Government. After a failed attempt to restore her to the throne in 1895, she was arrested and imprisoned in ʻIolani Palace, where on January 24, 1895, she was forced by representatives of the Republic of Hawaii to abdicate; she was held in the palace for about eight months, translating the Kumulipo and composing songs, and was later released and pardoned. Her memoir captions a picture of the palace: "My palace was my prison." A lifelong Christian and member of Kawaiahaʻo Church, she was baptized into the Episcopal faith in 1896. The United States annexed Hawaiʻi in 1898, the year she published her memoir, Hawaii's Story by Hawaii's Queen; in 1909 she executed a Deed of Trust for the benefit of orphan and other destitute children in the Hawaiian Islands. In 1993, on the 100th anniversary of the overthrow, the U.S. Congress apologized to Native Hawaiians on behalf of the people of the United States for the overthrow of the Kingdom of Hawaii (Public Law 103-150). She died at Washington Place on November 11, 1917, at the age of 79, from a stroke; the date, number and course of the stroke or strokes before her death are not given in the sources read. She lay in state in the Throne Room of ʻIolani Palace and at Kawaiahaʻo Church and was placed in the Royal Mausoleum at Mauna ʻAla; her death marked the end of the Kalākaua dynasty.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
