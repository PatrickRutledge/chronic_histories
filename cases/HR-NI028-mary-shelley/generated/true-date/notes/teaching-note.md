# Teaching Note — Mary Shelley pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Mary Wollstonecraft Godwin was born in London on August 30, 1797, the only child of the writer Mary Wollstonecraft and the philosopher William Godwin. Her mother died in London on September 10, 1797, eleven days after the birth, of puerperal fever; her father wrote that the placenta had been retained and had to be removed by hand. Her first child, a daughter born prematurely in February 1815, died after a few days. She began the tale that became Frankenstein in Switzerland in the summer of 1816, and Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus was published anonymously in January 1818, when she was 20. Her daughter Clara died in Italy in 1818, at about one year, and her son William died in Italy in June 1819, at three; she then had no living children. Her son Percy Florence, born in November 1819, was the only one of her four children to survive childhood. In June 1822 she nearly died of hemorrhage after a miscarriage about three months into a pregnancy; her husband stopped the bleeding by placing her in ice baths. In July 1822 her husband, the poet Percy Bysshe Shelley, drowned while sailing in the Gulf of Spezia, leaving her a widow before she was 25, with a son of two; she decided to earn her living by her writing and by publishing his. She published the novels Valperga (1823) and The Last Man (1826), a revised Frankenstein (1831), and Rambles in Germany and Italy (1844). At about fourteen she had a very painful skin condition of one entire arm, treated with poultices; at times she could not move the arm and wore it in a sling. It has been interpreted as eczema, psoriasis, or chickenpox. In her journal in June 1840 she wrote that she had been long oppressed by care, disappointment, and ill health, and that lately ill health and extreme depression had marred what little she could do for others. From 1839 she had recurrent severe headaches of varying severity, site, and duration, with dizziness, pain in the legs, right hand, and right eye, brief episodes of partial paralysis of the right leg and hand, tremors, and convulsive seizures. Her physicians called the illness psychosomatic in 1842, a functional derangement of the nerves or brain in 1845, and neuralgia of the heart in 1846. From 1846 to 1851 she lived at 24 Chester Square, Belgravia, London, and spent time at Field Place in Sussex, the home of her son and his wife after their marriage in 1848. Her symptoms worsened from late 1849, with impaired speech and difficulty moving; her nervous ailments increased until she lost power in one side, and she could not be moved from her house in Chester Square. She died at Chester Square, London, on February 1, 1851, aged 53. Her death certificate recorded "Disease of the Brain Supposed Tumour in left hemisphere, of long standing." No autopsy was done.
- **R:** Her death has long been attributed to a brain tumour: the Bodleian Libraries describe a long illness possibly caused by a brain tumour, and a reference biography says she succumbed to one. A 2018 Lancet Neurology letter and a 2022 paper argue instead that twelve years of recurrent episodes make a tumour unlikely and that she had hemiplegic migraine with recurrent and finally fatal stroke; the 2022 paper links these to the skin disease of her adolescence.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
