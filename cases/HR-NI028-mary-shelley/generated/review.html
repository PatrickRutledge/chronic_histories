Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Birth, name, and aliases. She was born Mary Wollstonecraft Godwin in London on August 30, 1797 (Gregorian; Britain had adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1752). "Mary Wollstonecraft Godwin" and "Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley" are her aliases; "Shelley" alone is not, because it is also her husband's name.
  2. Her mother's death. The brief said eleven days after the birth, of puerperal fever. Her father's memoir gives the birth on August 30 and the death on September 10, eleven days later, after a retained placenta removed by hand, and a reference entry gives puerperal fever as the cause. The Bodleian timeline and a reference biography say ten days. The record follows the dates in the memoir.
  3. Index age and dates. Her documented headaches with transient right-sided paralysis began in 1839, and her illness worsened from late 1849 until her death on February 1, 1851. The index visit on March 3, 2026 maps to March 3, 1849, when she was 51. The synthetic date of birth is August 30, 1974 (shift 177 years). The other visits (March 17, April 28, July 7, August 11, September 29, and October 20, 2026) map to the same days of 1849, before the documented worsening of late 1849. She lived at 24 Chester Square from 1846 and spent time at Field Place, her son's home after his 1848 marriage; an 1890 biography adds a trip to Nice in about 1849. The record does not place any visit at a particular address.
  4. Date of death. The Bodleian timeline and the Gale biography give February 1, 1851; the 1889 and 1890 biographies give February 21. February 1 is preferred and February 21 is recorded as a conflicting alternative.
  5. Cause of death recorded as R. The death certificate's words, "Disease of the Brain Supposed Tumour in left hemisphere, of long standing," are documented (D), as quoted in a 2022 paper; no autopsy was done. That she had a brain tumour is a retrospective interpretation (R, possible). A 2018 Lancet Neurology letter and a 2022 paper argue that twelve years of recurrent episodes fit hemiplegic migraine with recurrent and finally fatal stroke better than a tumour; this is recorded as a competing R claim (disputed).
  6. Meningioma not found in fetched sources. The roster and the brief mention a proposed meningioma. No fetched source proposed it: a 1999 World J Surg paper on her last illness (PMID 10501885) has no abstract and could not be read, and a blog quoting a letters edition was not used. The modern meningioma is a synthetic choice, linked to her documented symptoms and to both R claims; its provenance says it is not her historical diagnosis.
  7. Modern diagnosis decided. A 3.1 cm left frontoparietal convexity meningioma over the hand area of the motor cortex, with moderate vasogenic edema, causing focal aware seizures of the right hand and mild right-hand weakness. The left hemisphere and right-sided symptoms match her documented right-sided episodes and the certificate. She chose craniotomy and Simpson grade I resection after observation and radiosurgery were explained; pathology showed a meningothelial meningioma, CNS WHO grade 1, without TERT promoter mutation or CDKN2A/B deletion, so no radiation was given. Dexamethasone was used only briefly around surgery. Surveillance is MRI at three months, then yearly to five years, then every two years, combining the NCCN and EANO schedules.
  8. Antiseizure medicine. Levetiracetam was started at the diagnosis visit, not at presentation, because the nature of the spells was uncertain until the MRI and EEG a week later; the record shows one further focal seizure in between. The irritability and low mood after surgery (PHQ-9 9) are attributed in part to levetiracetam; she chose to continue it, and they resolved. A taper is to be discussed at one year after an EEG.
  9. Driving. The advice follows her state's seizure-free requirement, assumed to be three months, and neurosurgical clearance after craniotomy; no specific state law is cited.
  10. Grief and depression. Her many documented losses (her mother, three of her four children, her husband, and a near-fatal miscarriage) and her 1840 journal entry on "extreme depression" are D. The modern episode screens for depression at each visit (PHQ-9 never above 9, item 9 always 0), takes a grief history, and offers counseling, which she completes. No behavioral-health diagnosis is made, so no structured safety assessment is required. Prolonged grief disorder is not diagnosed, because the losses are decades old and not disabling.
  11. Relatives. Her husband, children, son, and daughter-in-law appear only in documented claims and as unnamed supports. No modern statement or decision is attributed to her son or anyone historical. The children's documented causes of death are left out, and no health facts about them are used. Her health care agent is not named.
  12. Her writing. Her work as a novelist, travel writer, and editor of her husband's poems is echoed in the modern episode, which makes protection of her right hand and her writing a goal.
  13. Not used. The Keats-Shelley House pages and the biographies by Miranda Seymour and Fiona Sampson were not fetched directly; Sampson is used only as quoted in the 2022 paper.   16. Focused visits.
