# Teaching Note — Lili Boulanger pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Lili Boulanger was born Marie-Juliette Olga Boulanger on August 21, 1893, in the 9th arrondissement of Paris. She was a composer, the sister of the composer and teacher Nadia Boulanger (1887–1979). At about two years old, in 1895, she had pneumonia, described as bronchial pneumonia; her health was frail from a very young age and she remained sickly for the rest of her life. In 1913, at 19, she became the first woman to win the Premier Grand Prix de Rome for musical composition, with the cantata Faust et Hélène; she had been the only woman among the five finalists that May. She went to the Villa Medici in Rome in 1914. The outbreak of war made her leave Rome for Nice, where she composed the song cycle Clairières dans le ciel; she stayed in Rome again in 1916 and then returned to France. Early in 1915, living in Paris, she began to have headaches, loss of appetite, and fever, followed by symptoms resembling colitis and appendicitis; she spent much of her time in bed but kept composing. During the war she and her sister helped to found the Franco-American Committee of the Conservatoire, which kept mobilized students in touch with the Conservatoire. After her return to Paris in 1917 she had an operation at the end of July; the operation and its findings are not named in the source read. In her last illness, unable to hold a pen, she dictated her music to her sister. She died on March 15, 1918, at Mézy-sur-Seine (Yvelines), at 24.
- **R:** The usual account is that the childhood pneumonia left her immune system weakened, so that she never fully recovered. Her chronic intestinal illness has been described as intestinal tuberculosis, and several reference works give tuberculosis as the cause of her death. Later writers have proposed that her chronic intestinal illness was Crohn disease, which was not described as a distinct disease until well after her death.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
