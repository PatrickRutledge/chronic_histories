Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Recent-death rule. He died in 1976, so the rule does not apply. Claims are limited to what the fetched sources state. Every claim about his mental health, suicide attempt, psychiatric treatment and the allegation about intelligence agencies, and the claim about the federal files, is marked sensitive_content true. No living person is named.
  2. Name. “Paul Robeson” splits simply into given and family names, so identity.name_parts is not used. The aliases “Paul Leroy Robeson” (Rutgers) and “Paul LeRoy Robeson” (the Robeson House) appear in no other record.
  3. Correction to the brief: onset of depression. The brief dates severe depression from about 1956. The fetched account of Duberman’s biography dates his first breakdown to 1955, after problems related to prostate surgery, with a period of driven energy followed by deep depression (CLM-0008). The record follows the source; the modern history describes a first depression about two years before the index visit and the current episode as a recurrence.
  4. Strength of the psychiatric sources (judgment call). The New York Times obituary could not be fetched, the Library of Congress and Britannica returned access errors, and no NMAAHC or National Archives page on his passport or illness was found; Duberman’s book was not read. The psychiatric history therefore rests on secondary accounts: a Psychology Today essay summarizing Duberman, the Kirkus review of Duberman, and a blog quoting his son’s 2010 book for the figure of 54 treatments. These claims are graded D with certainty “probable”, not “confirmed”, and should be checked against Duberman’s book. The Priory admission in September 1961 and the return in December 1963 come from the blog’s quotation of the son’s book; the East Berlin hospital comes from the Psychology Today essay. The name of the East Berlin clinic, and the claim that a relative consented to the London treatment, were seen only in unreliable search results and are left out.
  5. The 1961 suicide attempt. Recorded once (CLM-0010), without method, and after the index date; it does not appear in intake documents. The source’s wording about the method is not repeated, in line with safe messaging.
  6. Bipolar disorder (judgment call). The documented 1955 period of driven energy followed by depression, and one writer’s view that he had “classic symptoms of bipolar disorder” (CLM-0009, R, possible), call for bipolar screening before an antidepressant. The modern episode records a negative MDQ (5 of 13 items, not in one period) and a structured interview that finds no reduced need for sleep, elation, irritability or risky behavior in the earlier period, so major depressive disorder, recurrent, severe, without psychotic features (F33.2) is diagnosed and he is watched for activation at every visit. This is a synthetic modern assessment, not a judgment on his real history; a reviewer may prefer to record bipolar spectrum disorder as unresolved.
  7. The son’s allegation. His son’s belief that he was drugged by United States intelligence and treated by a doctor linked to MK-Ultra (CLM-0014) is recorded as R, disputed, and not adopted. The modern episode makes no claim about it. The federal files on him (CLM-0015, D, probable) are recorded separately because they support treating his modern concern about surveillance as realistic.
  8. ECT and the contrast with his documented care (judgment call). The modern episode decides that ECT is not needed now (no psychosis, refusal of food or drink, or acute suicidal risk, and his own choice of augmentation first), documents a full informed consent discussion, and keeps ECT available on equal terms because Black patients receive it less often (Sprenger 2025). The contrast with what is documented about the many treatments he later received in London is made in the claim’s clinical interpretation (CLM-0011) and in the teaching objective, not in the modern notes, because the visits fall in 1957 in the true frame and the intake documents must not show later events. The record does not judge whether his treatment met the standards of its time and does not adopt the allegations about it. His own stated wish, recorded at the preventive visit, that any future ECT be his own informed decision is synthetic.
  9. Index age and dates. The index visit on March 9, 2026 maps to March 9, 1957, when he was 58, after the 1955 breakdown and the 1956 HUAC hearing and while his passport was withheld, so he was in the United States. The synthetic date of birth is April 9, 1967 (shift 69 years). The last visit, October 5, 2026, maps to October 5, 1957, before the passport was restored in 1958. No fetched source gives his exact residence in 1957; the modern clinic is placed in New York City as a teaching setting, not a claim about where he lived.   10. Presenting problem.   11. Safety assessment. Required by the F33.2 code and the PHQ-9 of 22. Six weeks later, at the preventive visit, the PHQ-9 was 6: a response, not yet remission. Behavioral activation ran throughout.
  13. Focused visits.
