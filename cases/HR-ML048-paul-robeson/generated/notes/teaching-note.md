# Teaching Note — Paul Robeson pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Paul Leroy Robeson was born on April 9, 1898, in Princeton, New Jersey. His father, the Rev. William Drew Robeson, was a minister, and his mother was Maria Louisa Bustill. In 1915 he was awarded a four-year academic scholarship to Rutgers, where he was elected to Phi Beta Kappa and graduated as valedictorian in 1919; he had been named to college All-America football teams. In 1923 he earned a law degree from Columbia Law School. In 1950 the State Department cancelled his passport, saying his overseas travel was 'contrary to the best interests of the United States'. His passport was restored in 1958, after eight years, following a Supreme Court ruling in a similar case that the withdrawal of a passport violated a citizen's constitutionally protected liberty. He was blacklisted: his concerts were cancelled, record companies stopped recording him, his name was struck from the All-America teams and newsreel footage of him was destroyed, and his yearly income fell from more than $150,000 to less than $3,000. In 1956 he was called before the House Un-American Activities Committee, where he invoked the Fifth Amendment and closed by saying, 'You gentlemen belong with the Alien and Sedition Acts, and you are the nonpatriots, and you are the un-Americans, and you ought to be ashamed of yourselves.' His biographer Martin Duberman describes his first breakdown in 1955, after health problems related to prostate surgery: a period of intense, driven energy that was followed by a deep depression. In March 1961, in Moscow, he became acutely paranoid and suicidal and made a suicide attempt. From September 1961 he was treated at the Priory clinic in London for nearly two years, where he received a large number of electroconvulsive treatments, 54 by his son's later count, in five courses over about twenty months, together with heavy doses of drugs; reviewers of the Duberman biography note electroshock for recurrent depression and suicide attempts. He later spent time in a psychiatric hospital in East Berlin and returned to the United States in December 1963. Illness ended his professional career in 1961. Federal agencies kept files on him: the CIA opened a file in 1943, and the FBI kept a 'status of health' file on him between April and June 1961; his biographer drew on his FBI files. From 1966 he lived in Philadelphia under the care of his sister Marian until his death, and lived as a recluse. He died of complications of a stroke on January 23, 1976, in Philadelphia, at the age of 77.
- **R:** A writer reviewing the Duberman biography has argued that he had the classic symptoms of bipolar disorder; no formal diagnosis of bipolar disorder in his lifetime is quoted. His son, Paul Robeson Jr., believed that his father had been poisoned by United States intelligence with a mind-altering drug and that a doctor who treated him had links to the MK-Ultra program.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
