## Editorial resolutions

- **Recent-death rule.** He died in 1976, so the rule does not apply. Claims are limited to what the fetched sources state. Every claim about his mental health, suicide attempt, psychiatric treatment and the allegation about intelligence agencies, and the claim about the federal files, is marked sensitive_content true. No living person is named.
- **Name.** “Paul Robeson” splits simply into given and family names, so identity.name_parts is not used. The aliases “Paul Leroy Robeson” (Rutgers) and “Paul LeRoy Robeson” (the Robeson House) appear in no other record.
- **Correction to the brief: onset of depression.** The brief dates severe depression from about 1956. The fetched account of Duberman’s biography dates his first breakdown to 1955, after problems related to prostate surgery, with a period of driven energy followed by deep depression (CLM-0008). The record follows the source; the modern history describes a first depression about two years before the index visit and the current episode as a recurrence.
- **Strength of the psychiatric sources (judgment call).** The New York Times obituary could not be fetched, the Library of Congress and Britannica returned access errors, and no NMAAHC or National Archives page on his passport or illness was found; Duberman’s book was not read. The psychiatric history therefore rests on secondary accounts: a *Psychology Today* essay summarizing Duberman, the Kirkus review of Duberman, and a blog quoting his son’s 2010 book for the figure of 54 treatments. These claims are graded D with certainty “probable”, not “confirmed”, and should be checked against Duberman’s book. The Priory admission in September 1961 and the return in December 1963 come from the blog’s quotation of the son’s book; the East Berlin hospital comes from the *Psychology Today* essay. The name of the East Berlin clinic, and the claim that a relative consented to the London treatment, were seen only in unreliable search results and are left out.
- **The 1961 suicide attempt.** Recorded once (CLM-0010), without method, and after the index date; it does not appear in intake documents. The source’s wording about the method is not repeated, in line with safe messaging.
- **Bipolar disorder (judgment call).** The documented 1955 period of driven energy followed by depression, and one writer’s view that he had “classic symptoms of bipolar disorder” (CLM-0009, R, possible), call for bipolar screening before an antidepressant. The modern episode records a negative MDQ (5 of 13 items, not in one period) and a structured interview that finds no reduced need for sleep, elation, irritability or risky behavior in the earlier period, so major depressive disorder, recurrent, severe, without psychotic features (F33.2) is diagnosed and he is watched for activation at every visit. This is a synthetic modern assessment, not a judgment on his real history; a reviewer may prefer to record bipolar spectrum disorder as unresolved.
- **The son’s allegation.** His son’s belief that he was drugged by United States intelligence and treated by a doctor linked to MK-Ultra (CLM-0014) is recorded as **R**, disputed, and not adopted. The modern episode makes no claim about it. The federal files on him (CLM-0015, D, probable) are recorded separately because they support treating his modern concern about surveillance as realistic.
- **ECT and the contrast with his documented care (judgment call).** The modern episode decides that ECT is not needed now (no psychosis, refusal of food or drink, or acute suicidal risk, and his own choice of augmentation first), documents a full informed consent discussion, and keeps ECT available on equal terms because Black patients receive it less often (Sprenger 2025). The contrast with what is documented about the many treatments he later received in London is made in the claim’s clinical interpretation (CLM-0011) and in the teaching objective, not in the modern notes, because the visits fall in 1957 in the true frame and the intake documents must not show later events. The record does not judge whether his treatment met the standards of its time and does not adopt the allegations about it. His own stated wish, recorded at the preventive visit, that any future ECT be his own informed decision is synthetic.
- **Index age and dates.** The index visit on March 9, 2026 maps to March 9, 1957, when he was 58, after the 1955 breakdown and the 1956 HUAC hearing and while his passport was withheld, so he was in the United States. The synthetic date of birth is April 9, 1967 (shift 69 years). The last visit, October 5, 2026, maps to October 5, 1957, before the passport was restored in 1958. No fetched source gives his exact residence in 1957; the modern clinic is placed in New York City as a teaching setting, not a claim about where he lived. He turns 59 between the third and fourth visits.
- **Presenting problem.** Three months of low mood, exhaustion, poor sleep and withdrawal at intake is its own problem (R53.83, other fatigue), resolved at the psychiatric consultation three days later, when F33.2 is recorded. The intake documents show only what was known then. The persecution is coded at intake as Z60.5; the code’s word “perceived” is the ICD-10-CM display, while the display of the problem states that the persecution is documented.
- **Safety assessment.** Required by the F33.2 code and the PHQ-9 of 22. It records passive ideation without intent, plan, preparation or past attempt, no firearm in the home, medicines dispensed in limited supplies and locked (venlafaxine is more dangerous in overdose than an SSRI), a written safety plan and the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Safety is reviewed at every later visit, including the sick and preventive visits.
- **Treatment sequence (judgment call).** Sertraline was raised to 200 mg and judged after five weeks at that dose (twelve weeks in all): a 23 percent fall in the PHQ-9, a non-response, so he was switched to venlafaxine rather than augmented. After seven weeks at venlafaxine 225 mg the PHQ-9 had fallen by 41 percent, a partial response after two adequate trials of different classes, and aripiprazole was added, with the CMID metabolic and movement monitoring. Six weeks later, at the preventive visit, the PHQ-9 was 6: a response, not yet remission. Behavioral activation ran throughout.
- **Focused visits.** The four chronic-care follow-ups (the consultation visit, six weeks, three months and twenty-four weeks) are documented as focused visits on the CMID depression monograph, the APA 2019 and VA/DoD 2022 depression guidelines, the VA/DoD 2024 suicide-risk guideline and the APA ECT task force reports. One deviation is recorded at the six-week visit instead of an invented result: the PHQ-9 due about four weeks into treatment was not collected, because the two-week nurse call checked side effects and safety only and no visit was booked; no harm judging by the six-week score and safety review; the psychologist now rates the PHQ-9 at each session and reports any rise or positive item 9 the same day. Those session scores are described in words, not recorded as observations.
- **Consultation.** The psychiatric referral and the consultation by Esther Lindqvist-Adebayo, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the second visit, are **S**. The consultant answers the six referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: recurrent severe depression without psychotic features and without bipolar disorder; his concern about surveillance treated as realistic; outpatient care with a safety plan; behavioral activation and sertraline to 200 mg with measurement-based care; a switch to venlafaxine for non-response and augmentation for a partial response; and ECT reserved for urgent indications or failed augmentation, given only with his consent, with rTMS and esketamine as alternatives.
- **Equity and culturally responsive care.** The record cites the documented under-treatment and greater chronicity of depression in Black Americans (Williams 2007), the over-diagnosis of psychotic disorders in Black patients (2014 review) and the lower use of ECT in Black patients (Sprenger 2025), and acts on them: structured diagnosis, his account of surveillance recorded as realistic, measurement at every visit, a therapist of his choosing, attention to the confidentiality he asks about, social work for lost income, and ECT offered on equal terms.
- **Real people.** His parents, his sister, his son and his biographer appear only in documented history claims and sources. No modern statement, decision or health fact is attributed to them, and no relative is the health care agent; the agent is unnamed. Family history is left empty because no family medical history was found in the fetched sources.
- **Law degree.** Recorded as a documented education fact only (CLM-0004); the modern social history says he is trained in law.
- **Sick visit.** Acute bronchitis on venlafaxine, chosen because it teaches a common, dangerous interaction (dextromethorphan with an SNRI and serotonin syndrome) and the effect of decongestants on blood pressure with venlafaxine, and because antibiotics are not indicated.
