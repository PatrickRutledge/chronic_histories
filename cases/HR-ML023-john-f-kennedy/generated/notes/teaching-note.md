# Teaching Note — John F. Kennedy pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** John F. Kennedy was born on May 29, 1917, in Brookline, Massachusetts. A few months before his third birthday he had a serious case of scarlet fever and spent a month in the hospital; through childhood and adolescence he had nausea, joint pain, headaches and diarrhea and was hospitalized several times. By 1934 he was sent to the Mayo Clinic, where his bowel illness was diagnosed as colitis. During his college years at Harvard his abdominal pain, weight loss and weakness continued despite a bland diet and strong medicines, including steroids placed under the skin to dissolve. His chronic back pain began at Harvard, after he was tackled from the side in a football game, possibly damaging a spinal disc; he began wearing a corset brace to stabilize his spine. His back and intestinal problems caused him to fail Army and Navy physical examinations, but he was admitted to the Navy in October 1941. In August 1943 the patrol torpedo boat he commanded, PT-109, was struck by a Japanese destroyer; he led the survivors to safety, and his back worsened as a result. He came home from the war in the Pacific thin and sallow from malaria. Against the advice of his doctors at the Mayo Clinic, he had spinal surgery in June 1944 at New England Baptist Hospital; about six weeks later the pain returned. In 1946, at 29, he was elected to the U.S. House of Representatives from a Boston district. During the campaign his back was so bad that he wore a brace and used daily hot baths and massage. In 1947, at 30, while a congressman, he collapsed on a visit to England; a doctor there diagnosed Addison's disease and told one of his friends that he might not live a year. Returning to America aboard the Queen Mary, he was so ill that a priest was called to give him the last rites. He passed off the illness as a recurrence of his wartime malaria. In Boston an endocrinologist began treating him for Addison's disease with pellets of a synthetic adrenal hormone, desoxycorticosterone acetate (DOCA), implanted under his skin. By 1950, when cortisone was available by mouth, he also took 25 mg of cortisone each day. In October 1954 he had a spinal fusion with a metal plate; he developed a urinary tract infection and became so ill that a priest was called to give him the last rites, and he later developed a staphylococcal wound infection. In February 1955 the plate was removed. He was diagnosed with hypothyroidism during a hospital stay in New York in 1955, and he is reported to have taken liothyronine throughout his presidency. Dr. Janet Travell, who first saw him in May 1955 and later became his White House physician, treated his back with injections of procaine into the muscles; Pait and Dowdy write that he received hundreds, if not thousands, of injections over about four years, which with muscle strengthening and the corset brought marked improvement. Through his years in Congress and the Senate he denied rumors that he had Addison's disease. During the 1960 campaign his campaign issued a statement that denied he had Addison's disease, describing it as a tuberculous destruction of the adrenal gland, and called his health excellent; in 1960 he acknowledged an adrenal deficiency. Dallek reported that during his presidency he took many medicines each day: steroids for his Addison's disease, painkillers for his back, antispasmodics for his colitis, antibiotics for urinary tract infections, and antihistamines for allergies. From late 1961 Dr. Hans Kraus put him on a program of exercise, swimming, massage and heat and tried to wean him off the back brace, with marked improvement; in August 1963 he strained his back and wore the brace again. He was assassinated in Dallas on November 22, 1963.
- **R:** The historian Robert Dallek, who examined his medical records at the Kennedy Library, believed that many of his difficulties could be traced to corticosteroids taken perhaps as early as 1937 to relieve his colitis, noting that heavy long-term use can promote osteoporosis. Reviewing his medical records in 2009, Lee Mandel concluded that his Addison's disease and his hypothyroidism were most likely parts of a single autoimmune disorder, autoimmune polyglandular syndrome type 2. Macchia and colleagues proposed that his bowel symptoms from adolescence fit well with coeliac disease. Retrospective reviews of his back disagree. The physician who reviewed the records with Dallek said he had compression fractures in his low back and osteoporosis; Pait and Dowdy, reviewing his X-rays, found no sign of underlying bone disease or of a steroid-related fracture and judged that his four back operations (a discectomy, a fusion with instrumentation, and two smaller operations) failed to relieve his pain. Pinals and Hassett argued in 2013 that, because operations on the presumed structural cause brought little relief and a conservative program with trigger point injections and exercise helped modestly, his pain is better understood through current pain research than as a purely structural problem.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
