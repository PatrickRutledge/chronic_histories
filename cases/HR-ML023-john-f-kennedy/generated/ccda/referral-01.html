Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • John F. Kennedy was born on May 29, 1917, in Brookline, Massachusetts. [D]
  • A few months before his third birthday he had a serious case of scarlet fever and spent a month in the hospital; through childhood and adolescence he had nausea, joint pain, headaches and diarrhea and was hospitalized several times. [D]
  • By 1934 he was sent to the Mayo Clinic, where his bowel illness was diagnosed as colitis. [D]
  • During his college years at Harvard his abdominal pain, weight loss and weakness continued despite a bland diet and strong medicines, including steroids placed under the skin to dissolve. [D]
  • The historian Robert Dallek, who examined his medical records at the Kennedy Library, believed that many of his difficulties could be traced to corticosteroids taken perhaps as early as 1937 to relieve his colitis, noting that heavy long-term use can promote osteoporosis. [R]
  • His chronic back pain began at Harvard, after he was tackled from the side in a football game, possibly damaging a spinal disc; he began wearing a corset brace to stabilize his spine. [D]
  • His back and intestinal problems caused him to fail Army and Navy physical examinations, but he was admitted to the Navy in October 1941. In August 1943 the patrol torpedo boat he commanded, PT-109, was struck by a Japanese destroyer; he led the survivors to safety, and his back worsened as a result. [D]
  • He came home from the war in the Pacific thin and sallow from malaria. [D]
  • Against the advice of his doctors at the Mayo Clinic, he had spinal surgery in June 1944 at New England Baptist Hospital; about six weeks later the pain returned. [D]
  • In 1946, at 29, he was elected to the U.S. House of Representatives from a Boston district. During the campaign his back was so bad that he wore a brace and used daily hot baths and massage. [D]
  • In 1947, at 30, while a congressman, he collapsed on a visit to England; a doctor there diagnosed Addison's disease and told one of his friends that he might not live a year. [D]
  • Returning to America aboard the Queen Mary, he was so ill that a priest was called to give him the last rites. [D]
  • He passed off the illness as a recurrence of his wartime malaria. In Boston an endocrinologist began treating him for Addison's disease with pellets of a synthetic adrenal hormone, desoxycorticosterone acetate (DOCA), implanted under his skin. [D]
  • Macchia and colleagues proposed that his bowel symptoms from adolescence fit well with coeliac disease. [R]
