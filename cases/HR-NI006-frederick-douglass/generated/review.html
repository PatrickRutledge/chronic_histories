Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Name. The display name is Frederick Douglass; the alias is his birth name, Frederick Augustus Washington Bailey, which appears in no other record. No identity.name_parts is needed.
  2. Birth date. The day is unknown; the preferred birth date is February 1818 (month precision), as the National Park Service gives it. He chose February 14 as his birthday, and the synthetic date of birth (February 14, 1992) keeps that day; it is recorded as an unresolved alternative and not asserted as his true date of birth.
  3. Index age and dates. Synthetic date of birth February 14, 1992; shift 174 years. The index visit on March 10, 2026 (age 34) maps to March 10, 1852. The chronic-care visits on March 24, May 19, and August 18, 2026, the sick visit on June 23, 2026, and the preventive visit on October 6, 2026 map to the same days of 1852. In 1852 he lived in Rochester, New York, and edited his own paper; he was not abroad (his journeys abroad were 1845 to 1847 and 1859 to 1860), and none of the visit dates falls on a dated event in the chronology (the May 11 to 13 meeting, the July 5 speech, or the September 30 convention). Age 34 was chosen because he was well documented then, after two decades of the experiences the modern episode starts from and with the hand injury nine years old.
  4. The diagnostic choice: no PTSD diagnosis. The roster lists PTSD and complex trauma, and no fetched source supports either as a historical diagnosis; the roster claim is recorded as R, disputed (CLM-0021), and not adopted. For the modern frame, the record was built to decide by the evidence. His autobiographies document exposure that meets the trauma criterion many times over, and they show him choosing, for years, to write and speak publicly about exactly those experiences: the opposite of the avoidance that a PTSD diagnosis requires. They also record a stable sense of himself and of where blame lies, and, decades later, his own words that those sufferings “seem like a dream rather than like a stern reality.” No source describes the persistent intrusion, avoidance, negative change in mood and thinking, and arousal, with impairment, that the diagnosis needs. A PTSD code would therefore have been a label derived from exposure alone, the stereotype this record is meant to avoid. The modern episode instead records his trauma history as a history (Z91.49, personal history of psychological trauma), screens with the PC-PTSD-5 (3, below the cut-point of 4) and the PCL-5 (24, below the 31 to 33 range), and confirms with a structured interview that the synthetic symptoms (occasional nightmares, watchfulness when traveling, short sleep) fall below the threshold. Trauma-focused psychotherapy (cognitive processing therapy or prolonged exposure) and medication (sertraline, paroxetine, or venlafaxine) are explained and kept in reserve, as the guideline recommends them only for PTSD. The nightmares, watchfulness, and short sleep are synthetic; the watchfulness is framed as proportionate to the documented threats and attacks of his adult life, and his anger at injustice as conviction, not a symptom.
  5. Safety assessment. None is recorded: no problem carries an F10–F99 code, the highest PHQ-9 is 5, and the PHQ-2 is 0. Item 9 of each PHQ-9 is recorded as 0, and the consultant documents no thoughts of suicide. His own account (Life and Times, First Part, Chapter XV) that as a youth under Covey he was “sometimes tempted to take my life” was read and is deliberately left out of the claims, to keep the trauma history brief and because it describes his adolescence, not the modern episode; the owner may wish to review this choice.
  6. Enslavement and sensitive history. Enslavement is recorded as a social and historical fact in the historical basis, never as a condition, a code, or a risk factor. The trauma claims (CLM-0002 to CLM-0006, CLM-0008, CLM-0011, CLM-0014, CLM-0019, CLM-0021) are marked sensitive, quoted briefly in his own words, and kept free of graphic detail. The PMH lists only the three documented injuries with health consequences: the collapse and head blow in the heat (dated 1833 in the Narrative and 1834 in the chronology; recorded as “1833 or 1834”), the left-eye injury in 1836, and the broken right hand in 1843.
  7. Physical illness. The only documented physical illness found in the cited sources is his death of a heart attack in 1895, recorded as a later event and not used in the modern episode. No other illness was verified, so none is claimed. The modern post-traumatic osteoarthritis of the right hand (M19.141) is anchored to his own statement that the broken bones were not properly set and the hand never recovered its strength and dexterity; the metacarpals affected, the X-ray findings, and the joint findings are synthetic. Normal visual acuity in the left eye is synthetic and records that the documented injury left no measurable loss.
  8. Real people. Covey and the shipyard apprentices appear only in the historical claims, as he described them. His wife and children appear only as documented facts (CLM-0009, CLM-0016) and in the strengths he names; no decision, statement, or health fact is attributed to any of them, and no relative appears in the visit notes. The family history records only that his mother died when he was about seven, cause not recorded. No other historical figure with a record in this dataset is named.
  9. Trauma-informed care. Every focused, sick, and preventive note opens its examination with a consent and chaperone entry; the referral and consultation record his agreement before the screening and the interview. He declined a back examination at intake and agreed to it at the preventive visit, where old healed scars are noted (synthetic, anchored to the documented whippings).
  10. Strengths. His work, writing, family and community, and faith are recorded as he would name them (SOC-002, SOC-005, INT-005), anchored to the documented newspaper, Underground Railroad work, literacy, and religious conversion.
  11. Synthetic content.
