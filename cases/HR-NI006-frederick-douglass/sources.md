## Historical evidence

National Park Service, Frederick Douglass National Historic Site. “Chronology of the Life of Frederick Douglass.” Supports the dates used: his birth at Holme Hill Farm in February 1818; his mother's last visit and her death in late 1825 or early 1826; his years on the Lloyd plantation (1824 to 1826); learning his letters and teaching himself to write; his religious conversion and joining Bethel A.M.E. Church in 1831; the year under Edward Covey dated 1834; the Baltimore caulking apprenticeship in 1836; the escape on September 3, 1838; the births of five children from 1839 to 1849; his hiring as a lecturer in 1841; the mob attack at Pendleton, Indiana, on September 16, 1843, that broke his right hand; the Narrative in May 1845; the purchase of his freedom in 1846; The North Star from December 1847 and Frederick Douglass' Paper from 1851; his work as a station master of the Underground Railroad; the gang attack in New York City on May 5, 1849; the speech of July 5, 1852; My Bondage and My Freedom in August 1855; and the events of February 20, 1895. https://www.nps.gov/frdo/learn/kidsyouth/chronology.htm
3. Douglass F. *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave. Written by Himself* (Boston, 1845). Documenting the American South, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. Chapter I: separation from his mother, whom he saw no more than four or five times, her death when he was about seven, and witnessing his aunt's whipping (“I never shall forget it whilst I remember any thing”). Chapter V: “suffered much from hunger, but much more from cold,” and his frost-cracked feet. Chapter X: the year with Covey (“scarce a week passed without his whipping me”; “I was broken in body, soul, and spirit”), the collapse while fanning wheat in August 1833 with “a violent aching of the head, attended with extreme dizziness,” the kicks and the blow to the head, the fight after which he was “never again what might be called fairly whipped,” and the shipyard attack in which his “left eye was nearly knocked out.” Chapter XI: the escape on September 3, 1838. https://docsouth.unc.edu/neh/douglass/douglass.html
4. Douglass F. *Life and Times of Frederick Douglass, Written by Himself* (Boston, 1892 edition). Documenting the American South. First Part, Chapter XV: “My sufferings, as I remember them now, seem like a dream rather than like a stern reality.” Second Part, Chapter V: the Pendleton mob, which left him unconscious with his right hand broken, and “as the bones broken were not properly set, my hand has never recovered its natural strength and dexterity.” https://docsouth.unc.edu/neh/dougl92/dougl92.html

Supports the cold, hunger, and corn-bag sleeping of his childhood, the year with Covey (“I was broken in body, soul and spirit”), and his words that it was “a life-long, standing grief to me, that I know so little of my mother, and that I was so early separated from her.” https://docsouth.unc.edu/neh/douglass55/douglass55.html

## Editorial resolutions

- **Name.** The display name is Frederick Douglass; the alias is his birth name, Frederick Augustus Washington Bailey, which appears in no other record. No identity.name_parts is needed.
- **Birth date.** The day is unknown; the preferred birth date is February 1818 (month precision), as the National Park Service gives it. He chose February 14 as his birthday, and the synthetic date of birth (February 14, 1992) keeps that day; it is recorded as an unresolved alternative and not asserted as his true date of birth.
- **Index age and dates.** Synthetic date of birth February 14, 1992; shift 174 years. The index visit on March 10, 2026 (age 34) maps to March 10, 1852. The chronic-care visits on March 24, May 19, and August 18, 2026, the sick visit on June 23, 2026, and the preventive visit on October 6, 2026 map to the same days of 1852. In 1852 he lived in Rochester, New York, and edited his own paper; he was not abroad (his journeys abroad were 1845 to 1847 and 1859 to 1860), and none of the visit dates falls on a dated event in the chronology (the May 11 to 13 meeting, the July 5 speech, or the September 30 convention). Age 34 was chosen because he was well documented then, after two decades of the experiences the modern episode starts from and with the hand injury nine years old.
- **The diagnostic choice: no PTSD diagnosis.** The roster lists PTSD and complex trauma, and no fetched source supports either as a historical diagnosis; the roster claim is recorded as R, disputed (CLM-0021), and not adopted. For the modern frame, the record was built to decide by the evidence. His autobiographies document exposure that meets the trauma criterion many times over, and they show him choosing, for years, to write and speak publicly about exactly those experiences: the opposite of the avoidance that a PTSD diagnosis requires. They also record a stable sense of himself and of where blame lies, and, decades later, his own words that those sufferings “seem like a dream rather than like a stern reality.” No source describes the persistent intrusion, avoidance, negative change in mood and thinking, and arousal, with impairment, that the diagnosis needs. A PTSD code would therefore have been a label derived from exposure alone, the stereotype this record is meant to avoid. The modern episode instead records his trauma history as a history (Z91.49, personal history of psychological trauma), screens with the PC-PTSD-5 (3, below the cut-point of 4) and the PCL-5 (24, below the 31 to 33 range), and confirms with a structured interview that the synthetic symptoms (occasional nightmares, watchfulness when traveling, short sleep) fall below the threshold. Trauma-focused psychotherapy (cognitive processing therapy or prolonged exposure) and medication (sertraline, paroxetine, or venlafaxine) are explained and kept in reserve, as the guideline recommends them only for PTSD. The nightmares, watchfulness, and short sleep are synthetic; the watchfulness is framed as proportionate to the documented threats and attacks of his adult life, and his anger at injustice as conviction, not a symptom.
- **Safety assessment.** None is recorded: no problem carries an F10–F99 code, the highest PHQ-9 is 5, and the PHQ-2 is 0. Item 9 of each PHQ-9 is recorded as 0, and the consultant documents no thoughts of suicide. His own account (Life and Times, First Part, Chapter XV) that as a youth under Covey he was “sometimes tempted to take my life” was read and is deliberately left out of the claims, to keep the trauma history brief and because it describes his adolescence, not the modern episode; the owner may wish to review this choice.

The PMH lists only the three documented injuries with health consequences: the collapse and head blow in the heat (dated 1833 in the Narrative and 1834 in the chronology; recorded as “1833 or 1834”), the left-eye injury in 1836, and the broken right hand in 1843.
