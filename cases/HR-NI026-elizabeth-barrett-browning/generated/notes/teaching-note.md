# Teaching Note — Elizabeth Barrett Browning pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Elizabeth Barrett was born on 6 March 1806 at Coxhoe Hall, about five miles south of Durham, the eldest child of Edward and Mary Moulton Barrett, and the eldest of twelve children; her father had added the surname Barrett as heir to his maternal grandfather's Jamaican estates. In 1821, while the family lived at Hope End in Herefordshire, she was seriously ill and went to the spa at Gloucester for treatment in June, returning to Hope End in May 1822; she later wrote, 'At fifteen I nearly died.' Her first editor reported that at about fifteen she injured her spine, by one account in a fall while saddling her pony, and by her son's statement through a strain while tightening the pony's girths, the injury not being discovered for some time; the editor judged that it contributed to the general weakness of health to which she was always subject. After the illness of 1821 she took opium by medical prescription, developing a lifelong habit. In 1838, after the family settled at 50 Wimpole Street, her health was in a very serious condition owing to the breaking of a blood-vessel; from then until her marriage she lived the life of an invalid. In August 1838 she moved to Torquay for her health, where her favourite brother Edward ('Bro') was her chief companion. On 11 July 1840 her brother Edward and two friends went sailing and did not return; the bodies were found three days later after a boat foundered in Babbacombe Bay. Her first editor wrote that she had the misery of thinking it was to attend on her that he had come to Torquay, that the place became a memory from which she shrank, and that even the sound of the sea became a horror to her. Her health suffered after the drowning; in the autumn of 1840 Mary Russell Mitford did not dare expect more than a few months of life for her, but she slowly gathered strength through the winter and returned to London in September 1841. Her brother Sam died in Jamaica in February 1840. Her mother died on 1 October 1828 after a last illness. On 2 October 1841 she wrote to H. S. Boyd that her case was 'not tubercular consumption, not what is called a decline, but an affection of the lungs which leans towards it', that a blood-vessel had broken three years before, and that four physicians, two experienced in diseases of the chest, agreed in that opinion. Her correspondence with the poet Robert Browning began in January 1845. In October 1845 she wrote to Mrs. Martin that she had been told one lung was very slightly affected but her nervous system 'absolutely shattered', that she was in the habit of taking forty drops of laudanum a day and, as the medical man told her, could not do with less, and that two physicians, Dr. Chambers among them, advised a winter in Pisa. In a letter postmarked 15 November 1845 she told Robert Browning that her opium kept 'the pulse from fluttering and fainting', that she did not take it for her spirits, and that she suffered none of the usual effects, not even an opium headache. In a letter postmarked 5 February 1846 she wrote that, for restlessness, loss of sleep, weakness and palpitation, 'the medical people gave me opium—a preparation of it, called morphine, and ether'; that she had never increased upon the prescribed quantity; that Mr. Jago said it would be dangerous to leave it off except very slowly and gradually; and that she would try to do with less. In the summer of 1845 the physicians advised a warm climate for the winter; she wished to go to Pisa, but the plan was given up after her father's opposition, which she called one of the most painful griefs of her life. She married Robert Browning on 12 September 1846; they left for Italy on 19 September, reached Pisa on 14 October, and moved to Florence in April 1847. She had miscarriages in March 1847, March 1848, late 1849 and July 1850, and her son, known as Pen, was born on 9 March 1849, three days after her forty-third birthday. She died in Florence on 29 June 1861, in her husband's arms, after a short illness that she called 'merely the old attack', and was buried in the Protestant cemetery there on 1 July.
- **R:** Buchanan and Weiss argued in 2011 that her lifelong illness, with weakness, palpitations and sensitivity to heat and cold from adolescence, strongly suggests hypokalemic periodic paralysis. Conjectures by modern biographers about her illness have included anorexia nervosa, neurasthenia, tuberculosis, scoliosis, opium addiction and mental illness. Quirarte-Ruvalcaba argued in 2024 that her illness was probably a congenital malformation of the lung (pulmonary sequestration) undiagnosed in her time, reassessed the possibility of morphine dependence, and suggested that an accidental morphine overdose may have contributed to her death.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
