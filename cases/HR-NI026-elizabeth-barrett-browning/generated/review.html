Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Recent-death rule. She died in 1861, so the rule does not apply. Claims are limited to what the fetched sources state. Claims about opium and morphine, about her grief, and about proposed psychiatric explanations are marked sensitive.
  2. The nature of her chest illness is not documented as a diagnosis. In her lifetime her physicians called it an affection of the lungs that was not tubercular consumption (CLM-0011), and later she was told one lung was very slightly affected (CLM-0013). Modern proposals are R claims kept apart: hypokalemic periodic paralysis (Buchanan and Weiss 2011; CLM-0020), the conjectures listed by Penn State (CLM-0021), and pulmonary sequestration (Quirarte-Ruvalcaba 2024; CLM-0022). None is placed on the problem list as a historical diagnosis. The brief named tuberculosis and bronchiectasis as possibilities; no fetched source proposes bronchiectasis specifically, so it is not stated as a historical claim.
  3. Choosing the modern diagnosis (judgment call). The modern episode diagnoses non-cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis of the left lower lobe and lingula with Haemophilus influenzae, after tuberculosis and nontuberculous mycobacteria are excluded. It fits the presenting pattern (years of productive cough, streaks of blood, breathlessness, winter worsening) and teaches the most: testing for tuberculosis first, the BTS cause work-up, airway clearance and exacerbation care. The CT is written to look for and exclude a sequestration, in answer to the 2024 proposal. The problem (J47.9) is linked to CLM-0005, CLM-0011 and CLM-0022 with placement both_with_link, and its display says the historical illness is disputed. The record does not claim this was her real illness.
  4. Opioids: what the evidence supports (judgment call). The sources show prescribed opium from 1821 (CLM-0004), a prescribed daily dose in 1845 that her medical man said she could not do without (CLM-0013), and her own statement in 1846 that she never increased upon the prescribed quantity and that it should be reduced only slowly (CLM-0015). This supports long-term prescribed use with physiological dependence. Under DSM-5-TR, tolerance and withdrawal on prescribed use do not count toward opioid use disorder, and no fetched source documents taking more than prescribed, craving, failed efforts to cut down, or harm from use; the evidence does not support a diagnosis of opioid use disorder, and none is made. “Opium addiction” appears only as one of several modern conjectures (CLM-0021, R, disputed), and the 2024 overdose suggestion is recorded as R (CLM-0022). The modern problem is Z79.891 (long-term use of an opiate). The modern episode continues the dose without a forced taper, adds naloxone, monitoring-program checks and a urine drug test, and documents a DSM-5-TR assessment with no disorder; buprenorphine is named as the treatment that would be offered if opioid use disorder developed. The slow reductions (20 to 18, 16 and then 14 mg a day) are her own choice, echoing her 1846 wish to “do with less” slowly; that letter is after the index date and appears in no intake document.
  5. Morphine dose. Forty drops of laudanum is about 2 mL of a 1% morphine tincture, about 20 mg of morphine; the modern prescription is morphine sulfate oral solution 2 mg/mL, 5 mL twice daily (20 mg a day). Its start date, March 1, 2019 (about seven years before the index visit), is synthetic. The indication (cough, sleeplessness, palpitations) follows her own account; the CDC 2022 guideline is written for pain, and its principles for patients already on long-term opioids are applied, a judgment call.
  6. Grief (judgment call). Her bereavement and its effect on her are documented (CLM-0007, CLM-0008). The modern episode diagnoses prolonged grief disorder (F43.81) five years after the drowning, with a PHQ-9 of 12, and treats it with grief-focused psychotherapy; this is a synthetic modern diagnosis, not a claim about her real mental health. Because the problem has an F code and the PHQ-9 is 12, a safety assessment is recorded at intake (no ideation, intent or plan), with a safety plan, the 988 Lifeline, the morphine in a locked box and naloxone; safety is reviewed at every later visit.
  7. Presenting problem. The chronic cough with blood-streaked sputum at intake is its own problem (R05.3, chronic cough), resolved at the consultation one week later when J47.9 is recorded. The intake documents show only what was known then: the work-up, not its results.
  8. Index age and dates. The index visit on March 16, 2026 maps to March 16, 1845, when she was 39 and an invalid at 50 Wimpole Street. The synthetic date of birth is March 6, 1987 (shift 181 years). Visits on March 23, April 20, June 15, July 13, August 10 and October 19, 2026 map to the same days of 1845, all before her marriage, while she lived at Wimpole Street. The modern visits are a teaching episode, not a claim about where she was.
  9. Dates in the sources. The breaking of the blood-vessel is dated 1838 by Kenyon and by her own letter of October 1841 (“three years ago”), and 1837 by the Victorian Web timeline; the record uses 1838. The EBB Archive dates the family’s move to 50 Wimpole Street to 1838.
  10. Name (judgment call). In 1845 she was Elizabeth Barrett Barrett (Miss Barrett); the record uses her later name throughout, with identity.name_parts given “Elizabeth” and family “Barrett Browning” (family_first false), because Barrett is a family name, not a middle name, and she used both after her marriage. The notes call her Ms. Barrett Browning. Aliases: “Elizabeth Barrett Barrett” and “Elizabeth Barrett”, which appear in no other record.
