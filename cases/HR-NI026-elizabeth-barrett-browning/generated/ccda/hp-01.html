  • Elizabeth Barrett was born on 6 March 1806 at Coxhoe Hall, about five miles south of Durham, the eldest child of Edward and Mary Moulton Barrett, and the eldest of twelve children; her father had added the surname Barrett as heir to his maternal grandfather's Jamaican estates. [D]
  • In 1821, while the family lived at Hope End in Herefordshire, she was seriously ill and went to the spa at Gloucester for treatment in June, returning to Hope End in May 1822; she later wrote, 'At fifteen I nearly died.' [D]
  • Her first editor reported that at about fifteen she injured her spine, by one account in a fall while saddling her pony, and by her son's statement through a strain while tightening the pony's girths, the injury not being discovered for some time; the editor judged that it contributed to the general weakness of health to which she was always subject. [D]
  • After the illness of 1821 she took opium by medical prescription, developing a lifelong habit. [D]
  • In 1838, after the family settled at 50 Wimpole Street, her health was in a very serious condition owing to the breaking of a blood-vessel; from then until her marriage she lived the life of an invalid. [D]
  • In August 1838 she moved to Torquay for her health, where her favourite brother Edward ('Bro') was her chief companion. [D]
  • On 11 July 1840 her brother Edward and two friends went sailing and did not return; the bodies were found three days later after a boat foundered in Babbacombe Bay. Her first editor wrote that she had the misery of thinking it was to attend on her that he had come to Torquay, that the place became a memory from which she shrank, and that even the sound of the sea became a horror to her. [D]
  • Her health suffered after the drowning; in the autumn of 1840 Mary Russell Mitford did not dare expect more than a few months of life for her, but she slowly gathered strength through the winter and returned to London in September 1841. [D]
  • Her brother Sam died in Jamaica in February 1840. [D]
  • Her mother died on 1 October 1828 after a last illness. [D]
  • On 2 October 1841 she wrote to H. S. Boyd that her case was 'not tubercular consumption, not what is called a decline, but an affection of the lungs which leans towards it', that a blood-vessel had broken three years before, and that four physicians, two experienced in diseases of the chest, agreed in that opinion. [D]
  • Her correspondence with the poet Robert Browning began in January 1845. [D]
