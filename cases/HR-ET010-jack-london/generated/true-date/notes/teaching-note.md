# Teaching Note — Jack London pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** He was born on January 12, 1876, in San Francisco, and his birth certificate named him John Griffith Chaney. His mother, Flora Wellman, had lived with William Chaney, an astrologer, who left before the birth; she married John London, who adopted and raised the boy. He grew up poor and worked from boyhood: an oyster pirate at 15, on the fish patrol at 16, an able-bodied seaman at 17, and a tramp crossing America at 18, when he spent a month in jail. He went to the Klondike gold fields in 1897, at 21, and came home with very little after suffering scurvy. He became internationally famous with The Call of the Wild (1903) and The Sea Wolf (1904), completed more than 50 books between 1900 and 1916, was at his peak the highest paid writer in the United States, and pushed to write 1,000 words a day wherever he was. From 1905 he bought land at Glen Ellen in the Sonoma Valley and built up his Beauty Ranch; Wolf House, his nearly finished stone and redwood home, burned on August 22, 1913, a crushing financial loss. The loss left him depressed, but he forced himself back to work. On the voyage of his yacht Snark through the South Pacific (1907 to 1909), in the Solomon Islands he had sores that a physician in Fiji called yaws and a missionary said were not; his hands swelled and peeled and his toenails thickened, a malady the Australian specialists could not name; he spent five weeks in hospital in Australia, gave up the voyage, and blamed tropical light. He also had a 'silvery skin' that doctors called by old names for leprosy, which cleared without treatment in California. In John Barleycorn (1913) he wrote of his drinking from the first time he got drunk, at five years old. He held that he was no born alcoholic and had acquired the taste, and described how at the ranch, after writing his thousand words between nine and eleven each morning, he began to take a cocktail before midday dinner and then alone, 'not for the sake of the taste, but for the effect of the drink.' In July 1913 he had his appendix removed, and serious kidney damage was found; his physician diagnosed a significant kidney problem at that time. In 1914 he went to Vera Cruz as a war correspondent in the Mexican Revolution and was ill with dysentery and rheumatism. He spent February 24 to July 22, 1915, in Hawaii and sailed there again on December 16, 1915; his health was described that year as declining. His doctors urged him to change his work habits and diet, stop all alcohol and get more exercise, and he refused. He died at his ranch on November 22, 1916, at 40. His death certificate, signed by his own physician, gave the cause as uraemia following renal colic; his physicians' bulletin described a gastro-intestinal type of uraemia, with coma, after he was taken ill in the night. He married Bess Maddern in 1900; their daughters Joan and Bess were born in 1901 and 1902, and the marriage ended in divorce in 1905. He married Charmian Kittredge in November 1905.
- **R:** Physicians writing since have interpreted his illnesses differently: the 'silvery skin' has been read as psoriasis; one review proposed that mercury used to treat yaws damaged his kidneys and caused a dermatitis of the hands, feet and face; others suggested lupus, gout, and the effects of tobacco, alcohol and diet on his blood pressure and kidneys. None is established. Whether morphine contributed to his death is disputed. A story that he took his own life began after his death and was spread by a 1938 biographical novel that described empty morphine vials; his attending physicians denied it, and historians, a pharmacist and physicians have argued against it, noting that he had taken morphine for pain for some time and that failing kidneys would have let a usual dose build up. The roster's leads of depression and probable bipolar disorder are not documented as diagnoses in the sources read.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
