Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Sources. The case relies on Jack London State Historic Park (its biography and three documents by the park historian and docents, one of which cites the death certificate, the physicians' bulletin and Huntington Library correspondence), California State Parks, his own published accounts in The Cruise of the Snark and John Barleycorn (Project Gutenberg), and the PubMed records of Bomback and Klemmer (2007, 2008), Denko (1993) and Aronica and Norton (2015), none of which has an abstract; their arguments are taken only as summarized by the park historian. The Huntington Library (rate-limited), Sonoma State University's Jack London collection and the Library of Congress pages (access refused) could not be read.
  2. Name. The display name is “Jack London”; his birth name, “John Griffith Chaney,” is the alias and appears in no other record. Neither “Jack” nor “London” alone is an alias, because both words appear in other records. “Griffith” appears only inside the full birth name; the display names of the two other records that contain that word are never written. No other figure with a record is named; a writer whom the park biography quotes in comparison with him is left out.
  3. Index age and dates. The index visit on August 4, 2026 maps to August 4, 1915, at Glen Ellen, when he was 39: two years after kidney damage was found at his appendectomy in July 1913, and two weeks after he came home from Hawaii on July 22, 1915. The synthetic date of birth is January 12, 1987 (shift 111 years). The visits on August 18, September 1, 15 and 29, and October 13 and 27, 2026 map to the same days of 1915, before he sailed for Hawaii again on December 16, 1915. The sources do not say where he was each day of that autumn; the ranch is assumed. Past medical history includes only events up to 1914; claims about 1916 and after are kept out of the intake documents.
  4. Kidney disease (CLM-0009, CLM-0013, D). Kidney damage found in July 1913 and death from “uraemia following renal colic” are documented; the nature and cause of his kidney disease are not. The modern episode gives a synthetic cause, hypertension with two years of daily naproxen, chosen as common, teachable and consistent with the documented rheumatism (the joint pain that leads to naproxen). The ultrasound shows no stones, so the record does not claim that he had stones in 1915; the renal colic of his last illness is recorded only as history.
  5. Retrospective diagnoses (CLM-0007, R, disputed). The “silvery skin” read as psoriasis, the hand malady, mercury from the treatment of yaws as a cause of his kidney disease, lupus, gout, and the effects of tobacco and alcohol are recorded once as disputed interpretations and are not used as diagnoses. His own account of the Snark illnesses (CLM-0006) is graded D as his account; the yaws diagnosis was itself disputed by those who saw him, so the past medical history entry is graded R.
  6. Morphine and suicide (CLM-0014, R, disputed; sensitive). The record takes neither side. The story that he took his own life, the morphine vials of a 1938 biographical novel, his physicians' denial, and the argument that kidney failure would let a usual dose of morphine accumulate are recorded once, as later history, linked to the 1916 timeline event so that the intake documents do not show them. The modern episode does not use them: its opioid caution rests on naltrexone, kidney disease and early recovery.
  7. Alcohol (CLM-0008, D; sensitive). His drinking is recorded in his own published words. The modern severe alcohol use disorder, its seven DSM-5-TR criteria, the amounts, the AUDIT of 27 and the PEth values are synthetic and are not a claim about his real drinking. The claim that his doctors urged him to stop drinking and he refused (CLM-0012) is undated in the source and is linked to the 1916 event so that it is not shown at intake.
  8. Mood (CLM-0015, R, disputed; sensitive). The roster's depression and probable bipolar disorder are not documented as diagnoses in the sources read and are not used. The park's statement that the loss of Wolf House left him “depressed” is kept as its words (CLM-0005). The modern PHQ-9 is 9 at intake with item 9 scored 0 and falls to 4.
  9. Safety assessment. Required by the alcohol use disorder code. It records no current suicidal ideation, intent or plan, lethal-means counseling for the rifle and shotgun on the ranch (locked in the ranch office safe with the ammunition apart), five days' supply of chlordiazepoxide with daily telephone checks, a written safety plan and the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Safety is reviewed at every later visit. The firearms are synthetic.
  10. Relatives. His mother, stepfather, the astrologer named on his birth certificate, his two wives and two daughters appear only as documented history (CLM-0001, CLM-0016) and as “married; two daughters from an earlier marriage.” Their statements about his death in source 4 are not used. The advance care planning agent is unnamed. No relative has any statement, decision or health fact in the modern episode. No family medical history is recorded.
  11. Clinical judgment calls. (1) Losartan started at intake for hypertension with albuminuria, before the consultation, with potassium and creatinine at two weeks; raised to 100 mg at six weeks. (2) Outpatient withdrawal with a five-day fixed chlordiazepoxide taper (low withdrawal-risk score, no cirrhosis, no past seizures or delirium), dispensed for five days only with daily telephone checks, and thiamine. (3) Naltrexone chosen over acamprosate: no kidney dose change, liver tests acceptable, no opioid use, and a once-daily schedule; acamprosate needs 333 mg three times a day at a creatinine clearance of 30 to 50 mL/min and is avoided at 30 or below. (4) Dapagliflozin delayed until ten weeks (six weeks after his one lapse), because heavy drinking, missed meals and vomiting raise the risk of ketoacidosis; he met the KDIGO recommendation (eGFR 20 or more and albumin 200 mg/g or more). (5) No kidney biopsy: bland sediment, moderate albuminuria and a clear hypertensive and NSAID history. (6) Uric acid of 8.4 mg/dL not treated without gout. (7) No statin at 39 without diabetes, heart disease or stroke (KDIGO). (8) Acetaminophen limited to 2,000 mg a day after heavy drinking; a short course of topical diclofenac for the sprain accepted because blood levels are low. (9) Liver stiffness of 7.2 kPa read as advanced fibrosis unlikely, with the caveat that stiffness reads high soon after heavy drinking. (10) Vaccines for kidney and liver disease, alcohol use disorder and smoking: influenza, PCV20, hepatitis B (two doses) and hepatitis A. (11) Smoking addressed but cessation deferred at his request until he is surer of his sobriety.
  12. Synthetic clinical picture. Everything in the modern episode is synthetic: every result, the urinalysis, ultrasound and elastography, the insurance creatinine, the hepatitis and HIV results (recorded as narrative), the CIWA-Ar, PAWSS and FIB-4 values, the kidney failure risk, the examination findings, the scores (AUDIT, PHQ-9, PHQ-2, GAD-7), the medicines and the outcomes. Height (173 cm) and weights are synthetic.
  13. Preventive visit. A periodic adult preventive visit at 39 (CPT 99395 with 99497 in plain text). Judgment calls: influenza, PCV20, hepatitis B and hepatitis A vaccines; COVID-19 vaccine at the pharmacy; HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C screening done at the consultation; colorectal and lung cancer screening not yet indicated; a dental visit for gum disease; advance care planning that will be revisited before any discussion of dialysis.
  14. Sick visit. A right ankle sprain from stepping into a burrow on the ranch, chosen because it tests the plan: no oral NSAIDs with kidney disease, no opioid on naltrexone, acetaminophen within a lower limit and a short course of topical diclofenac, the Ottawa ankle rules, and one evening of drinking for the pain, handled as a lapse with a safety review. It is not a historical claim.
  15. Focused visits. The four chronic-care follow-ups (two weeks with the consultation, six, ten and twelve weeks) are documented as focused visits on the CMID chronic kidney disease and hypertension monographs with KDIGO 2024, KDIGO 2021 and the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline, the APA 2018 and VA/DoD 2021 alcohol use disorder guidelines, the ASAM withdrawal guideline, the AASLD liver guidance and the VA/DoD suicide-risk guideline. One deviation is recorded instead of an invented result: the baseline 25-hydroxyvitamin D was left off the intake order; no harm was likely with normal calcium and phosphate and a mildly high PTH; it was drawn at six weeks (24 ng/mL, insufficient), vitamin D3 was started, and the clinic's new-CKD order set was checked. Synthetic findings introduced: a lapse at four weeks with a PEth of 36 ng/mL, the fall in urine albumin from 240 to 96 mg/g, and the expected 9 percent dip in eGFR two weeks after dapagliflozin.
  16. Consultation. The nephrology referral and the consultation by Teodor Mbatha-Rasmussen, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the two-week visit, are S. The consultant answers the five referral questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: chronic kidney disease G3b A2, most likely hypertensive with naproxen contributing, without a biopsy; losartan to 100 mg with potassium and creatinine checks and a systolic target below 120; dapagliflozin delayed until abstinence, then started at ten weeks; naltrexone rather than acamprosate; and no oral NSAIDs or opioids, with acetaminophen and short topical courses. Addiction medicine care is the clinic's weekly addiction-focused counseling, recorded in the alcohol plan.
