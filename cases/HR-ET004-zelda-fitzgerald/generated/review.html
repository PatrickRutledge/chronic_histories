Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Recent-death rule. She died in 1948, so the rule does not apply. Every historical claim is limited to what the fetched sources state; mental-health, eating, exercise, and hospitalization claims are marked sensitive.
  2. Birth and death dates. Her birth on July 24, 1900 is from the Encyclopedia of Alabama. NCpedia says the fire began late on the night of March 10, 1948; the Encyclopedia of Alabama gives the night of March 10–11. March 10 is preferred and March 11 recorded as a conflicting alternative. Her death is recorded in CLM-0010 as history only, without detail; the record does not describe how she was identified.
  3. Index age and dates. The index visit on May 11, 2026 maps to May 11, 1930, when she was 29, in the year of her first breakdown and hospitalization. The synthetic date of birth is July 24, 1996 (shift 96 years); she turns 30 before the three-month visit. The visits on May 18, June 8, August 10, and October 26, 2026, the sick visit on June 1, 2026, and the preventive visit on September 14, 2026 map to the same days of 1930. The risperidone start on April 20, 2026 stands for the inpatient start. In the true frame these outpatient dates fall during her long hospitalizations of 1930, including Prangins from June; the modern episode compresses that history into a twelve-day admission and outpatient care, and the synthetic visits are not a claim about where she was. The fetched sources date her first hospitalization only to 1930, so the past medical history gives the year only.
  4. Diagnosis. The historical diagnosis of schizophrenia is a documented fact (CLM-0005, D); that later experts contested it is R, disputed (CLM-0011); bipolar illness as the better fit is R, possible, from a physician’s letter (CLM-0012); Seeman’s reading of mood swings and course is R, possible (CLM-0013). None is coded. The modern episode starts with F29 (provisional; differential: bipolar I disorder with psychotic features, schizoaffective disorder, and schizophreniform disorder or schizophrenia). At the three-month visit, a clearer history (her journal and, with her consent, her roommate’s account) shows that the psychosis began only after weeks of mania and ended with it, so F29 is resolved (abatement date that visit) and a new problem, bipolar I disorder, most recent episode manic, in full remission (F31.74), is recorded that day with verification still provisional and a stated rule for revising it to schizoaffective disorder.
  5. Eating and exercise. Her training of almost eight hours a day is documented (D); “an obsession bordering on mania” is a description, and the roster’s reading of an exercise compulsion with an eating disorder is R, possible, with no fetched clinical source behind it (CLM-0014). The modern episode therefore screens without assuming: SCOFF 1 at intake and 0 at the preventive visit, no bingeing or purging, guilt on rest days, low weight, and amenorrhea attributed to low energy availability with a contribution from risperidone’s prolactin rise. No eating disorder is coded. Secondary amenorrhea (N91.1) is a presenting problem resolved at the three-month visit when her periods had returned and prolactin was normal.
  6. Real people. Her parents, husband, daughter, Lubov Egorova, and Dr. Oscar Forel appear only in the claims. No modern decision, statement, or health fact is attributed to any real person: the modern episode gives her a roommate and friend from her dance class, unnamed, and her instructors appear only as the object of a delusion during the episode. No family history is recorded because no fetched source gives her relatives’ health. The aliases “Zelda Sayre” and “Zelda Sayre Fitzgerald” appear in no other record.
  7. Sick visit. A same-day visit for viral gastroenteritis two weeks after lithium was started, with dehydration and a lithium level of 1.4 mmol/L (A08.4 and T43.595A; ICD-10-CM classifies lithium salts with the other antipsychotics and neuroleptics, and the 5th character 5 records an adverse effect at a prescribed dose).
