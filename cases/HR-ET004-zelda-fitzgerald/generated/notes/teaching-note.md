# Teaching Note — Zelda Fitzgerald pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Zelda Sayre was born on July 24, 1900, in Montgomery, Alabama, the youngest child of Alabama Supreme Court Justice Anthony Dickson Sayre and Minnie Buckner Machen Sayre; as a girl she starred in ballet recitals. In July 1918 she met F. Scott Fitzgerald; they married in New York on April 3, 1920, and their only child, Frances "Scottie" Fitzgerald, was born in 1921. In the late 1920s she returned to dance. She began ballet lessons at 27 with Lubov Egorova, hoping to dance with the Ballets Russes, and took classes every morning and afternoon, practising for almost eight hours a day. In 1930 the stress of her frustrated attempts to become a professional ballerina led to the first of many breakdowns. On a trip to Europe that year she was hospitalized for nervous exhaustion. From June 1930 to September 1931 she lived at the Les Rives de Prangins clinic in Nyon, Switzerland, where she was treated for schizophrenia; her main physician was Dr. Oscar Forel. She was diagnosed as schizophrenic in 1930 and gave up ballet. In February 1932 she entered the Phipps Clinic of Johns Hopkins University, where she completed her only novel, Save Me the Waltz. By 1934 she was spending her weekdays in the Sheppard and Enoch Pratt Hospital in Towson, Maryland. After a brief period of wellness her illness worsened; she was moved to Craig House in New York and returned to Sheppard Pratt in May 1934. In April 1936 she moved to Highland Hospital in Asheville, North Carolina, where she lived until 1940; afterwards she returned there at times when depression became debilitating, and her stays were intermittent until her death. She wrote and painted throughout her life; a 1934 exhibition in New York showed her paintings and drawings. Late on the night of March 10, 1948, a fire that started in a kitchen of the main building of Highland Hospital spread through the building; nine patients died, among them Zelda Fitzgerald.
- **R:** Although she was treated for schizophrenia, mental-health experts later contested both the diagnosis and the treatment prescribed by her main physician. A psychiatrist writing to the Princeton Alumni Weekly argued that her history of remissions and exacerbations makes schizophrenia highly unlikely and bipolar illness more likely. The psychiatrist Mary Seeman, drawing on biographies, describes her illness as showing specific precipitants, discontinuities in its course, pronounced mood swings, preserved intellect and agency, and flare-ups associated with immune and hormonal changes, as an example of how psychosis can present in women. Her ballet training has been described as an obsession bordering on mania. The project roster reads it as an exercise compulsion with an eating disorder, an interpretation that no fetched clinical source makes.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
