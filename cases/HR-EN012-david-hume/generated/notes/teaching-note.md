# Teaching Note — David Hume pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** David Hume was born at Edinburgh on April 26, 1711, Old Style (May 7, 1711, in the Gregorian calendar), the second son of Joseph Hume of Ninewells in Berwickshire. In My Own Life Hume wrote that his father died when he was an infant, leaving him, an elder brother, and a sister in their mother's care, and that his mother had died by the time he went to live at his brother's country house in 1749. In a letter to a physician written about 1734, Hume described an illness beginning about September 1729, when he was eighteen: loss of his ardour for study, scurvy spots on his fingers, and from about April 1730 a watery mouth, which his physician called 'the disease of the learned'; he was treated with bitters, anti-hysteric pills, a pint of claret a day, and riding, and in 1731 had a ravenous appetite and palpitations. In My Own Life, dated April 18, 1776, Hume wrote that in spring 1775 he was struck with a disorder in his bowels, which at first gave him no alarm but which he came to judge mortal and incurable; he wrote that he had suffered very little pain and that his spirits had never abated. In an undated letter written early in 1776, the physician attending Hume reported that he had been declining slowly for several years and more rapidly for about a year, with a sensation of excessive heat chiefly at night, and inferred a hemorrhage in the upper part of the intestines; the letter reports Hume's statement that his mother had the same constitution and died of the same disorder. Writing from Bath on May 13, 1776, Hume reported that his physicians disagreed about his disorder: one called it a hemorrhage, another a stricture ('spincture') of the colon that would be easy to cure, and a third a bilious complaint. Hume left Edinburgh in late April 1776, passing Morpeth on April 23, and travelled by London to Bath to take the waters; an early improvement had faded by June, and he returned to Edinburgh. In a letter of August 20, 1776, Hume described his distemper as a diarrhoea, or disorder in his bowels, which had been gradually undermining him for two years and had been visibly hastening his end within the last six months. Hume died at Edinburgh about four in the afternoon on August 25, 1776, aged 65; his physician wrote that he remained perfectly sensible and free from much pain to the last. His body was not opened after death, so the cause of his illness was never established.
- **R:** Hume's final illness has been interpreted retrospectively as colon cancer; a modern essay calls his death 'probably from colon cancer,' and a reference work describes a terminal abdominal cancer.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
