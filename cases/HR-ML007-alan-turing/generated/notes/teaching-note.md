# Teaching Note — Alan Turing pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Alan Mathison Turing was born on 23 June 1912 in a nursing home in Paddington, London. He was a long-distance runner: he built his strength in cross-country running, was known for running to scientific meetings, and in 1951 was still training for long-distance running on the roads around Wilmslow, where he had bought his first house. After reporting a burglary at his home in 1952 he was arrested over a relationship with another man and tried on 31 March 1952 for "gross indecency"; rather than go to prison he accepted, for a year, oestrogen treatment intended to neutralise his libido. His biographer Andrew Hodges writes that the drug was probably stilboestrol, a synthetic oestrogen; the 2009 government statement and other sources describe the treatment as a series of injections of female hormones. Hodges describes the injurious and humiliating effect of the hormones on his body; it is often repeated that the treatment made him grow breasts, though he is known to have mentioned this only once. In a letter of 23 November 1952 he wrote that he had decided to have "another, and rather more co-operative go at a psychiatrist"; this was Jungian analysis with a refugee analyst, and he became very enthusiastic about it and wrote down all his dreams. His conviction led to the removal of his security clearance, so that he could no longer work for Government Communications Headquarters as he had since the war. After the trial he returned to a wide range of academic work, including morphogenesis, quantum theory, and relativity, at Manchester University. He died on 7 June 1954 at his home in Wilmslow, Cheshire, of cyanide poisoning, and the coroner's verdict was suicide. On 10 September 2009 the Prime Minister apologised on behalf of the British government, calling his treatment inhumane and describing his sentence as chemical castration by a series of injections of female hormones, accepted as the alternative to prison. On 24 December 2013 he received a posthumous royal pardon; the Justice Secretary called the conviction unjust and discriminatory, and the Prime Minister said the sentence of chemical castration had caused physical and emotional damage.
- **R:** In 2012 Professor Jack Copeland argued that the inquest evidence would not today support a suicide verdict, that friends had described him as cheerful in the days before he died, and that accidental inhalation of cyanide from a home electrolysis experiment was equally likely. Hodges judges it more credible that Turing ended his own life and arranged matters so that it could be taken for an accident. The roster lists depression as a primary condition; the sources read document his prosecution, the hormone treatment, and his decision to seek Jungian analysis, but record no diagnosis of depression, so a depressive illness is a retrospective interpretation.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
