## Historical evidence

1. Hodges A. “Alan Turing” (overview biography revised from his Oxford Dictionary of National Biography entry), The Alan Turing Home Page. Supports his birth on 23 June 1912 in a Paddington nursing home; his distance running (“developed his strength in cross-country running”; running to scientific meetings); the house he bought at Wilmslow; his arrest and trial on 31 March 1952 and that “rather than go to prison he accepted, for the period of a year, injections of oestrogen intended to neutralise his libido”; that he sought closer contact with friends “and with a Jungian therapist”; his death on 7 June 1954 of cyanide poisoning, found the next day; the coroner's verdict of suicide; and Hodges's own view that it is “more credible that he had successfully contrived his death” so that his mother could believe it an accident. https://www.turing.org.uk/publications/dnb.html
2. Hodges A. The Alan Turing Internet Scrapbook, “Defiant until death.” Supports that “the drug was probably stilbestrol”; “the injurious and humiliating effect of the hormones on his body”; his letter of 23 November 1952 (“Have decided to have another, and rather more co-operative go at a psychiatrist”); that “it was Jungian analysis with a Dr Franz Greenbaum, a pre-war refugee from Germany” and that in 1953 he became very enthusiastic about it and wrote down his dreams; and the inquest verdict of suicide. https://www.turing.org.uk/scrapbook/wondrous.html
3. O'Connor JJ, Robertson EF. “Alan Mathison Turing.” MacTutor History of Mathematics, University of St Andrews. Supports birth on 23 June 1912 in London (Paddington) and death on 7 June 1954 in Wilmslow, Cheshire; the trial on 31 March 1952 and that, found guilty, “he was given the alternatives of prison or oestrogen injections for a year”; his return to “a wide range of academic pursuits”, including morphogenesis, quantum theory, and relativity; and the apology of 10 September 2009. https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Turing/
4. Hodges A. The Alan Turing Internet Scrapbook, “Non-linear Life.” Supports his semi-detached house at Wilmslow and that in 1951 “he continued training for long-distance running” on the roads around Wilmslow. https://www.turing.org.uk/scrapbook/morph.html
5. Pease R. “Alan Turing: Inquest's suicide verdict ‘not supportable’.” BBC News, 23 June 2012. Reports Professor Jack Copeland's argument that the inquest evidence would not today establish suicide and that “Turing's death may equally probably have been an accident”, that the apple was never tested, that friends described his good humour in the days before his death, and his suggested alternative of accidental cyanide exposure from an electrolysis experiment in his spare room; that he faced the possible loss of his Manchester post; that “it is often repeated that the chemicals caused him to grow breasts, though Turing is only known to have mentioned this once”; and that he “tolerated the year-long hormone treatment … with amused fortitude”. https://www.bbc.com/news/science-environment-18561092
6. UK Ministry of Justice. “Royal pardon for WW2 code-breaker Dr Alan Turing.” GOV.UK press release, 24 December 2013. Supports the posthumous pardon under the Royal Prerogative of Mercy; the Justice Secretary's description of the conviction as one “we would now consider unjust and discriminatory”; and the Prime Minister's statement that the conviction “resulted in a sentence of ‘chemical castration’”, that “as well as physical and emotional damage, his conviction led to the removal of his security clearance”, and that he could no longer work for GCHQ. https://www.gov.uk/government/news/royal-pardon-for-ww2-code-breaker-dr-alan-turing
7. The University of Manchester. “Gordon Brown issues apology for treatment of Alan Turing,” 11 September 2009. Quotes the Prime Minister's statement that “he was treated so inhumanely”, that “in 1952, he was convicted of ‘gross indecency’ – in effect, tried for being gay. His sentence – and he was faced with the miserable choice of this or prison – was chemical castration by a series of injections of female hormones”, and the apology on behalf of the British government. https://www.manchester.ac.uk/about/news/gordon-brown-issues-apology-for-treatment-of-alan-turing/
8. HISTMED Master Roster v3. Internal project source. Its clinical claims (depression, chemical castration, gynecomastia, probable suicide, pardon) were provisional and were not treated as independent corroboration.

## Clinical guidance

1. Kanakis GA, et al. “EAA clinical practice guidelines—gynecomastia evaluation and management.” *Andrology* 2019;7(6):778–793. PMID 31099174. A full history and examination of the breasts and genitalia, including testicular ultrasound; testosterone, estradiol, SHBG, LH, FSH, TSH, prolactin, hCG, AFP, liver and kidney tests; breast imaging only when the examination is equivocal; investigation even when a drug cause is apparent; watchful waiting after the cause is removed; SERMs, aromatase inhibitors, and non-aromatizable androgens not justified in general; surgery for long-standing gynecomastia. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31099174/
2. Bhasin S, et al. “Testosterone Therapy in Men With Hypogonadism: An Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline.” *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 2018;103(5):1715–1744. PMID 29562364. Diagnosis of hypogonadism only with symptoms and unequivocally, consistently low fasting morning testosterone, confirmed by a repeat measurement; free testosterone when SHBG is altered; further evaluation to find the cause; and testosterone therapy for symptomatic deficiency only after discussing benefits and risks with the patient and involving him in the decision. The abstract was read. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29562364/
3. Watts NB, et al. “Osteoporosis in Men: An Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline.” *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 2012;97(6):1802–1822. PMID 22675062. Bone density testing for men 70 and older and for men 50 to 69 with risk factors, laboratory testing for contributing causes, and adequate calcium, vitamin D, and weight-bearing exercise.
