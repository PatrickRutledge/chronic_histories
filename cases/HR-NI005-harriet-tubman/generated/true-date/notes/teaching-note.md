# Teaching Note — Harriet Tubman pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Harriet Tubman was born into slavery in Dorchester County, Maryland, in early 1822 by the National Park Service account, and was named Araminta, or Minty, by her parents, Ben and Rit Ross; she was the fifth of nine children. Her birth year is uncertain: the Dictionary of Canadian Biography gives 1820 or 1821, while the National Park Service and the Maryland State Archives give about 1822. Hired out from the age of six, Tubman was struck in the head in her early teens, about age 13 in the mid-1830s, by an iron weight an overseer threw at another enslaved person at a village store; she was nearly killed, received little or no medical care, and was affected by the injury for the rest of her life. After the injury Tubman had headaches and sudden spells of sleep that came on even in the middle of work or conversation, at frequent intervals, and ended abruptly after a few minutes, along with vivid dreams and visions; contemporaries described the spells into her old age. In her later years Tubman said that a Boston surgeon had opened her skull to relieve her headaches; historians place the operation in the late 1890s at Massachusetts General Hospital, but no record of it has been found. Tubman married John Tubman in 1844, escaped to Philadelphia in 1849, guided about 70 people to freedom by 1860, served the Union army in South Carolina as a nurse, scout, and spy, and settled in Auburn, New York, where she married Nelson Davis in 1869. Tubman died of pneumonia in Auburn, New York, on March 10, 1913.
- **R:** Historians have proposed that the injury caused temporal lobe epilepsy, citing dreamlike trances in which she appeared conscious. A 2023 review argued that temporal lobe epilepsy is unlikely, because she could be roused and no auras, automatisms, or post-ictal confusion were described, and proposed post-traumatic narcolepsy instead, while concluding that no definitive explanation is possible.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
