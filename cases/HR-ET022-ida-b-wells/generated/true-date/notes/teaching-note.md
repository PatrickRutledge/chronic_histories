# Teaching Note — Ida B. Wells pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Ida B. Wells was born enslaved on July 16, 1862, in Holly Springs, Mississippi, during the Civil War. In the yellow fever epidemic of 1878, when she was 16, both her parents and an infant brother died; she left school and took on the care of her surviving younger siblings, teaching to support them. She was an investigative journalist and editor who documented lynching in the United States, notably in A Red Record (1895), and campaigned against it for the rest of her life; she was also a suffragist and civil rights leader. On June 27, 1895, she married the Chicago attorney Ferdinand L. Barnett, kept her own name alongside his, and settled in Chicago; they had four children. She signed the call for the 1909 conference that led to the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People and is considered one of its founders, though she was left off the list of its founding committee and later drew away from it. In 1930 she ran, unsuccessfully, for the Illinois State Senate. She began writing her autobiography in her last years; it was published after her death as Crusade for Justice: The Autobiography of Ida B. Wells, edited by her youngest daughter, Alfreda M. Duster. She died in Chicago on March 25, 1931, at 68, after a brief illness; the cause is given as kidney disease or uremia (uremic poisoning).
- **R:** Whether she had chronic kidney disease before her final illness, and what caused her kidney failure, is not recorded in any source consulted; a short final illness ending in uremia is compatible with acute kidney failure, with chronic kidney disease made worse by an acute illness, or with long-standing kidney disease that had not been recognized.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
