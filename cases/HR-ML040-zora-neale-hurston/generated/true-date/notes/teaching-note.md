# Teaching Note — Zora Neale Hurston pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Zora Neale Hurston was born on January 7, 1891, in Notasulga, Alabama, the fifth of eight children of John Hurston, a carpenter and Baptist preacher, and Lucy Potts Hurston, a former schoolteacher. She was born as Zora Lee Hurston, according to the Florida Artists Hall of Fame. Her family moved to Eatonville, Florida, the nation's first incorporated Black township, when she was a toddler (at about three, in 1894), and her father later became its mayor; for her, Eatonville was always home. Her mother died in 1904, when Zora was 13. In 1917, aged 26 and needing to present herself as a teenager to qualify for free public schooling in Baltimore, she gave her age as 16 and her birth year as 1901; from then on she presented herself as at least ten years younger than she was. The headstone placed on her grave in 1973 reads 1901. She attended Howard University and Barnard College, where she studied anthropology with Franz Boas and received a bachelor of arts degree in 1928; she did folklore field work in the South and became a writer and anthropologist of the Harlem Renaissance. Over more than 30 years she published four novels, including Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937), two books of folklore, an autobiography, and many stories, essays, articles and plays. She never received the financial rewards her work deserved; the largest royalty she earned from any of her books was $943.75. In 1950 she worked as a maid in Florida while still publishing in national magazines, and she struggled to find work throughout the 1950s. She worked as a librarian at Patrick Air Force Base in Florida (from June 1956 by one account, for about a year by another) and then moved to Fort Pierce, where she wrote a column on "Hoodoo and Black Magic" for the Fort Pierce Chronicle from 1957 to 1959; a family friend from Eatonville invited her to live in a small house in Fort Pierce rent-free. In 1958 she worked as a substitute teacher at Lincoln Park Academy in Fort Pierce. Rarely a drinker, she was remembered by friends for her humor and storytelling and for the gift of walking into hearts. She had a stroke in early 1959 and in October 1959 was forced by her health and finances to enter the St. Lucie County Welfare Home in Fort Pierce. The Florida Artists Hall of Fame dates the stroke to 1960. She died on January 28, 1960, at the St. Lucie County Welfare Home in Fort Pierce, of hypertensive heart disease, aged 69. Her neighbors took up a collection for her funeral, which did not cover a headstone; she was buried in an unmarked grave in the Garden of Heavenly Rest, a segregated cemetery in Fort Pierce, and the grave stayed unmarked until 1973, when a younger writer found it and placed a headstone reading "A Genius of the South".
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
