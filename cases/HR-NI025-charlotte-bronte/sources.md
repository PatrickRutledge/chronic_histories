1. Gaskell EC. *The Life of Charlotte Brontë*, Volume 1 (1857). Project Gutenberg eBook 1827. 2. Gaskell EC. *The Life of Charlotte Brontë*, Volume 2 (1857). Project Gutenberg eBook 1700, chapter XIII. Supports the wedding fixed for “the 29th of June”; the walk to the waterfall in late November 1854, after which “she began to shiver … and had a bad lingering sore throat and cold, which hung about her; and made her thin and weak”; the visit to Gawthorpe early in 1855 and a long walk “over damp ground in thin shoes”; that “she was attacked by new sensations of perpetual nausea, and ever-recurring faintness”, that a doctor “assigned a natural cause for her miserable indisposition”, and that “the dreadful sickness increased and increased, till the very sight of food occasioned nausea”; that the family servant Tabby died in this time; that her servant Martha “tried to cheer her with the thought of the baby that was coming”; her letter of 15 February: “At present I am confined to my bed with illness, and have been so for three weeks. ## Editorial resolutions

- **Index age and dates.** The episode presents her at 38, early in the pregnancy she is reported to have carried when she died. The synthetic date of birth is April 21, 1987, so the whole-year shift is 171 years and 2026 maps to 1855. The index visit is January 13, 2026, at 7 weeks 1 day (true frame January 13, 1855, about when the documented nausea began). The other visits are January 20 (consultation), January 29 (sick visit and admission), February 10, February 24, and March 24, 2026 (true frame January 20 and 29, February 10 and 24, and March 24, 1855). The last falls a week before her death on March 31, 1855, and she is 38 throughout. Because she died less than three months into 1855, the whole visit set had to fit between January 1 and March 30; it does, so no earlier pre-conception index was needed. The visits are spaced as prenatal care for hyperemesis would be (one week, then after the admission, then at the nuchal translucency window, then at 17 weeks). The modern episode is a deliberate counterfactual: with modern care she recovers, while the true-frame dates fall within her documented last illness, when Gaskell records her confined to bed. No documented event is contradicted by a date, and the episode makes no claim about what happened to her.
- **Diagnostic conflict.** Her nausea, faintness, inability to eat, confinement to bed, late delirium with hunger, and death are **D** from Gaskell. The death certificate's phthisis is **D** as a record, and its correctness is **R** (disputed). Hyperemesis gravidarum (Wikipedia, the Brontë family article, Allison and Lobo) is **R**, possible; refeeding syndrome (Allison and Lobo) is **R**, possible; typhoid or another bacterial infection from the servant who had died (Lyndall Gordon, as reported in the OUPblog essay, and the Brontë family article) is **R**, possible; tuberculosis with secondary Addison disease, and doubt that she was pregnant at all (Weiss 1991), is **R**, disputed. The conflict is recorded and not resolved. The pregnancy itself is **D**, probable (Gaskell's account of the coming baby, her letter as reported, and the reference works), with Weiss's doubt recorded. The synthetic hyperemesis diagnosis (O21.1) is placed both_with_link, as a modern teaching diagnosis linked to these claims, not as her historical diagnosis.
- **Typhus.** The assignment mentioned typhus. The sources I could read name typhus only for the 1825 outbreak at the Cowan Bridge school; the infectious explanation for her own death that I could verify is typhoid (or “a bacterial infection like typhoid”). Typhus is therefore not claimed as a proposed cause of her death.
- **Sources not read.** The Brontë Society and Brontë Parsonage Museum pages either redirected without end or did not cover her death; the Oxford DNB, Juliet Barker's *The Brontës*, the journal *Brontë Studies*, and the full texts of the BMJ piece “Saving Charlotte Brontë” (Drife 2012, PMID 22279091, no abstract) and Fitzgerald 1979 (PMID 375134, no abstract) could not be read and are not cited. A Hektoen International essay that says phthisis was recorded for her mother, all her sisters, and her brother was not used, because it conflicts with the memorial dates and the reference works. Charlotte's own height and weight are not documented in the sources read; the synthetic 152 cm and 46.0 kg are not claims.
- **Family history.** Only documented deaths are recorded: her mother (death in 1821 **D**; possible cancer **R**, so the entry is **R**), her eldest sisters (tuberculosis in 1825, **D**, probable), her brother (several conditions with heavy drinking and tuberculosis among them, **D**, probable, marked sensitive), and Emily and Anne (tuberculosis in 1848 and 1849, **D**). Her father and husband appear only in documented claims (her father's occupation, her marriage); the modern episode says only that she lives with her husband and her father, names neither, and attributes no statement, decision, or health fact to either. The family member who drives her to the sick visit is unnamed. Gaskell's remark that she yielded to her husband's wish to call a doctor is not used.

The nausea that began “early in the new year” is the presenting concern of the modern episode, not past history. Her own belief that her schooling damaged her health, and her poor eyesight, are not used.

The unscheduled sick visit is the day her nausea and vomiting becomes hyperemesis gravidarum and she is admitted directly from clinic; her admission (three nights) is described in the notes and medications, not as separate encounters. No `preventive_adult` visit was added: prenatal care is the preventive care of pregnancy, and its preventive elements (screening tests, influenza vaccine, folic acid, aspirin, mood screening, intimate partner violence screening, and tuberculosis screening) are in the prenatal visits. A separate adult preventive examination during the first trimester, while she was being admitted for vomiting, would not be sensible.

One protocol deviation is recorded as `deferred`, with its outcome and corrective action, and no result was invented: the electrolyte check planned within a week of discharge was not booked and was first done nine days after discharge, with normal results.
