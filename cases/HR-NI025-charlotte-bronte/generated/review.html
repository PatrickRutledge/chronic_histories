Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Index age and dates. The episode presents her at 38, early in the pregnancy she is reported to have carried when she died. The synthetic date of birth is April 21, 1987, so the whole-year shift is 171 years and 2026 maps to 1855. The index visit is January 13, 2026, at 7 weeks 1 day (true frame January 13, 1855, about when the documented nausea began). The other visits are January 20 (consultation), January 29 (sick visit and admission), February 10, February 24, and March 24, 2026 (true frame January 20 and 29, February 10 and 24, and March 24, 1855). The last falls a week before her death on March 31, 1855, and she is 38 throughout. Because she died less than three months into 1855, the whole visit set had to fit between January 1 and March 30; it does, so no earlier pre-conception index was needed. The visits are spaced as prenatal care for hyperemesis would be (one week, then after the admission, then at the nuchal translucency window, then at 17 weeks). The modern episode is a deliberate counterfactual: with modern care she recovers, while the true-frame dates fall within her documented last illness, when Gaskell records her confined to bed. No documented event is contradicted by a date, and the episode makes no claim about what happened to her.
  2. Diagnostic conflict. Her nausea, faintness, inability to eat, confinement to bed, late delirium with hunger, and death are D from Gaskell. The death certificate's phthisis is D as a record, and its correctness is R (disputed). Hyperemesis gravidarum (Wikipedia, the Brontë family article, Allison and Lobo) is R, possible; refeeding syndrome (Allison and Lobo) is R, possible; typhoid or another bacterial infection from the servant who had died (Lyndall Gordon, as reported in the OUPblog essay, and the Brontë family article) is R, possible; tuberculosis with secondary Addison disease, and doubt that she was pregnant at all (Weiss 1991), is R, disputed. The conflict is recorded and not resolved. The pregnancy itself is D, probable (Gaskell's account of the coming baby, her letter as reported, and the reference works), with Weiss's doubt recorded. The synthetic hyperemesis diagnosis (O21.1) is placed both_with_link, as a modern teaching diagnosis linked to these claims, not as her historical diagnosis.
  3. Typhus. The assignment mentioned typhus. The sources I could read name typhus only for the 1825 outbreak at the Cowan Bridge school; the infectious explanation for her own death that I could verify is typhoid (or “a bacterial infection like typhoid”). Typhus is therefore not claimed as a proposed cause of her death.
  4. Sources not read. The Brontë Society and Brontë Parsonage Museum pages either redirected without end or did not cover her death; the Oxford DNB, Juliet Barker's The Brontës, the journal Brontë Studies, and the full texts of the BMJ piece “Saving Charlotte Brontë” (Drife 2012, PMID 22279091, no abstract) and Fitzgerald 1979 (PMID 375134, no abstract) could not be read and are not cited. A Hektoen International essay that says phthisis was recorded for her mother, all her sisters, and her brother was not used, because it conflicts with the memorial dates and the reference works. Charlotte's own height and weight are not documented in the sources read; the synthetic 152 cm and 46.0 kg are not claims.
  5. Family history. Only documented deaths are recorded: her mother (death in 1821 D; possible cancer R, so the entry is R), her eldest sisters (tuberculosis in 1825, D, probable), her brother (several conditions with heavy drinking and tuberculosis among them, D, probable, marked sensitive), and Emily and Anne (tuberculosis in 1848 and 1849, D). These household tuberculosis deaths are the reason for the tuberculosis screening; in the modern episode an interferon-gamma release assay is negative, so latent infection is excluded and no chest radiograph is needed. Her father and husband appear only in documented claims (her father's occupation, her marriage); the modern episode says only that she lives with her husband and her father, names neither, and attributes no statement, decision, or health fact to either. The family member who drives her to the sick visit is unnamed. Gaskell's remark that she yielded to her husband's wish to call a doctor is not used.
  6. Past medical history. Only the lingering cold and sore throat of November 1854 is recorded (D, Gaskell). The nausea that began “early in the new year” is the presenting concern of the modern episode, not past history. Her own belief that her schooling damaged her health, and her poor eyesight, are not used.
  7. Visit types. The index visit is the first prenatal visit (the H&P). The four prenatal follow-ups are focused chronic-care visits. The unscheduled sick visit is the day her nausea and vomiting becomes hyperemesis gravidarum and she is admitted directly from clinic; her admission (three nights) is described in the notes and medications, not as separate encounters. No `preventive_adult` visit was added: prenatal care is the preventive care of pregnancy, and its preventive elements (screening tests, influenza vaccine, folic acid, aspirin, mood screening, intimate partner violence screening, and tuberculosis screening) are in the prenatal visits. A separate adult preventive examination during the first trimester, while she was being admitted for vomiting, would not be sensible.
  8. Presenting problem. Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (O21.9) is the intake problem, resolved on January 29, 2026, when hyperemesis gravidarum with metabolic disturbance (O21.1) and gestational transient thyrotoxicosis (O99.281) are recorded. The thyroid finding resolves at the 17-week visit. Intake documents show only what was known at intake: the admission plan and thyroid plan carry `recorded_date` January 29, 2026.
  9. Instruments. No LOINC code for the PUQE or PUQE-24 was found on the public terminology server (searches for “PUQE”, “Pregnancy-Unique Quantification”, “nausea vomiting pregnancy”, and “Motherisk”), so the scores (10, 11, 14, 9, 7, 4) are kept in the note text and monitoring tables. The PHQ-9 (44261-6) and EPDS total score (99046-5) are coded surveys. Scores are below the thresholds that need a safety assessment, with item 9 and item 10 scored 0, so none is recorded.
  10. Ondansetron and other drug choices. Ondansetron is offered at the consultation visit before 10 weeks with the RCOG counseling about a very small absolute increase in orofacial clefting; she chooses to keep it in reserve, takes metoclopramide as the second drug, and accepts ondansetron on admission, after an electrocardiogram (QTc 438 ms) and with potassium and magnesium being replaced. Thiamine is given intravenously before any dextrose, then orally. Enoxaparin is weight-adjusted (20 mg, weight under 50 kg); RxNorm has no current 20 mg prefilled syringe, so the 100 mg/mL solution is coded with a 20 mg dose. The magnesium and phosphate checks and refeeding precautions echo, without asserting, the refeeding interpretation of her death.
  11. Focused visits. The four prenatal follow-ups (8, 11, 13, and 17 weeks) are documented as focused visits on ACOG Practice Bulletin 189 and RCOG Green-top Guideline 69 (2024), with routine prenatal items: the PUQE-24 score, weight and hydration, electrolytes until stable, magnesium and phosphate after refeeding, liver tests until normal, TSH and free T4, antiemetic side effects, thiamine, the thromboembolism risk assessment, mood screening, aneuploidy screening (cell-free DNA, low risk, kept in the note text; nuchal translucency 1.5 mm), low-dose aspirin, blood pressure, and the fetal heart. Thrombosis risk was reassessed at discharge: age over 35 is her only persisting risk factor, so no prophylaxis after discharge. One protocol deviation is recorded as `deferred`, with its outcome and corrective action, and no result was invented: the electrolyte check planned within a week of discharge was not booked and was first done nine days after discharge, with normal results. The prenatal panel results, the interferon-gamma release assay, and the cell-free DNA result are qualitative and are kept in the note text rather than as coded observations.
  12. Consultation. The maternal-fetal medicine referral and consultation (Margaret Oyelaran-Hughes, MD, a fictional consultant, on the day of the one-week visit) are S. The consultant recommends what the record then shows: doxylamine-pyridoxine raised to four tablets a day with metoclopramide, ondansetron as the next step after counseling, the same-day thresholds that led to her admission nine days later, and the inpatient plan used (saline with potassium, thiamine before dextrose, magnesium and phosphate with refeeding precautions, weight-adjusted heparin, thyroid and liver tests); cell-free DNA with a nuchal translucency scan (done at 11 and 13 weeks); aspirin from 12 to 16 weeks (started at 13 weeks); no further tuberculosis testing; and the later prenatal plan.
  13. Name and alias. The display name keeps the diaeresis, “Charlotte Brontë”, and splits normally into a given and a family name, so no `name_parts` is needed. Her pen name “Currer Bell” appears in no other record and is used as the alias.
  14. Primary clinician. Eleanor Thwaite, MD, is cast as an obstetrician-gynecologist providing her prenatal care.
  15. Charlotte Brontë died in 1855, so the recent-death review rule does not apply. Ordinary clinical, provenance, and safe-messaging reviews remain required. Claims about her final illness, delirium, and pregnancy are marked sensitive.
