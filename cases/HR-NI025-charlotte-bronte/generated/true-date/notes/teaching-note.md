# Teaching Note — Charlotte Brontë pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Charlotte Brontë was born on 21 April 1816 in Thornton, Yorkshire, the third of six children of Maria Branwell and her husband, an Anglican clergyman. Jane Eyre was first published in 1847 under the pseudonym Currer Bell. Her mother died on 15 September 1821, in the 39th year of her age, after a long and painful illness. Her two elder sisters, Maria and Elizabeth, fell ill at the Clergy Daughters' School at Cowan Bridge and died at home in 1825, Maria on 6 May aged 11 and Elizabeth on 15 June aged 10; reference works give tuberculosis. Her brother Branwell died on 24 September 1848, aged 31, of several conditions made worse by heavy drinking; tuberculosis is listed among them. Her sisters Emily and Anne died of tuberculosis, Emily on 19 December 1848 aged 30 and Anne on 28 May 1849 aged 29. On 29 June 1854 she married Arthur Bell Nicholls, her father's curate, at Haworth. In late November 1854, after a long walk on the moors to a waterfall that ended in heavy rain, she began to shiver and had a bad lingering sore throat and cold that made her thin and weak. Early in 1855, after a visit to Gawthorpe and a long walk over damp ground in thin shoes, she was attacked by "new sensations of perpetual nausea, and ever-recurring faintness"; a doctor who was called assigned a natural cause for her indisposition. The sickness increased until the very sight of food caused nausea. On 15 February 1855 she wrote that she had been confined to bed for three weeks, that up to then she had had excellent health since her marriage, and that she was much reduced and very weak. Tabitha Ackroyd, the family's elderly servant, died during Charlotte's illness; a literary historian writes that she died of a digestive infection six weeks before Charlotte. About the third week of March 1855 a low wandering delirium came on, in which she begged constantly for food; she died early on Saturday 31 March 1855 at Haworth, in the 39th year of her age. Her death certificate gave the cause of death as phthisis, a term for pulmonary tuberculosis. She is widely reported to have been in the early stages of pregnancy when she died; Gaskell records that her servant cheered her with the thought of the baby that was coming, and a letter of hers suggests that she was pregnant. The death certificate does not mention pregnancy.
- **R:** Her mother's illness may have been cancer; one reference work says uterine cancer. The cause is a later interpretation. Hyperemesis gravidarum has been proposed as the cause of her final illness and death. A 2019 clinical nutrition paper agrees that hyperemesis gravidarum is the most likely diagnosis and suggests that the refeeding syndrome, after her appetite returned in the third week of March, caused her death; it argues there is no more than circumstantial evidence for tuberculosis and that Addison's disease fits poorly. A bacterial infection such as typhoid has also been suggested, possibly caught from the servant who died weeks earlier. A 1991 obstetrics paper doubts that she was ever pregnant and argues that tuberculosis with secondary Addison disease explains her terminal illness. Tuberculosis as the cause of her death, as the death certificate records, is disputed; the diagnosis was made without modern tests, and later writers have proposed hyperemesis gravidarum, refeeding syndrome, typhoid, and Addison disease instead.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
