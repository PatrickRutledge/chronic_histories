Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Marcus Aurelius was born at Rome, in a villa on the Caelian Hill, on the sixth day before the Kalends of May (26 April in the Julian calendar) in 121 CE. [D]
  • He was educated in rhetoric by Fronto, was adopted by the emperor Antoninus Pius in 138 CE, became emperor in 161 CE, at first jointly with Lucius Verus, and ruled alone from 169 CE. [D]
  • Much of his reign was spent on campaign in central Europe, and the Meditations were probably written there, about 171 to 175 CE; the first book ends 'Among the Quadi at the Granua' and the second 'This in Carnuntum'. [D]
  • A great pestilence struck the empire during his reign, from 165 CE, spread with the Parthian war and the wars on the Danube; the Life of Marcus says the dead were removed in carts and wagons and that thousands died, many nobles among them. Galen witnessed the epidemic. [D]
  • The cause of the Antonine Plague is uncertain: Galen's case reports suggest smallpox to many authors, but paleopathological confirmation is lacking. [R]
  • Cassius Dio, a senator of the period, writes that he was so frail in body that at first he could not endure the cold, that he ate very little and only at night, and that by day he took nothing but the drug called theriac, 'not so much because he feared anything, as because his stomach and chest were in bad condition'. [D]
  • In the Meditations he thanks the gods that remedies were shown to him in dreams, among them remedies 'against bloodspitting and giddiness', and that his body had held out so long in such a kind of life. [D]
  • Galen became court physician to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus, and prepared his theriac at the court; Marcus Aurelius took theriac daily, to protect against poisons and for his general health. [D]
  • Theriac was a compound remedy of many ingredients, among them viper's flesh, opium, honey, wine and cinnamon. [D]
  • Galen relates that the emperor disliked the drowsiness the theriac caused, so the poppy juice was left out; he then could not sleep, and the opium-containing preparation was resumed. [D]
  • Whether he was dependent on or addicted to opium is disputed. Thomas Africa argued for addiction in 1961; later reviews find no explicit ancient description of opium dependence, call his the one putative case, and favour the most parsimonious reading, that the opium was medicinal rather than hedonic. [R]
  • As a boy he studied so hard that it impaired his health, which the Life of Marcus calls the only fault to be found in his entire childhood. [D]

Guideline: 2021 AHA/ACC/ASE/CHEST/SAEM/SCCT/SCMR Guideline for the Evaluation and Diagnosis of Chest Pain (2021); CMID: Clinical Monitoring Intelligence Database, hyperlipidemia and ischemic heart disease monographs (2026) [S]
  • Respectful review of the opium-containing remedy: what it is, why he takes it, and what it does for him; a DSM-5-TR assessment; a prescription drug monitoring program check; a urine drug test with his consent, explained as part of care; naloxone at home; no alcohol, sleep medicines or other sedatives on the same night; never more than his usual amount; stored closed and out of reach; how opioids slow the gut; no forced or abrupt taper, and any reduction his own choice, made slowly; reviewed at every visit, with the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.
