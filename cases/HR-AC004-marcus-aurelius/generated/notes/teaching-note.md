# Teaching Note — Marcus Aurelius pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Marcus Aurelius was born at Rome, in a villa on the Caelian Hill, on the sixth day before the Kalends of May (26 April in the Julian calendar) in 121 CE. He was educated in rhetoric by Fronto, was adopted by the emperor Antoninus Pius in 138 CE, became emperor in 161 CE, at first jointly with Lucius Verus, and ruled alone from 169 CE. Much of his reign was spent on campaign in central Europe, and the Meditations were probably written there, about 171 to 175 CE; the first book ends 'Among the Quadi at the Granua' and the second 'This in Carnuntum'. A great pestilence struck the empire during his reign, from 165 CE, spread with the Parthian war and the wars on the Danube; the Life of Marcus says the dead were removed in carts and wagons and that thousands died, many nobles among them. Galen witnessed the epidemic. Cassius Dio, a senator of the period, writes that he was so frail in body that at first he could not endure the cold, that he ate very little and only at night, and that by day he took nothing but the drug called theriac, 'not so much because he feared anything, as because his stomach and chest were in bad condition'. In a speech that Dio gives him, addressed to his soldiers after hearing of the revolt of Avidius Cassius, he describes himself as already an old man and weak, 'unable to take either food without pain or sleep without anxiety'. In the Meditations he thanks the gods that remedies were shown to him in dreams, among them remedies 'against bloodspitting and giddiness', and that his body had held out so long in such a kind of life. Galen became court physician to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus, and prepared his theriac at the court; Marcus Aurelius took theriac daily, to protect against poisons and for his general health. Theriac was a compound remedy of many ingredients, among them viper's flesh, opium, honey, wine and cinnamon. Galen relates that the emperor disliked the drowsiness the theriac caused, so the poppy juice was left out; he then could not sleep, and the opium-containing preparation was resumed. He died on 17 March (Julian) 180 CE, on campaign, at 58; Dio gives his age as fifty-eight years, ten months and twenty-two days. The Life of Marcus says that, eager to die, he refrained from food and drink, worsening the disease, sent his son away for fear he would catch it, and died on the seventh day. Tertullian places his death at Sirmium and Aurelius Victor at Vindobona (Vienna). As a boy he studied so hard that it impaired his health, which the Life of Marcus calls the only fault to be found in his entire childhood.
- **R:** The cause of the Antonine Plague is uncertain: Galen's case reports suggest smallpox to many authors, but paleopathological confirmation is lacking. Whether he was dependent on or addicted to opium is disputed. Thomas Africa argued for addiction in 1961; later reviews find no explicit ancient description of opium dependence, call his the one putative case, and favour the most parsimonious reading, that the opium was medicinal rather than hedonic. The cause of his death is unknown. Dio reports being told that the physicians hastened it to please Commodus; the Life of Marcus suggests a contagious illness, and its editor notes that the plague theory is not well supported.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
