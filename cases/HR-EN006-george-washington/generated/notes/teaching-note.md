# Teaching Note — George Washington pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** George Washington was born on February 22, 1732, at Popes Creek farm in Westmoreland County, Virginia (February 11 in the Julian, or Old Style, calendar then in use in England and its colonies). He died at Mount Vernon late in the evening of December 14, 1799, at 67, of an infection of the throat that began with a sore throat and hoarseness the day before; he was bled four times, about thirty-two ounces at the last bleeding, and the National Library of Medicine exhibition states that he lost three quarts, or half his blood volume. In November 1751, at 19, on his only journey outside mainland North America, he went to Barbados with his older half-brother, who was seeking relief from tuberculosis; there he wrote that he was "strongly attacked with the small Pox," was in bed for about three weeks, and recovered with slight scarring and lifelong immunity. Through his adult life he had repeated attacks of malarial fever, a common summer and autumn illness at Mount Vernon, which he treated with Peruvian (cinchona) bark. In June 1755, as an aide on the Braddock expedition, he had dysentery with "violt Fevers & P[ai]ns in my h[ea]d," travelled lying in a wagon, rode into battle on cushions, and took about five weeks to recover; the National Library of Medicine exhibition says he nearly died of it. He had poor dental health from his twenties, with regular toothaches, decay and tooth loss; at 24 he paid a "Doctr Watson" to remove a tooth, and through his life he wrote of aching teeth, lost teeth, inflamed gums and ill-fitting dentures and bought toothbrushes, scrapers, toothache medicine and cleaning solutions. By 1781, missing several teeth, he wore false teeth wired to his remaining natural teeth; a dentist treated him in New York in 1783, and he sent a wax and plaster model of his mouth to a dentist so that more false teeth could be made. In May 1784 his plantation accounts recorded a cash payment to enslaved people at Mount Vernon for nine teeth, "on acc[oun]t of the French Dentis Doctr Lemay," a dentist then visiting the area and offering tooth transplants. Mount Vernon reads the entry as teeth bought for the dentist, does not know whether any were later used for him, and notes that the threat of punishment was always present for enslaved people, so that they could have felt compelled to provide them. When he was inaugurated as president in 1789, only one natural tooth remained. That year a New York dentist made the first of four sets of full dentures for him, of hippopotamus ivory with human teeth, gold wire springs and brass screws, leaving a hole for the remaining tooth. All his dentures caused him pain and changed the shape of his face, pushing out his lower lip, and he found them difficult to use when eating or speaking. His last natural tooth was removed in 1796, when he was 64, and he ordered a new set of dentures; in 1797 he wrote to his dentist that his dentures were "uneasy in the mouth and bulge my lips out" and had "by degrees, worked loose." To try to avoid an early death he exercised, ate and drank moderately, tried to get enough sleep, and avoided tobacco. He was inaugurated as the first president at Federal Hall in New York on April 30, 1789, and lived at 3 Cherry Street until February 1790, when the household, which included his family, many enslaved workers and often some of his advisers, moved to a larger house at 39 Broadway. Less than two months after the inauguration he had "a very large and painful tumor on the protuberance of my thigh" that kept him from walking or sitting; after weeks of treatment, two physicians, father and son, removed it on June 17, 1789, and the wound took six weeks to heal, while he lay mostly on his right side. A similar, milder tumor returned two years later. In New York in January 1790 his diary records that he was "At home all day—not well" on January 17 and on January 18 "Still indisposed with an Aching tooth, and swelled and inflamed Gum." On May 9, 1790, in New York, he was "Indisposed with a bad cold"; on May 10 he was seized with "a severe illness" that left him convalescent for several weeks. The editors of his diaries write that the cold developed into pneumonia; three New York physicians attended him, and by May 15 his life was despaired of before he began to recover. Mount Vernon describes the illness as influenza and pneumonia that affected his hearing and eyesight. After recovering he wrote that within less than a year he had had "two severe attacks—the last worse than the first," more and severer sickness than in the thirty preceding years, and that he still felt "the remains of the violent affection of my lungs—The cough, pain in my breast, and shortness in breathing." He enslaved people throughout his adult life: at least 577 enslaved people lived and worked at Mount Vernon over the course of his life, and 317 were held there when he died. His will ordered that the people he owned be freed after his wife's death, which applied to fewer than half of those in bondage at Mount Vernon.
- **R:** His final illness is usually interpreted today as acute epiglottitis: two medical-history reviews and a 2020 otolaryngology article name it, one concluding that massive bloodletting, shock and respiratory failure made death inevitable, and the exhibition notes that some have suggested the bloodletting and purging hastened his death; a 2021 infectious-disease review re-examines the diagnosis and calls it a rapidly progressive upper respiratory infection. The four surviving dentures include hippopotamus, walrus and probably elephant ivory; cow, horse and human teeth; and lead, brass, silver, gold and small wood pegs. Two contain human teeth, whose source is unknown: Mount Vernon writes that dentists then kept a stock of teeth bought from poor people, enslaved or free, or taken from the dead, and that teeth from enslaved people may be in them but no record shows it. What the 1789 thigh tumor was is not settled in the sources read. Mount Vernon calls it a tumor; the National Library of Medicine exhibition lists anthrax among his serious illnesses without saying when; the roster calls it a carbuncle.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
