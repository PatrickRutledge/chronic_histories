History of Present Illness

Mr. Washington, a 57-year-old head of government, is seen at the request of Dr. Pritchard-Boateng. He lost his teeth gradually over about thirty years and has worn complete dentures for more than eight years. The lower denture has become loose and painful; it lifts when he chews or speaks, and the upper clicks. He has limited his diet to soft food and lost weight. For two weeks he has kept the lower denture out except at meals, and the soreness has gone. He cleans the dentures daily. He has never used tobacco and has no diabetes or bone-modifying treatment. His work requires frequent public speaking and dining. [S]

  • George Washington was born on February 22, 1732, at Popes Creek farm in Westmoreland County, Virginia (February 11 in the Julian, or Old Style, calendar then in use in England and its colonies). [D]
  • In November 1751, at 19, on his only journey outside mainland North America, he went to Barbados with his older half-brother, who was seeking relief from tuberculosis; there he wrote that he was "strongly attacked with the small Pox," was in bed for about three weeks, and recovered with slight scarring and lifelong immunity. [D]
  • Through his adult life he had repeated attacks of malarial fever, a common summer and autumn illness at Mount Vernon, which he treated with Peruvian (cinchona) bark. [D]
  • In June 1755, as an aide on the Braddock expedition, he had dysentery with "violt Fevers & P[ai]ns in my h[ea]d," travelled lying in a wagon, rode into battle on cushions, and took about five weeks to recover; the National Library of Medicine exhibition says he nearly died of it. [D]
  • He had poor dental health from his twenties, with regular toothaches, decay and tooth loss; at 24 he paid a "Doctr Watson" to remove a tooth, and through his life he wrote of aching teeth, lost teeth, inflamed gums and ill-fitting dentures and bought toothbrushes, scrapers, toothache medicine and cleaning solutions. [D]
  • By 1781, missing several teeth, he wore false teeth wired to his remaining natural teeth; a dentist treated him in New York in 1783, and he sent a wax and plaster model of his mouth to a dentist so that more false teeth could be made. [D]
  • In May 1784 his plantation accounts recorded a cash payment to enslaved people at Mount Vernon for nine teeth, "on acc[oun]t of the French Dentis Doctr Lemay," a dentist then visiting the area and offering tooth transplants. Mount Vernon reads the entry as teeth bought for the dentist, does not know whether any were later used for him, and notes that the threat of punishment was always present for enslaved people, so that they could have felt compelled to provide them. [D]
  • When he was inaugurated as president in 1789, only one natural tooth remained. That year a New York dentist made the first of four sets of full dentures for him, of hippopotamus ivory with human teeth, gold wire springs and brass screws, leaving a hole for the remaining tooth. [D]
  • All his dentures caused him pain and changed the shape of his face, pushing out his lower lip, and he found them difficult to use when eating or speaking. [D]
  • The four surviving dentures include hippopotamus, walrus and probably elephant ivory; cow, horse and human teeth; and lead, brass, silver, gold and small wood pegs. Two contain human teeth, whose source is unknown: Mount Vernon writes that dentists then kept a stock of teeth bought from poor people, enslaved or free, or taken from the dead, and that teeth from enslaved people may be in them but no record shows it. [R]
  • To try to avoid an early death he exercised, ate and drank moderately, tried to get enough sleep, and avoided tobacco. [D]
  • He was inaugurated as the first president at Federal Hall in New York on April 30, 1789, and lived at 3 Cherry Street until February 1790, when the household, which included his family, many enslaved workers and often some of his advisers, moved to a larger house at 39 Broadway. [D]
  • Less than two months after the inauguration he had "a very large and painful tumor on the protuberance of my thigh" that kept him from walking or sitting; after weeks of treatment, two physicians, father and son, removed it on June 17, 1789, and the wound took six weeks to heal, while he lay mostly on his right side. A similar, milder tumor returned two years later. [D]
  • What the 1789 thigh tumor was is not settled in the sources read. Mount Vernon calls it a tumor; the National Library of Medicine exhibition lists anthrax among his serious illnesses without saying when; the roster calls it a carbuncle. [R]
  • In New York in January 1790 his diary records that he was "At home all day—not well" on January 17 and on January 18 "Still indisposed with an Aching tooth, and swelled and inflamed Gum." [D]

The worn dentures with a closed bite account for the pain, the instability and the change in the support of his lips.   • Implant candidacy (K08.103).
