## Assessment

1. **Tourette syndrome** (F95.2). Multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic, beginning in childhood and lasting decades, with premonitory urges, temporary suppressibility, and waxing and waning course. This meets criteria for Tourette syndrome. The movements are stereotyped and preceded by an urge, unlike chorea, which is random and flowing; the examination is otherwise normal, and no imaging or further tests are needed. The tics are moderate and cause real social impairment. **S**
2. **Obsessive-compulsive symptoms**. Counting and threshold rituals driven by a need for things to feel just right, with a mild Y-BOCS of 13, are common in Tourette syndrome. They do not need separate treatment now; sertraline treats obsessive-compulsive symptoms as well as depression, and they can be reassessed as his mood improves. **S**
3. **Depression and tics** (F33.2). Depression and anxiety commonly accompany Tourette syndrome and often cause more impairment than the tics themselves. Treating the depression comes first. Sertraline does not worsen tics. Tics are not a sign of mental decline, and telling him so matters. **S**

## Recommendations

1. Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention for Tics (CBIT) as the initial tic treatment: eight sessions over about ten weeks with a trained therapist, starting in about two weeks.
2. No tic medication now; if tics remain impairing after CBIT, an alpha-2 agonist such as guanfacine is a first medication option, and aripiprazole or another agent would need cardiac and metabolic monitoring.
3. Continue sertraline and adjust it for the depression as Dr. Bell plans; avoid adding serotonergic drugs such as tramadol without review.
4. Yale Global Tic Severity Scale at three and six months, then yearly; reassess obsessive-compulsive symptoms when the depression has lifted.
5. Psychoeducation about the nature and natural history of tics, for him and, if he wishes, for those he lives with.
