Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Samuel Johnson was born at Lichfield, in Staffordshire, on September 18, 1709 (New Style), and was baptized the same day at St. Mary's church. [D]
  • His father, Michael Johnson, a Lichfield bookseller and magistrate, had "a vile melancholy," which Johnson said he inherited and which "made him mad all his life, at least not sober." [D]
  • As a young child he had scrofula (the king's evil), which scarred his face and damaged his sight so that he did not see at all with one of his eyes; Boswell thought him only near-sighted. On the advice of the Lichfield physician Sir John Floyer, his mother took him to London to be touched by Queen Anne, without effect. [D]
  • In the college vacation of 1729, when he was about twenty, he was "overwhelmed with an horrible hypochondria," with irritation, fretfulness, impatience, dejection, gloom, and despair; he walked to Birmingham and back to fight it, wrote an account of his case in Latin for his godfather, the physician Dr. Swinfen, and was never afterwards perfectly relieved of it. [D]
  • Insanity was "the object of his most dismal apprehension," and he at times feared he was losing his reason, when his friends saw no sign of it. [D]
  • His wife, Elizabeth, died on March 17, 1752 (Old Style), a loss that affected him with the deepest distress; he kept the anniversary of her death with prayer. [D]
  • In 1764 he had "a very severe return of the hypochondriack disorder": though he loved company he became entirely averse to society, and his friend Dr. Adams found him "sighing, groaning, talking to himself, and restlessly walking from room to room," saying "I would consent to have a limb amputated to recover my spirits." [D]
  • On his birthday in September 1764 he wrote a prayer: "I have now spent fifty-five years in resolving ... I have done nothing." [D]
  • Boswell described how, talking or musing in his chair, he held his head to one side toward his right shoulder and shook it, rocked his body backwards and forwards, and rubbed his left knee with his palm; between words he made sounds like chewing the cud, a half whistle, clucking like a hen, and "too, too, too" under his breath, and after an argument he blew out his breath "like a Whale." He also often talked to himself, muttering fragments of the Lord's Prayer. [D]
  • He had an "anxious care" to go in or out of a door by a certain number of steps, or with a particular foot first; Boswell saw him stop, count his steps, and go back to begin again when he had gone wrong, and Joshua Reynolds saw him go a long way round rather than cross a particular alley in Leicester Fields. [D]
  • Alexander Pope, in a note, described him as having "an infirmity of the convulsive kind, that attacks him sometimes, so as to make him a sad Spectacle"; Boswell likened the movements to St. Vitus's dance; Reynolds wrote that they were improperly called convulsions, since he could sit motionless when told to, and thought them a habit of accompanying his thoughts with untoward actions, and that Johnson preferred any company to being alone, the great business of his life being "to escape from himself." [D]
  • When he was young his appearance was forbidding: he was lean, the scars of the scrofula were deeply visible, and his "convulsive starts and odd gesticulations" tended "to excite at once surprize and ridicule." [D]
  • In 1979 the neurologist T. J. Murray concluded that Johnson's involuntary jerking movements and complex motor acts, involuntary vocalizations, and compulsive actions such as touching posts and measuring his steps constitute the symptom complex of Gilles de la Tourette syndrome, from which he suffered most of his life; later neurologists agreed, and historians have examined how the diagnosis was constructed. [R]
