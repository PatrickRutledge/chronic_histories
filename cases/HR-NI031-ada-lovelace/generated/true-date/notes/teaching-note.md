# Teaching Note — Ada Lovelace pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Augusta Ada Byron was born on December 10, 1815, in Piccadilly, London, the daughter of the poet Lord Byron and Anne Isabella Byron. She was often ill as a child. In 1829, at 13, she caught measles, from which she took a long while to recover; she was bedridden for a whole year. On July 8, 1835, she married William King, who was created an earl in 1838, making her Countess of Lovelace. Her health suffered after a bout of cholera in 1837, and she had lingering problems with asthma and her digestive system. Her doctors gave her painkillers such as laudanum and opium; she reportedly had mood swings and hallucinations. Between 1842 and 1843 she translated L. F. Menabrea's article on Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine and added her own extensive Notes, published in 1843 under the initials A.A.L. The Notes, which include a method for computing Bernoulli numbers on the engine, are often described as the first published computer program. Her health, always fragile, declined sharply after 1843. By the end of that year she was taking several medicines for different health problems; in 1844 she wrote that a recent illness had left her 'utterly unable to think even of my studies', and she thought of writing a scientific study of the effects of opium and wine based on her own experience. In the 1840s she had what she called heart or rheumatic attacks. Gambling on horses was a passion of her later years, and she pawned some of her jewels to finance it. As she lay dying it came out that she had led a ruinous horse-betting ring, borrowed money from shady people, and pawned her diamonds twice; she owed about 2,000 pounds in gambling debts when she died. By 1851 she was extremely sick with uterine cancer. By January 1852 she was wracked with pain as the cancer became more acute. She died on November 27, 1852, in Marylebone, London, at 36, the age at which her father had died, and was buried at her request next to her father at the Church of St. Mary Magdalene in Hucknall, Nottinghamshire.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
