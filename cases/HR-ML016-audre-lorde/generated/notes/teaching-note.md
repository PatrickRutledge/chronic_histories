# Teaching Note — Audre Lorde pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Audre Lorde was born Audrey Geraldine Lorde on February 18, 1934, in New York City; her parents were immigrants from Grenada. Lorde was a poet, essayist, and novelist. In her poetry and prose she wrote about matters important to her as a woman of color, as a lesbian, as a mother, and as a feminist; a reference biography describes her as an African American lesbian feminist and poet. In 1977 a doctor found a tumor in Lorde's breast that, after about three weeks of uncertainty, proved benign. In a talk given that year to the Modern Language Association she said: "Your silence will not protect you." On Labor Day 1978, during a routine self-examination, Lorde found a lump in her right breast; a diagnosis of malignancy and a mastectomy followed. She described the experience in The Cancer Journals. After her mastectomy Lorde chose never to hide the fact of her missing breast. She refused to wear the "wad of lambswool pressed into a pale pink breast-shaped pad" pushed on her by nurses and medical counselors, and she was repeatedly scolded for posing a threat to cancer patient "morale." The Cancer Journals (Spinsters, Ink, 1980), her first prose collection, combines journal entries from her hospital stay and recovery with essays; it won the American Library Association Gay Caucus Book of the Year award for 1981. In 1984, six years after her mastectomy, Lorde learned that her breast cancer had spread to her liver. This time she chose alternative treatments rather than more surgery, and she wrote about it in the title essay of A Burst of Light. A Burst of Light, published by Firebrand in 1988, won a book award from the Before Columbus Foundation. Lorde was the poet laureate of New York State in 1991 and 1992. Lorde died on November 17, 1992, at Christiansted on St. Croix in the U.S. Virgin Islands, at 58. Sources give the cause as liver cancer, the spread of her breast cancer.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
