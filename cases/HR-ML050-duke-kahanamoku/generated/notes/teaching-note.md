# Teaching Note — Duke Kahanamoku pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Duke Kahanamoku was born on August 24, 1890, in Honolulu, at Haleʻākala, the Honolulu home of Princess Bernice Pauahi Bishop; he was named after his father, who had been named Duke for a visit of the Duke of Edinburgh to Hawaiʻi. Olympedia gives his full name as Duke Paoa Kahino Makoe Hulikohoa Kahanamoku. He won the Olympic 100-meter freestyle in 1912 at Stockholm (with silver in the 4 x 200-meter relay) and again in 1920 at Antwerp (with gold in the relay), and silver in the 100-meter freestyle in 1924 at Paris: three gold and two silver medals. He co-founded the Hui Nalu surf club in 1911, introduced surfing to the Atlantic coast of the United States in 1914 and gave a surfing exhibition at Freshwater Beach in Sydney on December 24, 1914, widely regarded as a seminal event for surfing in Australia; he was inducted into the International Swimming Hall of Fame in 1965 and was the first inductee of the International Surfing Hall of Fame in 1966. From 1922 he lived in Los Angeles for years and found small roles in many Hollywood films. He was elected sheriff of Honolulu for 13 consecutive terms, from 1934 until the post was eliminated in 1960, after which he was named Hawaiʻi's "Ambassador of Aloha"; other sources date his first election to 1932 and his departure to 1961. He married Nadine Alexander on August 2, 1940, in Kailua-Kona. He never drank alcohol or smoked and trained consistently. He had a heart attack in 1952 and suffered several heart attacks in his later years, continuing to swim and sail almost to the end of his life. In 1962 he had a cerebral blood clot and survived a brain operation. On January 22, 1968, he collapsed with a heart attack at the Waikiki Yacht Club while working on his boat, was taken to Kaiser Foundation Hospital and died there, aged 77. On January 27, 1968, his ashes were scattered off Waikīkī in a beachboy funeral attended by thousands. He had no great personal wealth. He was the official United States representative at the 1956 Melbourne Olympic Games.
