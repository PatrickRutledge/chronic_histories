# Teaching Note — Higuchi Ichiyō pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Higuchi Ichiyō, whose given name was Natsu (also written Natsuko), was born in Tokyo on the 25th day of the 3rd month of Meiji 5 in the Japanese lunisolar calendar, which is 2 May 1872 in the Gregorian calendar, in official housing within the grounds of the Tokyo prefectural office in Uchisaiwai-chō (now in Chiyoda). Japan replaced its lunisolar calendar with the Gregorian calendar at the end of Meiji 5: the 3rd day of the 12th month of Meiji 5 became 1 January 1873 (Meiji 6). Dates before then, including her birth, were recorded in the old calendar. From 1886 she studied classical poetry (waka) at the Haginoya academy of Nakajima Utako. Her father died of illness in the summer of 1889, when she was 17, leaving debts from a failed business; she became head of the household and took on the support of her mother and younger sister, who did washing and needlework, and the family lived in poverty. In 1891 she became a pupil of the novelist Nakarai Tōsui and began to write fiction, choosing to earn her living by writing at a time when this was very hard for a woman; her story Umoregi (1892) brought her into the literary world. From July 1893 to April 1894 she ran a small shop selling household goods and cheap sweets in Shitaya Ryūsenji-chō, beside the Yoshiwara pleasure quarter (now in Taitō); the shop closed after about ten months, and her life there became material for Takekurabe. In May 1894 she moved to Hongō Maruyama-Fukuyama-chō (now Nishikata, Bunkyō). At Maruyama-Fukuyama-chō she wrote the works that made her name, among them Ōtsugomori (1894), Nigorie (1895), Takekurabe (1895 to 1896) and Jūsan'ya; Nippon.com calls the period 'the miraculous 14 months', and the Nipponica encyclopedia calls the time up to January 1896 'the miraculous year'. She died in poverty. From the beginning of 1896 her health declined rapidly. She died of tuberculosis on 23 November 1896 at her home in Hongō, at the age of 24. The National Diet Library gives the cause as lung tuberculosis; the Nipponica encyclopedia gives miliary tuberculosis. Her portrait appears on the Bank of Japan 5,000-yen note first issued on 1 November 2004, whose back shows Ogata Kōrin's painting of irises; a new 5,000-yen note with a different portrait was first issued on 3 July 2024.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
