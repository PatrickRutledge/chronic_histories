## Editorial resolutions

- **Recent-death rule.** Hepburn died in 2003. The case uses only what she said publicly (her 1993 documentary statement as quoted in a TIME obituary, and her remarks as recalled by a magazine editor who interviewed her), the tremor that was visible in her public work, and basic biographical facts and the executor’s public statement on her death. No physician of hers made a public statement about the tremor that was found. No medical records, accounts by relatives, or speculation about undisclosed conditions are used. Her scores, examination findings beyond the visible head tremor, medicines, therapy, and preventive care are synthetic. The recent-death review rule applies.
- **Diagnosis label.** Sources conflict. Her own public account was a shaking head inherited from her grandfather, and she denied having Parkinson’s disease; an encyclopedia article, a public radio report, and a physician’s newspaper column call it essential tremor; an Associated Press obituary described “tremors similar to Parkinson’s disease”; and several sources say it was widely mistaken for Parkinson’s disease. The essential tremor label is therefore graded **R** (certainty probable). The modern record codes essential tremor (G25.0) with verification provisional and placement both_with_link, as the brief requires for a contested label, and the movement disorders consultation applies the MDS consensus criteria in the synthetic episode only. Parkinson’s disease is not coded.
- **Voice tremor.** The only fetched description of her voice tremor (an obituary calling her voice in her last years “quivery”) describes a later period than the episode. The mild voice tremor at 72, and its coding as R49.8, are synthetic extrapolations anchored to that description, kept mild so they do not read as a claim about her health at that age.
- **Family history.** The paternal grandfather’s shaking head is recorded only as she described it publicly (grade D); no diagnosis is attributed to him, and no other relative is mentioned. A niece’s public comments, reported by secondary sites, were not used.
- **Index age.** The modern episode presents Hepburn at 72, when her head tremor was already visible in her film work. The synthetic date of birth is May 12, 1953 (shift 46 years). The index visit on January 13, 2026 maps to January 13, 1980; the chronic-care visits on February 10, May 5, and August 4, 2026 map to the same days of 1980; and the annual wellness visit on October 6, 2026 maps to October 6, 1980, when she was 73. Past medical history includes only the tremor visible before the index date; the 1981 film, the 1991 autobiography, and the 1993 documentary are claims only.
- **Synthetic clinical picture.** The visible side-to-side head tremor is from the public record. Everything else is synthetic and kept to findings typical of essential tremor and to normal or negative results, so that nothing reads as a claim about undisclosed private health: bilateral postural and kinetic hand tremor, a mild voice tremor, no signs beyond tremor, MoCA 28, PHQ-9 2 and 3, normal TSH and chemistry, no falls, and no asthma or heart disease. The propranolol treatment and its benefit, the voice therapy, the occupational therapy, and the preventive care are synthetic; her actual treatment, if any, was not made public. TETRAS, spiral drawing, Timed Up and Go, and seated and standing blood pressure have no verified LOINC code and are recorded as narrative.
- **Annual wellness visit.** A subsequent Medicare annual wellness visit (age 73, so `annual_wellness_subsequent`) was added on October 6, 2026 (true frame October 6, 1980), two months after the six-month review. No sick visit was added: under the recent-death rule the case adds no synthetic illness episode. Judgment calls: (1) the health risk assessment records no tobacco and an occasional drink (AUDIT-C 1); these are synthetic, and no fetched source about her drinking habits was used; (2) the mammogram, DXA, stool test, and hepatitis C test are ordered with no results recorded; (3) advance care planning names an unnamed health care agent and records only general priorities (independence, continuing to work, being told plainly), with no treatment preference, so nothing reads as a claim about her real choices; (4) supports are a household assistant and friends, and no relative is named or given a statement.
- **Focused visits.** The three chronic-care follow-ups (four weeks, three months, and six months after the first visit) are documented as focused visits on the AAN essential tremor guidelines, the MDS classification and treatment review, and CMID general items for older adults, because the CMID has no tremor monograph. Each lists every item those protocols call for at that point, with its interval and status. The four-week visit is the same day as the movement disorders consultation. Protocol deviations recorded instead of invented results: (1) the 3-minute standing blood pressure was not measured at the three-month visit after propranolol was started; she had no lightheadedness or falls, and the standing pressure at six months showed no drop, so no harm is judged to have resulted; (2) the occupational therapy referral recommended at four weeks was not booked for five months; she managed without injury, and the evaluation was done two weeks after the six-month visit. Primidone, botulinum toxin, and focused ultrasound or deep brain stimulation are recorded as not indicated, with the reason.
- **Consultation.** The movement disorders referral and the consultation by Tomasz Wierzbicki, MD (a fictional consultant), on the day of the four-week visit, are **S**. The consultant answers the six referral questions with what the record shows happened afterwards: essential tremor by the MDS criteria with no imaging, propranolol extended-release 60 mg with pulse and standing blood pressure checks and a repeat PHQ-9, primidone held in reserve, voice therapy and occupational therapy, botulinum toxin only if head or voice tremor becomes disabling, focused ultrasound or deep brain stimulation only after failed adequate trials of both first-line medicines, and primary care follow-up every three to six months. The examination adds only findings needed for the classification (no head turning or null point, no dystonic posture, normal tone and tapping), all consistent with Dr. Price’s same-day note.
- **Not used.** A hip or ankle fracture during a 1976 stage tour (the sources disagree on which bone), a 1982 car accident, her later hospital stays, the cause of death given by some secondary sources, a chronic eye problem, and accounts by relatives were not used. Her height is not recorded in a fetched source; the 170 cm in the record is synthetic.
