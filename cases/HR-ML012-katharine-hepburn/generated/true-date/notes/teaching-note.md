# Teaching Note — Katharine Hepburn pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Katharine Hepburn was born in Hartford, Connecticut, on May 12, 1907. Hepburn died on June 29, 2003, at her home in Old Saybrook, Connecticut, at 96. The executor of her estate said she had been in declining health in recent years and died of old age. By the 1980s Hepburn had a noticeable tremor that left her head constantly shaking; it was noticeable by the time of a 1979 television film and worsened later in her life. Hepburn spoke publicly about the tremor. In a 1993 documentary she said that she had inherited her shaking head from her grandfather Hepburn, and she denied the common belief that she had Parkinson's disease. A magazine editor who interviewed her recalled that she called it a familial tremor inherited from her grandfather. An obituary described her famous voice in her last years as quivery. Hepburn won a fourth Academy Award for Best Actress for On Golden Pond (1981) and published her autobiography, Me: Stories of My Life, in 1991.
- **R:** Her tremor is identified as essential tremor by an encyclopedia article, a public radio report, and a physician's newspaper column, all of which note that it was widely mistaken for Parkinson's disease; an Associated Press obituary described tremors similar to Parkinson's disease. No public statement by a physician who examined her was found.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
