# Teaching Note — Marilyn Monroe pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** She was born Norma Jeane Mortenson on June 1, 1926, at Los Angeles General Hospital. Her father's identity is unconfirmed; her mother was often confined to asylums, and she spent her childhood in orphanages and foster homes. She worked as a photographic model, signed with Twentieth Century-Fox in 1946 under the name Marilyn Monroe, starred in films of the 1950s including Gentlemen Prefer Blondes and The Seven Year Itch, and studied at the Actors Studio in the mid-1950s. Her mother, Gladys, was diagnosed with paranoid schizophrenia and was institutionalized, for the first time at the state hospital in Norwalk. She is described as one of the most famous people with endometriosis. Her biographer Anthony Summers wrote that the condition was so severe that it destroyed her marriages, her wish for children, her career and ultimately her life; a review of endometriosis for clinicians quotes this account, and museum and medical-history writers list endometriosis among her health problems. She married the playwright Arthur Miller on June 29, 1956. She had a miscarriage in September 1956, lost an ectopic pregnancy in August 1957, and in all is reported to have had two miscarriages and an ectopic pregnancy. In February 1961, after telling a doctor that she had considered suicide, she was admitted to the Payne Whitney Clinic in New York; her stay was brief. Most of the drugs she misused had been prescribed to her by physicians; they included the barbiturate Nembutal and chloral hydrate. She died at her Los Angeles home in August 1962, at 36. The cause of death was officially ruled a drug overdose.
- **R:** The same biographical account holds that, in the days before effective conservative surgery or medical therapy, her endometriosis led to progressively increasing use of strong analgesics, tranquilizers and hypnotics, and to drug dependency. The physician and historian Howard Markel writes that she had several mental health problems, including substance abuse, depression and, most likely, bipolar disorder, as well as endometriosis and gall bladder disease. A retrospective psychological autopsy based on biographies, films and her writings proposes autism spectrum traits progressing to borderline personality disorder and bipolar disorder; the roster lists borderline personality disorder and depression.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
