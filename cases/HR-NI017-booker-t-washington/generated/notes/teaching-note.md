# Teaching Note — Booker T. Washington pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Booker T. Washington was born into slavery on the James Burroughs plantation in Franklin County, Virginia, in 1856; the National Park Service timeline gives the date as April 5, 1856. Washington wrote in Up from Slavery (1901) that he was not sure of the exact date of his birth and estimated the year as 1858 or 1859; the April 5, 1856 date traces to a report by his brother John after his death and was adopted by the Tuskegee trustees. After emancipation in 1865 Washington moved with his mother, Jane, and his family to Malden, West Virginia, where he worked in the salt and coal mines; a National Park Service release gives his mother's death as 1876. Washington opened Tuskegee Institute in Tuskegee, Alabama, on July 4, 1881, at age 25, and led it for the rest of his life; in March 1915 he sponsored National Negro Health Week. In 1911 Washington was beaten in New York City and received two large gashes in his head and a torn ear, recovering without apparent sequelae; he had chronic indigestion, especially when traveling, and spent several days in 1911 at the Battle Creek Sanitarium. When Washington was admitted to the Rockefeller Institute hospital on November 2, 1915, his blood pressure was 220/150. He reported palpitations two weeks earlier, tiring quickly, frontal headaches one or two days out of every eight or nine, and slower thinking; the record also notes weight loss, failing vision, frequent urination, and avid thirst. In early November 1915, after several months of failing health, Dr. W. A. Bastedo in New York diagnosed Bright's disease, a contemporary term for kidney disease; Dr. Lucas G. Cole at the Rockefeller Institute hospital agreed, and Washington was moved to St. Luke's Hospital. Washington traveled home from New York and died at Tuskegee on November 14, 1915, at age 59; the New York Times reported hardening of the arteries as the cause, and the National Park Service attributes his death to conditions related to high blood pressure. A 1915 remark by Dr. Bastedo attributing the death partly to 'racial characteristics' was read by Washington's Chicago physician, Dr. George C. Hall, as implying syphilis; the hospital Wassermann test was negative, and the 2006 review did not support that explanation.
- **R:** A 2006 historical clinicopathological conference at the University of Maryland School of Medicine reviewed his hospital record and concluded that he presented with malignant hypertension that caused kidney failure.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
