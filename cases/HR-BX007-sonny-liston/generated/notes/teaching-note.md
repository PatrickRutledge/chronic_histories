# Teaching Note — Sonny Liston pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Charles L. Liston was born in St. Francis County, Arkansas, to sharecroppers Tobe and Helen Liston. His birth date is uncertain: there was no birth certificate (Arkansas did not require them until 1965); he filed for one in 1953 giving May 8, 1932, which the Encyclopedia of Arkansas and the Scribner encyclopedia use with a question; his family's 1930 census record does not list him, so he was probably born no earlier than late 1930; his mother gave several dates; and other sources suggest 1927 or 1928. He was one of many children (the ninth of his mother's ten; one account counts twenty-two siblings and half-siblings), worked full-time in the cotton fields by the age of eight, and was beaten regularly by his father. He had virtually no schooling and never learned to read or write. At thirteen he followed his mother to St. Louis. Arrested in 1950 after a series of robberies, he was sentenced to five years in the Missouri State Penitentiary in Jefferson City, where a Catholic chaplain encouraged him to box; he was paroled in 1952. He turned professional in September 1953 and broke his jaw in a 1954 loss. He won the world heavyweight title in Chicago on September 25, 1962, with a first-round knockout, and lost it in Miami Beach on February 25, 1964, when he did not come out for the seventh round, citing a shoulder injury; he lost the rematch in 1965. His record was 50 wins (39 by knockout) and 4 losses; he moved to Las Vegas in 1966. He was described as a heavy drinker and a reckless driver with frequent arrests. His wife found him dead in their Las Vegas home on January 5, 1971, on returning from a Christmas visit; he had been dead for about a week, and the coroner set the date of death as December 30, 1970. The death was ascribed to pulmonary congestion and heart failure, and the coroner recorded natural causes; traces of heroin and a needle mark on his arm were found, and heroin and marijuana were found in the house. He fought for the last time on June 29, 1970, and entered the International Boxing Hall of Fame in 1991.
- **R:** The cause of his death is disputed: natural causes, an accidental heroin overdose and homicide have all been proposed, and none is established. The project roster describes probable heroin dependency. No source read documents heroin or other opioid use during his life; the findings at his death are disputed.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
