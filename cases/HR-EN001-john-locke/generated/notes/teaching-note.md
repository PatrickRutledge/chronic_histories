# Teaching Note — John Locke pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** John Locke was born on 29 August 1632 (Julian; 8 September 1632, Gregorian) at Wrington, Somerset, to Puritan parents of modest means. His father, John Locke (1606 to 1661), was a country attorney who served in a cavalry company on the Parliamentary side early in the Civil War; his mother was Agnes Keene. He had one brother, Thomas, born in 1637. He was educated at Westminster School and at Christ Church, Oxford, where he was elected to a studentship in 1652 and took his BA in 1656. His father died on 13 February 1660/1 (Julian), at about 54, of consumption or some kindred malady, by his biographer's account; his only brother, Thomas, died of consumption soon afterwards, at about 26, leaving no children. He studied medicine at Oxford and took the degree of Bachelor of Medicine in February 1674/5, with a licence to practise, but never took the doctorate. From 1667 he lived in the household of Lord Ashley (later the first Earl of Shaftesbury) as his physician, and in June 1668 he organized the surgical drainage of a large abscess above Ashley's liver, with advice from leading physicians including Thomas Sydenham. From about 1667 he was a close friend of the physician Thomas Sydenham, accompanied him on visits to patients, helped him with his writings, and was praised by him; Sydenham embraced careful observation of disease. His biographer H. R. Fox Bourne wrote that about 1670 Locke began to have a cough that seriously troubled him through the remainder of his life, and that an undated fragment in his hand from those years, titled 'Tussis', was the start of a paper on coughs. His health was already weak by the early 1670s; a letter from Sydenham, probably at the end of 1674, advised him to go to bed early and be very temperate and cautious, and from late 1675 to 1677 he lived in France, mostly at Montpellier, then much visited by invalids. Lady Masham later wrote that although his travels in France had done his health some good, after his return he was still less able than before to stay long in London without retreating to a neighbouring village 'to recover the prejudice his health received from the smoke of that vast quantity of sea-coal which is burnt in this great city'. From then on he left London as often as he could, and always in the winter months. Among his medical papers, an account of consumption that Fox Bourne describes as drawn from Sydenham's observation and confirmed by Locke attributes the frequency of consumptions in London to 'a perpetual mist' mixed with the fumes of the city's trades, 'but especially the sulphur and fumes of sea coals, with which the air is repleted; and these being sucked into our lungs ... give an occasion to a cough'. He left England in the autumn of 1683, when he was under suspicion for his association with Shaftesbury, lived in Holland, and landed at Greenwich on 12 February 1688/9 (Julian). He soon declined a diplomatic mission on the ground of insufficient health, and wrote in April 1689 that he hoped to breathe more easily and be less troubled by his cough. He found the smoke of London unfavourable to his health, and from the spring of 1691 lived at Oates, in the parish of High Laver, Essex, the home of Sir Francis and Lady Masham (Damaris Cudworth). On 29 February 1691/2 (Julian; 10 March 1692, Gregorian) he wrote to Philipp van Limborch that since visiting Robert Boyle on his deathbed in December, 'my health has kept me in the country, for I found my lungs could not bear the smoke of the city'. In May 1692 he spent a day or two at Cambridge after Isaac Newton wrote to him on 3 May (Julian) that, now the churlish weather was almost over, he hoped to see him there. In November 1692 he wrote that after a single day in London he fell so ill that he could hardly breathe and had to come away; back at Oates on 28 November (Julian) he told Edward Clarke, 'well I cannot say I yet am, under so troublesome a cough as I have, but my lungs move easier than they did'. As a commissioner of the Board of Trade from 1696 to 1700 he suffered so much from his chest illness, which the Stanford Encyclopedia calls asthma, that he could bear the smoke of London only in the four warmer months; he attended its meetings in the summer and autumn and was confined to Oates the rest of the year. In October 1698 he wrote that 'a troublesome cough and great difficulty of breathing' were forcing him out of town. Summoned to London to see the king on 23 January 1697/8 (Julian), he wrote to Lord Somers that when he lay down in bed his breath failed him, so that he had to sit up most of the night and at three in the morning got up and sat by the fire till morning; he returned to Oates at once, and declined the employment offered because of his health. From 1700 he was often kept in bed by sore legs and wrote of 'my old evil of my breast'; by 1702 he had become so deaf for a time that he could hardly take part in conversation. In June 1704 he wrote that his shortness of breath and uneasiness increased every day, despite the season that usually restored him. That summer his legs swelled, more so towards the end of October. Lady Masham wrote that all the faculties of his mind were perfect to the last, and that he died of weakness alone. On 28 October 1704 (Julian; 8 November 1704, Gregorian), carried to his chair while she read the psalms to him, he died quietly at Oates, aged 72. He was buried in the churchyard at High Laver.
- **R:** Modern accounts describe him as asthmatic: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy states that he was asthmatic, and a 2006 history of medicine article that his health was blighted throughout life by asthma, which often incapacitated him for long periods. Fox Bourne judged that his cough was connected with disease in the lungs, and that probably only extreme care in treating himself had kept it from passing into a rapid consumption.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
