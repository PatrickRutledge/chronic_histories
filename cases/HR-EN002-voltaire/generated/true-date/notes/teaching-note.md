# Teaching Note — Voltaire pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Voltaire, the pen name of François-Marie Arouet, was born in Paris; his birth certificate gives 21 November 1694, although he stated on several occasions that he was in fact born on 20 February. He was born into a wealthy bourgeois family; his putative father, François Arouet, became a receiver in the Cour des Comptes (the audit office), and he lost his mother when he was seven. He adopted the name Voltaire in 1718. He was imprisoned in the Bastille for nearly a year in 1717 after mocking the regent, and in May 1726, after a quarrel with the chevalier de Rohan, was beaten, taken to the Bastille, and conducted to Calais, whence he left for London. In a letter to the baron de Breteuil dated December 1723, he gave an account of the smallpox from which he was recovering: he fell ill on 4 November at Maisons; the disease declared itself after two days of fever with a slight eruption; the physician Gervasi had him take an emetic eight times and drink two hundred pints of lemonade, he had himself bled a second time on his own authority, and by the 15th he was out of danger. His biographer Jean Orieux, as summarized in a 1970 review in the Revue d'histoire de la pharmacie, describes a lifelong preoccupation with illness and remedies: he suffered from colic, took as many as eight purges and twelve enemas in a month, was persuaded he would die before forty, and really did suffer from his kidneys and bladder. In a letter of 1769 he wrote that being very ill was the common lot of old age, especially for one who had always had a feeble constitution. He returned to Paris on 10 February 1778, for the first time in 28 years, to direct rehearsals of his tragedy Irène; more than 300 people called on him the day after his arrival. On 30 March he went to the Académie amid acclamations and was crowned in his box at a performance of Irène. His deteriorating health did not permit his return to Ferney. His secretary Jean-Louis Wagnière recorded that on 25 February 1778, at a quarter past noon, while dictating from his bed, he coughed three times and said that he was spitting blood, and that blood then spurted from his mouth and nose. A declaration in his hand of 2 March 1778 states that he had been attacked for four days by a vomiting of blood, and the blood loss continued for several days. In mid-May 1778, according to Wagnière's account, he was persuaded to take a liquor brought by an apothecary and then, in great agitation, asked the maréchal de Richelieu to send him some of his prepared opium; one witness said he saw him finish the bottle. The anonymous manuscript says he took the whole vial at once instead of dividing it into three or four doses. Orieux's account has him refuse the apothecary's remedy and accept the opium preparation brought by Richelieu, after which he felt a trail of fire from throat to bowels and became frenzied. In his last weeks he suffered from strangury and needed frequent catheterization by the surgeon Try; the anonymous manuscript describes a bladder holding as much pus as urine, which could be emptied only with a catheter, and a burning heat over the lower abdomen. Britannica records that on 18 May he was stricken with uremia and that he suffered much pain on his deathbed. He died in Paris on 30 May 1778, aged 83, peacefully it seems, according to Britannica; his body was taken to the Abbey of Scellières and given Christian burial there, and his remains were later transferred to the Panthéon. The report of the opening and embalming of his body, dated 31 May 1778 and signed by the surgeon Try, found the brain and the organs of the chest healthy; the bladder was diseased, more than an inch thick at its upper and posterior part, with a lard-like substance and suppurating tubercles whose pus had leaked into the abdomen over the nearby intestines; the right kidney bore gangrenous marks; and the prostate was very large and entirely scirrhous. The anonymous manuscript adds that the bladder held about a pint of pus and was perforated at its base.
- **R:** An anonymous and hostile manuscript account of 1778, published by Frédéric Lachèvre in 1908, claims that in May 1778, before a meeting at the Académie, he secretly took as many as eighteen cups of coffee. Retrospective readings of his final illness differ. A 2025 paleoproteomic study of blood residues by Charlier and colleagues concluded that he died of a perforated squamous cell cancer of the bladder with peritoneal extension; Britannica describes uremia; the autopsy's very large, hard prostate leaves prostate disease, benign or malignant, as a further possibility.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
