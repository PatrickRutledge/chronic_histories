Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Voltaire, the pen name of François-Marie Arouet, was born in Paris; his birth certificate gives 21 November 1694, although he stated on several occasions that he was in fact born on 20 February. He was born into a wealthy bourgeois family; his putative father, François Arouet, became a receiver in the Cour des Comptes (the audit office), and he lost his mother when he was seven. He adopted the name Voltaire in 1718. [D]
  • He was imprisoned in the Bastille for nearly a year in 1717 after mocking the regent, and in May 1726, after a quarrel with the chevalier de Rohan, was beaten, taken to the Bastille, and conducted to Calais, whence he left for London. [D]
  • In a letter to the baron de Breteuil dated December 1723, he gave an account of the smallpox from which he was recovering: he fell ill on 4 November at Maisons; the disease declared itself after two days of fever with a slight eruption; the physician Gervasi had him take an emetic eight times and drink two hundred pints of lemonade, he had himself bled a second time on his own authority, and by the 15th he was out of danger. [D]
  • His biographer Jean Orieux, as summarized in a 1970 review in the Revue d'histoire de la pharmacie, describes a lifelong preoccupation with illness and remedies: he suffered from colic, took as many as eight purges and twelve enemas in a month, was persuaded he would die before forty, and really did suffer from his kidneys and bladder. In a letter of 1769 he wrote that being very ill was the common lot of old age, especially for one who had always had a feeble constitution. [D]
  • He returned to Paris on 10 February 1778, for the first time in 28 years, to direct rehearsals of his tragedy Irène; more than 300 people called on him the day after his arrival. On 30 March he went to the Académie amid acclamations and was crowned in his box at a performance of Irène. His deteriorating health did not permit his return to Ferney. [D]
  • His secretary Jean-Louis Wagnière recorded that on 25 February 1778, at a quarter past noon, while dictating from his bed, he coughed three times and said that he was spitting blood, and that blood then spurted from his mouth and nose. A declaration in his hand of 2 March 1778 states that he had been attacked for four days by a vomiting of blood, and the blood loss continued for several days. [D]
