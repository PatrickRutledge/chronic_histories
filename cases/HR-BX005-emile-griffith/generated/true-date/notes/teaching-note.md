# Teaching Note — Emile Griffith pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Emile Alphonse Griffith was born on February 3, 1938, in St. Thomas, U.S. Virgin Islands, one of eight children. He moved to New York City as a young man and turned professional in 1958. He boxed professionally for nineteen years, from 1958 to 1977, in 112 bouts (a record of 85 wins, 24 losses and 2 draws), won five world titles at welterweight and middleweight, was the first boxer from the U.S. Virgin Islands to become a world champion, and retired at 39, after losing his last three fights; he entered the International Boxing Hall of Fame in 1990. On March 24, 1962, at Madison Square Garden, he regained the welterweight title by stopping Benny Paret in the twelfth round; Paret never regained consciousness, had emergency brain surgery, and died ten days later. Sports Illustrated reported in 2005 that an anti-gay slur Paret directed at him at the weigh-in may have fueled him. The fight's outcome stayed with him for decades. His biographer said he saw visions of Paret at night and in a mirror and refused to box again for months; Griffith said he was "never the same fighter after that"; and in a documentary released in 2005 he told Paret's son, "Sometimes I still have nightmares." Over the years he described himself at various times as straight, gay and bisexual; in a 2005 interview with Sports Illustrated he said, "I don't know what I am." His biographer said he lived openly, going to gay bars, but did not say so publicly until after the 2005 documentary about him. In 1992 he was attacked and badly beaten by a group of men, one with a baseball bat, after leaving a gay bar in midtown Manhattan near Times Square; he spent several months in hospital, much of it in intensive care. The motive was never established. A 2005 review of the documentary reported that he had had short-term memory problems since the 1992 attack. After boxing he trained other boxers, including world champions, and worked as a corrections officer and a bartender. Late in life he had pugilistic dementia and needed full-time care; he lived in a nursing home in Hempstead, New York. He died on July 23, 2013, at 75, at an extended care facility in Hempstead, New York; his biographer said kidney failure and complications of dementia caused his death.
- **R:** The project roster describes post-traumatic stress disorder from the ring death. No source read records that diagnosis; the documented nightmares, visions and lasting distress are consistent with it only as a retrospective interpretation.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
