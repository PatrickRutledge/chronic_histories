# Teaching Note — Sun Yat-sen pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Sun Yat-sen was born on November 12, 1866, into an ordinary farming family in Cuiheng Village. He was called Dixiang in childhood and also had the names Mingwen, Deming and Rixin; the name Yat-sen came into use while he was in Japan. He went to a private school at nine, studied in Honolulu from the age of thirteen, and returned home at seventeen. He began studying Western medicine at the Boji Hospital in Guangzhou in 1886 and in 1887 transferred to the College of Medicine for Chinese in Hong Kong, as one of its twelve students; his teachers included Patrick Manson. He was one of two students who passed the graduation examination in 1892 and received his diploma at the college's first graduation ceremony on July 23, 1892. After graduating he practiced medicine in Macau, first at the Kiang Wu Hospital and then in a dispensary of his own, and then in Guangzhou, opening pharmacies that offered Chinese and Western medicines; about two years after graduating he gave up medicine for politics. In 1894 he founded the China Revival Society in Honolulu; in 1905 he founded the Chinese Revolutionary League in Tokyo; on New Year's Day 1912 he became Provisional President of the Republic of China in Nanjing; and in January 1924 he presided over the First National Congress of the Kuomintang in Guangzhou. In the autumn of 1924, after the Beijing Coup, he was invited to Beijing to discuss state affairs, and he arrived in Beijing in poor health on the last day of 1924. Several months before his operation in January 1925 a mass had been noted in his right upper abdomen and was thought to be his liver; it grew larger shortly before surgery. At the preoperative examination he had no jaundice and not much loss of weight. He was admitted to the Peking Union Medical College Hospital on January 26, 1925, too ill to eat, and had an exploratory laparotomy that day by Adrian S. Taylor, the head of surgery, which found extensive involvement of the liver by carcinoma. He was treated with radium for the rest of his stay, left the hospital against advice on February 18, 1925, and was then treated by old-style Chinese doctors while his condition declined. He died in Beijing on March 12, 1925.
- **R:** His fatal illness has long been described as liver cancer, the diagnosis taken from the findings at the laparotomy; the museum at his birthplace states that he died of liver cancer. A limited autopsy at the Peking Union Medical College Hospital on March 13, 1925, by James R. Cash, reported adenocarcinoma of the gallbladder with obstruction of the cystic duct, direct extension to the liver and diaphragm, and metastases to the lungs, peritoneum, ileum and colon, with healed cholecystitis, gallstones, generalized arteriosclerosis, healed calcified tuberculosis of the lungs, emphysema, ascites and roundworm infestation. A 2016 review concludes that gallbladder adenocarcinoma, not a primary liver cancer, caused his death.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
