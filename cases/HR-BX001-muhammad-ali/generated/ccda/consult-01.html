History of Present Illness

Mr. Ali, a 54-year-old man with parkinsonism diagnosed twelve years ago at 42, is seen at the request of Dr. Moreau. Slowness of movement is his main limitation: buttoning, handwriting, and rising from low chairs take longer, most of all in the late morning and late afternoon. He has a mild right-hand tremor at rest. His voice is soft, and people have trouble hearing him in crowds. He takes carbidopa-levodopa 25/100 mg one tablet three times a day, often with meals, and notices no wearing-off before doses, no involuntary movements, no freezing, and no lightheadedness on standing. He has had no falls, hallucinations, dream enactment, or new urges to gamble, shop, or eat. He boxed professionally until three years before the diagnosis. [S]

Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Muhammad Ali was born Cassius Clay in Louisville, Kentucky, on January 17, 1942. [D]
  • In 1984, at age 42 and three years after he retired from boxing, Ali was diagnosed with a parkinsonian condition by the neurologist Stanley Fahn in New York; it has been publicly described as Parkinson's disease since. [D]
  • The diagnosing neurologist later said publicly that slowness of movement was Ali's main limitation at the time of diagnosis, with a masked face, decreased blinking, a typical parkinsonian tremor, and a shuffling gait, and that some features were too early for classic Parkinson's disease. [D]
  • In a 1996 television interview, Ali's physician Dennis Cope described his condition as Parkinson's syndrome attributed to pugilistic brain syndrome from boxing, and said testing showed his cognitive function had not declined: "His mind is fine." [D]
  • Whether boxing caused Ali's Parkinson's disease is disputed. The diagnosing neurologist thought the early symptoms were probably caused by repeated blows to the head but could not be certain; his later physician Abraham Lieberman did not think boxing contributed, though he was not a hundred percent certain; and the director of the Muhammad Ali Parkinson Center said there was no medical evidence that boxing contributed. Assessment

  • Parkinson's disease, young onset, Hoehn and Yahr stage 2.5 (G20.A1). Clinically established Parkinson's disease by the MDS criteria: bradykinesia with rest tremor and rigidity, a clear and lasting response to levodopa, and no red flags or absolute exclusions. There is no early falling, no vertical gaze palsy, no cerebellar or pyramidal sign, no early autonomic failure, and no early dementia. Whether repeated blows to the head in his boxing career contributed cannot be confirmed or excluded during life. The course and examination are those of Parkinson's disease, and the question does not change his treatment. [S]
  • Bradykinesia on a modest levodopa dose. Bradykinesia limits daily tasks on levodopa 100 mg three times daily, with no motor complications. Taking doses with meals blunts absorption. Raising levodopa is the simplest and best-tolerated step. A dopamine agonist would add risks of sleepiness, impulse-control disorders, and hallucinations, and an MAO-B inhibitor would add only a small benefit. [S]
  • Hypokinetic dysarthria with soft speech (R47.1). Soft, monotone speech typical of Parkinson's disease, which responds well to intensive voice treatment. [S]
  • Gait, balance, and fall risk. No falls, but short steps and a three-step pull-test recovery show early postural instability. Supervised exercise and balance training lower fall risk and improve gait. [S]
  • Genetic testing. Genetic testing with counseling is now offered to everyone with Parkinson's disease, and young onset makes it more informative. The result would not change his current treatment. [S]
