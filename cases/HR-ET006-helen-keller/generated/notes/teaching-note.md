# Teaching Note — Helen Keller pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Helen Keller was born on June 27, 1880, in Tuscumbia, Alabama. In February 1882, at 19 months, she had a febrile illness that her doctor, as she later wrote, called acute congestion of the stomach and brain and thought she would not survive; the fever left suddenly, but she never saw or heard again. From March 1887 her teacher, a graduate of the Perkins School for the Blind, taught her language by spelling words into her hand; she learned the manual alphabet and raised print for blind readers, and began to learn speech at 10. In her own account, others spoke to her by spelling into her hand with the single-hand manual alphabet while she rested her hand lightly on theirs; she spoke for herself, read the lips of some people by touch, and read and wrote braille. In 1904 she graduated cum laude from Radcliffe College, the first deafblind person to earn a college degree. From 1924 she worked as a public spokesperson for the American Foundation for the Blind for the rest of her life, and from 1946 she made world tours to 35 countries in 11 years on behalf of people with disabilities. A receipt dated September 1911 records the purchase of an artificial eye for her. She lived at Arcan Ridge in Easton, Connecticut, from the late 1930s for the rest of her life. Her father died in 1896 and her mother in 1921. She had a stroke and retired from public appearances and work; Perkins dates this to 1961 and the American Foundation for the Blind to 1960. She died in her sleep at Arcan Ridge on June 1, 1968, a few weeks before her 88th birthday.
- **R:** The cause of the 1882 illness is unknown; scarlet fever, meningitis, and rubella have been proposed.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
