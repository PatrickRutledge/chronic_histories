# Teaching Note — Yul Brynner pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Yul Brynner was born Yuliy Borisovich Bryner on July 11, 1920, in Vladivostok, Russia. He gave varying accounts of his origins, saying at times that he was born on Sakhalin Island, with the year given as 1915 (UPI gives July 11, 1915; another reference gives July 12, 1915, under the name Taidje Kahn). He first played the King of Siam in The King and I on Broadway in 1951, won the Academy Award for best actor in the 1956 film, and played the role 4,625 times over 34 years. He smoked heavily for decades: UPI reported that at one time he smoked five packs of cigarettes a day, and Cancer Today reports two to four packs a day from the age of 12 and that he quit in 1971. In September 1983 he was found to have lung cancer; Cancer Today reports that he had found a lump on his vocal cords and that the tumor was too close to his heart for surgery, and UPI reports that he was treated for lung cancer that month and said he would beat the disease and resume touring. He returned to the stage and kept playing the King for about two more years; his last performance came in New York in the summer of 1985, less than four months before his death. He said in an interview that he believed his cancer was directly caused by smoking, calling smoking "suicide." After his death the American Cancer Society released a 30-second television message taken from a January 1985 Good Morning America interview, in which he says: "Now that I'm gone, I tell you: Don't smoke, whatever you do. Just don't smoke." He died on October 10, 1985, at 65, at New York Hospital-Cornell Medical Center, of complications of cancer.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
