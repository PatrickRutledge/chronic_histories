## Assessment

1. **Post-polio syndrome** (G14). She meets the modified March of Dimes criteria. Prior paralytic polio is confirmed by history, examination, and EMG, which shows chronic reinnervation in the right leg and, more mildly, in the left vastus lateralis and right deltoid, so polio affected more muscles than she knew. Partial recovery was followed by decades of stable function, and the new weakness and fatigue have lasted more than a year. Normal nerve conduction excludes neuropathy and entrapment, the EMG shows no myopathy and no decrement on repetitive stimulation, and the blood tests exclude thyroid disease, anemia, diabetes, B12 deficiency, and inflammation; the mildly raised creatine kinase fits overuse. Depression is mild and secondary. No drug has a proven effect on post-polio syndrome, so pyridostigmine, amantadine, and corticosteroids are not recommended. **S**
2. **Exercise**. Supervised, individualized training at submaximal effort improves strength, fatigue, and pain without harming motor units if it does not cause exhaustion or aching the next day. Warm-water exercise suits her cold intolerance. **S**
3. **Orthosis and falls**. Both falls come from right foot drop, and the knee hyperextension strains the knee. A custom lightweight carbon-fiber ankle-foot orthosis will clear the foot and control the knee, and is light enough to wear on field days; a cane in the left hand unloads the right leg. A knee-ankle-foot orthosis is not needed now because the knee does not buckle. **S**
4. **Respiratory and sleep**. No breathlessness or bulbar symptoms, a normal cough, and normal spirometry today make respiratory muscle weakness unlikely. Unrefreshing sleep should be screened with home overnight oximetry; sleep apnea and nocturnal hypoventilation are more common in polio survivors. **S**
5. **Sedation and anesthesia**. Polio survivors can be sensitive to sedatives, opioids, and neuromuscular blocking drugs and may have unrecognized respiratory weakness, cold intolerance, and positioning problems. For an endoscopy, moderate sedation at reduced doses with oxygen and carbon dioxide monitoring is appropriate. **S**
6. **Pacing and outlook**. Post-polio syndrome usually progresses slowly, over years, and pacing, the right exercise, and an orthosis often stabilize function or improve it. She can keep working in the field with planned rest and less to carry. **S**

## Recommendations

1. Twelve weeks of supervised physical therapy, twice a week: submaximal strengthening with rest days, aerobic and warm-water exercise, gait and balance training, and energy conservation; then a home program. Stop any exercise that leaves her exhausted or aching the next day.
2. Custom lightweight carbon-fiber right ankle-foot orthosis, fitted within four weeks, and a single-point cane in the left hand.
3. Pacing for field days: a lighter camera on a harness, a seat stick, an assistant to carry the tripod, rest breaks every hour, and a rest day after each field day.
4. Baseline spirometry today, then yearly or with symptoms; home overnight oximetry for unrefreshing sleep.
5. Record sedation and anesthesia precautions in the chart: reduced sedative and opioid doses, continuous oxygen and carbon dioxide monitoring, warming, careful positioning of the right leg, and caution with neuromuscular blocking drugs.
6. No disease-specific drug; acetaminophen, heat, and pacing for pain; no NSAIDs after her ulcer surgery.
7. Bone density of both hips and the spine, since disuse of the right leg and falls raise fracture risk; the scan done today by primary care shows osteoporosis of the right hip, which primary care will treat.
8. Review in this clinic at six months, or sooner for new weakness, breathlessness, or swallowing trouble.
