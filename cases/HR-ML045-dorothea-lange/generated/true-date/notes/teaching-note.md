# Teaching Note — Dorothea Lange pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Dorothea Lange was born Dorothea Margaretta Nutzhorn in Hoboken, New Jersey, on May 26, 1895. At the age of seven, in 1902, Lange had polio, which left her with a weakened right leg and a lifelong limp. In her oral history of 1960 to 1961 Lange said: "I was physically disabled, and no one who hasn't lived the life of a semi-cripple knows how much that means. I think it perhaps was the most important thing that happened to me, and formed me, guided me, instructed me, helped me, and humiliated me." In the same interview Lange said that her lameness as a child, and her acceptance of it, "truly opened gates for me", and that working among strangers as a disabled person gave "an immense advantage. People are kinder." Lange was a documentary photographer. In 1935 she was hired as a photographer for the Resettlement Administration, later the Farm Security Administration, and in March 1936 she made the photograph known as "Migrant Mother" in Nipomo, California. From the mid-1930s she lived in Berkeley, California. In the late 1940s ill health stopped Lange working: she had a succession of severe stomach ulcers that were controlled only by radical surgery, and pain and discomfort troubled her from then on. In the early 1950s Lange returned to work with photographic essays for Life and Fortune and helped to found the photography magazine Aperture; her poor health limited her to short bursts of work. From 1958 she traveled widely abroad with her husband, in Asia, the Middle East, and South America, although poor health and unfamiliar languages made the work hard. A review of her standard biography says that bleeding ulcers and esophageal problems troubled Lange as she grew older, so that she often had difficulty eating. In 1964 Lange was diagnosed with inoperable cancer of the esophagus. Knowing she was dying, Lange spent her last months preparing a retrospective of her life's work at the Museum of Modern Art in New York, which opened in January 1966. Lange died of cancer in San Francisco on October 11, 1965, at 70. After her death her personal archive, more than 40,000 negatives and 6,000 prints, was given to the Oakland Museum of California.
- **R:** The same review says that Lange developed post-polio symptoms in late middle age and that post-polio fatigue tired her.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
