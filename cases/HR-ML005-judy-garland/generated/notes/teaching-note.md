# Teaching Note — Judy Garland pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Judy Garland was born Frances Ethel Gumm on June 10, 1922, in Grand Rapids, Minnesota, to parents in vaudeville, and first appeared on stage as a small child. She was signed by Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer at 13 and became a national star at 17 in The Wizard of Oz. As a teenage studio performer she was given stimulant "pep pills" (amphetamines; Benzedrine is named) to suppress her appetite and keep up her energy through long working days, and sleeping pills (barbiturates; Seconal is named) so that she could sleep. She later described being given pills to stay on her feet and then being put to sleep with sleeping pills at the studio hospital. She struggled with dependence on prescribed medicines, stimulants and sleeping pills, for the rest of her life; by her twenties she was already dependent on pills. Suicide attempts were reported publicly, including one in 1950; a biographical encyclopedia describes others in the 1940s and early 1950s. In her twenties she was treated for what was publicly called exhaustion and spent periods in hospitals and sanitariums, including a stay at Peter Bent Brigham Hospital in Boston in 1949. Her contract with Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer ended in 1950, and she returned to the stage in concert. She starred in A Star Is Born, released in 1954, and was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Actress. She gave birth to her third child, a son, in March 1955. On September 24, 1955, she made her television debut on the first Ford Star Jubilee broadcast. In 1959 she was hospitalized with acute hepatitis and liver disease. She died in London on June 22, 1969, at 47; the coroner ruled her death an accidental overdose of barbiturates.
- **R:** Accounts differ on who first gave her the pills. A biography by Gerald Clarke reports that her mother gave her pills for energy and sleep in early childhood, before she joined the studio; other accounts attribute the pills to the studio and its doctors.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
