Assessment

  • Sedative, hypnotic, or anxiolytic use disorder (barbiturate), moderate (F13.20). Tolerance and withdrawal are not counted for a medicine taken under medical supervision. She still meets five criteria: taking more than intended, unsuccessful efforts to cut down, craving, a great deal of time spent getting prescriptions from several prescribers, and continued use despite knowing that it causes morning grogginess and worsens her low mood. That is a moderate use disorder, with physiological dependence. It began with pills given to her as a minor, and that should be said plainly to her: this is harm done to her, not a failing of hers. [S]
  • Barbiturate withdrawal risk (F13.20). Daily short-acting barbiturate at 200 to 300 mg for years carries a real risk of seizures and delirium if it is stopped or cut quickly. The 1954 joint benzodiazepine tapering guideline does not cover barbiturates, but its principles apply: no abrupt stop, a long-acting agent, slow reductions she agrees to, pauses when needed, and inpatient care when severe withdrawal is likely. Phenobarbital, at about 30 mg for each 100 mg of secobarbital, is the usual long-acting substitute and should be started under supervision by clinicians experienced in its use. [S]
  • Stimulant use disorder (prescribed amphetamine), mild (F15.10). Two criteria: extra doses beyond the prescription and unsuccessful efforts to cut down. No medicine is approved for stimulant use disorder, and there is no medical indication to continue it. Stopping can bring tiredness, sleepiness and low mood for a few weeks, so it should not be stopped during the barbiturate change; a short taper once phenobarbital is stable is reasonable. [S]
  • Depression with anxious distress (F32.1). Sertraline is a good choice; phenobarbital may lower its level a little, so the dose should follow her response. Psychotherapy and CBT for insomnia support the taper. Avoid benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, trazodone and sedating antihistamines for sleep, which would swap one sedative for another. [S]
  • Naloxone and monitoring. Naloxone reverses opioids, not barbiturates. She takes no opioid and has none in the monitoring program, and her pills all come from pharmacies, so naloxone is not needed now; it should be offered at once if an opioid is ever prescribed. Overdose risk from the barbiturate is lowered by limited supplies, locked storage, no alcohol, and the safety plan. [S]
