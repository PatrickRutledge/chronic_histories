# Teaching Note — Wilma Mankiller pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Wilma Pearl Mankiller was born on November 18, 1945, in Tahlequah, Oklahoma, the capital of the Cherokee Nation, the sixth of eleven children; her father was a full-blood Cherokee. A Cherokee Nation release gave her birthplace as the W.W. Hastings Indian Hospital in Tahlequah; the Oklahoma Hall of Fame says she was born at Rock Mountain in Adair County. As a child she moved with her family from Mankiller Flats in Adair County to San Francisco under the Bureau of Indian Affairs relocation program (1956 or 1957 by different accounts); she later described the move as her own little Trail of Tears. The occupation of Alcatraz Island in 1969 and her work with American Indian communities in California in the 1970s set the course of her activism. She returned to eastern Oklahoma with her two daughters in the later 1970s (1976 or 1977 by different accounts), worked for the Cherokee Nation, and lived on her grandfather's allotment, Mankiller Flats, in Adair County. In 1979 she was nearly killed in a head-on car crash on a curve, in which the driver of the other car, one of her best friends, died. She needed about 17 operations; accounts say her leg was barely saved from amputation and that she was told she might never walk again. The Oklahoma Hall of Fame dates the crash to 1978 and describes shattered legs, broken ribs and a crushed face. Her recovery was made harder by the emotional trauma of her friend's death in the crash. Less than a year after the crash, while still recovering, she was diagnosed with myasthenia gravis (dated 1980 in one reference account). With help from Cherokee medicine people, surgery and drug therapy, she recovered and returned to work with the Cherokee Nation in 1981; she later became a spokesperson for the Myasthenia Gravis Foundation. She became the first woman deputy chief of the Cherokee Nation in August 1983 and principal chief in December 1985; she was elected in 1987, re-elected in 1991, and served until 1995, the first woman to lead the Cherokee Nation. Hereditary polycystic kidney disease, which she called her old nemesis, led to a kidney transplant in 1990, with a kidney donated by her brother; in 1991, a year after repeated hospital stays and the transplant, she won re-election with 82 percent of the vote. The transplanted kidney was damaged by her treatment for lymphoma in 1996, and she had a second kidney transplant in 1998. Another account says ill health, including a second kidney operation and treatment for lymphatic cancer, led her to decline a third term in 1995. She was treated for breast cancer in 1999. Her autobiography, Mankiller: A Chief and Her People, written with Michael Wallis, was published in 1993; she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1998. In the autobiography she wrote that she wanted to be remembered for emphasizing that 'we have indigenous solutions to our problems.' On March 2, 2010, a news release announced that she had stage 4 metastatic pancreatic cancer; in it she wrote that she was mentally and spiritually prepared for the journey. She died on the morning of April 6, 2010, at her home in rural Adair County, Oklahoma, aged 64, of pancreatic cancer.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
