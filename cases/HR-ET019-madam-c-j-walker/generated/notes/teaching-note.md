# Teaching Note — Madam C. J. Walker pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Madam C. J. Walker was born Sarah Breedlove on December 23, 1867, in Delta, Louisiana, the daughter of Owen and Minerva Anderson Breedlove. Both parents had been enslaved on a Madison Parish plantation, and she was their first child born into freedom. Both her parents had died by the time she was seven, and she went to live with an older sister and moved across the river to Vicksburg, Mississippi. She married Moses McWilliams at fourteen, had her only child, Lelia (later A'Lelia), and was widowed at twenty. She then moved to St. Louis, where she worked as a laundress and cook, sometimes for as little as a dollar a day. While working as a laundress she began to lose her hair, a problem many Black women of her time shared. Her biographers attribute it to scalp disease such as dandruff, poor diet, a hard life and harsh washing products, in an era when most homes lacked indoor plumbing. In 1904 she began using and selling another Black woman's hair grower as a sales agent, then developed her own sulfur-containing ointments and shampoos to treat scalp disease. In Denver she married Charles Joseph Walker, a newspaper advertising salesman (in January 1906, or 1905 in one account), and began calling herself Madam Walker, in part to avoid the familiar forms of address then used for Black women. She launched her own hair-care line there, set up headquarters and a training school for her agents in Pittsburgh in 1908, and moved the company to Indianapolis in 1910. She trained thousands of sales agents, "hair culturists," who earned a share of the profits. She kept the name after the marriage ended. As her wealth grew she became a philanthropist and activist: she gave to the YWCA and paid tuition for students at Tuskegee Institute, and in 1915 she sued over discrimination at an Indianapolis theater. In 1917 she joined the committee of the Negro Silent Protest Parade in petitioning the President and Congress to make lynching a national crime. She gave $5,000 to the NAACP's anti-lynching work, and by her own report her company had more than twenty thousand agents by 1919. The physical demands she placed on herself undermined her health, and she suffered from high blood pressure. She went for a rest cure at Hot Springs, Arkansas, in 1916, but otherwise did not slow her pace. By mid-November 1917 a Chicago physician, Dr. George Sauer, diagnosed nephritis, an often irreversible inflammation of the kidneys made worse by her worsening hypertension, and advised an indefinite stay at the Battle Creek Sanitarium in Michigan, where she took a rest cure that year. She moved into Villa Lewaro, her house at Irvington-on-Hudson, New York (in 1918; 1917 in one account). In April 1919 she became gravely ill in St. Louis and returned to New York. She died on May 25, 1919, at Villa Lewaro, at 51. Accounts of the cause differ: chronic interstitial nephritis; kidney failure; or high blood pressure and kidney failure.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
