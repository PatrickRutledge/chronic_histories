# Teaching Note — Benjamin Franklin pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Benjamin Franklin was born in Boston on January 17, 1706 (New Style); an editor's note to his autobiography gives the date as Sunday, January 6, old style, 1706, in a house on Milk Street. In a letter of July 31, 1786, Franklin recalled that in 1724, as a compositor at Palmer's printing house in London, he sometimes heated his case of type before the fire to warm his hands, until an old workman warned him he might lose the use of his hands, and that he had felt a kind of obscure pain in the bones of his hand when working over very hot type. A 2008 review of his medical history by Finger and Hagemann concluded that he suffered repeated attacks of gout and a large bladder stone, which caused considerable pain and markedly decreased his mobility. Franklin wrote the Dialogue between Franklin and the Gout, headed Midnight, October 22, 1780, in which the Gout tells him he has eaten and drunk too freely and indulged his indolence; a 2023 rheumatology review states that he woke with a gout attack that night. In a letter from Passy dated March 12, 1781, to Samuel Huntington, President of Congress, Franklin wrote that he had passed his seventy-fifth year and that the long and severe fit of the gout he had the last winter had shaken him exceedingly, leaving him far from his former bodily strength. The 1806 edition of his works states that he had been little subject to disease, except an attack of the gout occasionally, until about the year 1781, when he was first attacked with symptoms of the calculous complaint, which continued during his life. In a letter from Passy dated May 23, 1785, to George Wheatley, Franklin agreed that the gout is bad and the stone is worse, and wrote that at fourscore he found himself subject to the gout and the stone. Writing from Philadelphia on April 15, 1787, he said he had never had the least doubt that his malady was the stone, that it had increased but gave him no more pain than at Passy, and that he thought himself favored to have only the stone and gout; in a letter of October 24, 1788, he wrote that he was recovering from a long-continued gout. In his autobiography Franklin wrote that at the beginning of February 1726/7 he was taken ill with a pleurisy that very nearly carried him off. The 1806 edition of his works adds that in 1735 he had a severe pleurisy that ended in an abscess of the left lobe of his lungs, and that a second similar attack some years later left no lasting effect on his breathing. His autobiography records that he never knew his father or mother to have any sickness but that of which they died, his father Josiah at 89 in 1744 and his mother Abiah at 85 in 1752. Franklin wrote that in 1736 he lost a son, a boy of four, to smallpox taken in the common way, and regretted that he had not had him inoculated. His physician's account of his last illness states that the stone had for the last twelve months confined him chiefly to his bed, and that during the most painful paroxysms he took large doses of laudanum. About sixteen days before his death he developed a fever, then pain in the left side of the chest with cough and labored breathing; an abscess in his lungs burst, and he died in Philadelphia at about eleven at night on April 17, 1790, aged 84.
- **R:** The same review argues that several factors that raise uric acid converged in him, including sex, genetics, diet, drinking, advanced age, psoriasis, and possible lead poisoning from lead exposure. A 2023 review describes him as overweight and as probably having had consequences of the metabolic syndrome, including gout.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
