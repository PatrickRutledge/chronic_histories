# Teaching Note — Michelangelo Buonarroti pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Michelangelo was born at Caprese on March 6, 1475, by the modern reckoning; his father's notebook records the birth as March 6, 1474, in the Florentine style, on a Monday morning before daybreak. He died in Rome a little before five o'clock in the afternoon of February 18, 1564, within a few weeks of his 89th birthday, attended to the end by two physicians. A friend's letter of February 14, 1564, reported finding him out on foot in the rain, saying he was ill and could not find rest anywhere, with uncertain speech; he spent his last four days partly in an armchair and partly in bed. In 1548 he wrote to his nephew that he had lately been in great difficulty from dysuria but was better. In a letter to his nephew dated March 15, 1549, he wrote that he had been very ill with dysuria, groaning in pain night and day without sleep, that his doctors thought he was suffering from calculus, and that he had been advised to try the baths at Viterbo. On March 23, 1549, he wrote that after drinking a water sent to him he passed a large quantity of thick white matter with portions of the shell of the stone, and was much better. On April 5, 1549, he wrote that it was now quite certain that he had urinary calculus, not very advanced, and that it was gradually diminishing with the water he was drinking. In a letter the editor dates to June 8, 1549, he wrote that for about two months he had drunk, morning and evening, a water from a spring about forty miles from Rome that breaks up the stone, that it had broken his and let him pass water with tolerable ease, and that he would have to use nothing else for drinking or cooking and alter his mode of life. On June 16, 1557, he wrote to his nephew that he suffered from all the ailments of old age: the stone, so that he could not pass water, and pains in his side and back, so that he often could not go up or down stairs. A postscript to a letter of 1559 (dated June 15; the editor notes the nephew altered it to July 15) reads: writing is very troublesome to my hand, my eyes and my memory; such is the effect of age. Lazzeri and colleagues report that he was seen hammering up to six days before his death, and suggest that his continued, intense work helped him keep the use of his hands. His father, Lodovico, was born on June 11, 1444, and died in 1533 or 1534; Michelangelo's poem on his death says he was ninety. He had two acute illnesses, the first in the summer of 1544 and the second toward the close of 1545, when news reached Florence that he was dying; he recovered from both.
- **R:** Lazzeri and colleagues (2016), studying portraits, proposed that degenerative, non-inflammatory osteoarthritis affected the base of his left thumb, the thumb's metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints, and the index finger's metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints; earlier portraits show no deformity, and they place the onset around 1547 to 1553, with worsening in his last fifteen years. The same authors report that letters to his nephew speak of gout, a period term for many arthritic conditions, and of a cruel pain in one foot; they judged gouty arthritis of the hands unlikely because the portraits show no inflammation or tophi.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
