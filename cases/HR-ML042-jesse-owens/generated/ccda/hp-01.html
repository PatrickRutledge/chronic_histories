Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • James Cleveland (J.C.) Owens was born on September 12, 1913, in Oakville, Alabama, the youngest of the children of Henry and Mary Emma Owens, who were sharecroppers. The Ohio State University Libraries give ten children; contemporary newspaper accounts and his own account give nine. [D]
  • As a child in Alabama he worked in the fields with his family. At about 7 he had pneumonia and nearly died; a brother and a sister died during the same epidemic. He later described himself as a sickly, emaciated, poverty-stricken child, and said that his parents knew the family had to leave to survive. In the early 1920s the family moved north to Cleveland, following an older sister who had written home about jobs there. [D]
  • In Cleveland a teacher misheard his initials J.C. and called him Jesse, and the name stuck. Coached by Charles Riley from junior high school, he won the long jump, set a world record in the 220-yard dash and tied the world record in the 100-yard dash at the National Interscholastic Championship in 1933. [D]
  • At the Ohio State University, which he entered in 1933, he was barred from the men's dormitory because of his race, lived in a boarding house with other African American students, and could not eat in the restaurants along High Street. He worked as a freight elevator operator. At the Big Ten championships in May 1935 he set world records in the 220-yard dash, the 220-yard low hurdles and the long jump. [D]
  • At the 1936 Olympic Games in Berlin he won four gold medals, in the 100 meters, the 200 meters, the long jump and the 4 x 100-meter relay, and he was given a ticker-tape parade in New York on his return. [D]
  • The offers that followed the Games did not become steady work. He earned money racing against horses and local sprinters, and said that Black athletes were not allowed in any professional sport then. A dry-cleaning business he founded in 1938 went into debt, and he filed for personal bankruptcy in 1939. He owed taxes on his income after the Games, and decades later was in trouble with the Internal Revenue Service again for failing to file returns. He later worked through public relations firms, as a speaker, and with youth organizations. [D]
  • He met Minnie Ruth Solomon at Fairmount Junior High School in Cleveland in 1930; they married and had three daughters. In the early 1970s, with their children grown, they moved from Chicago, their home of more than 20 years, to the Phoenix area of Arizona. Ruth Owens died in 2001. [D]
  • The Ohio State University awarded him an honorary doctorate of athletic arts, and in 1976 he received the Presidential Medal of Freedom. He served on the board of directors of the U.S. Olympic Committee from 1973. [D]
  • He smoked cigarettes for decades: at his death newspapers described him as a pack-a-day smoker for the past 35 years, and his doctors said he had smoked about a pack a day. The Ohio State University Libraries call him a lifetime smoker. [D]
