# Teaching Note — James Madison pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** James Madison was born at Port Conway, Virginia, on 16 March 1751 in the Gregorian calendar (5 March, Old Style), the first of twelve children of James Madison and Nelly Conway Madison; his mother was visiting her own mother's home there. A family sketch he sent in 1834 gives the date as '5th of March, 1751 (O. S.)'. The Madison family's wealth lay in land, enslaved people, and tobacco. In the early 1760s the plantation's enslaved labor force built the brick house half a mile from the first family home, the estate later named Montpelier, in Orange County, Virginia. The Library of Congress describes him as an excellent student but a frail and sickly child. His health had always been delicate, and Williamsburg was regarded as unhealthy, which was among the reasons he went north to the College of New Jersey (Princeton) rather than to William and Mary. He entered the College of New Jersey in 1769, graduated in 1771, and stayed on several months to study Hebrew and other subjects under John Witherspoon. His biographer Gaillard Hunt wrote that for several months he spared only three hours in twenty-four for sleep, until he found his constitution unable to endure the strain. In his own late autobiography he wrote that 'his very infirm health, had been occasioned not a little by a doubled labor', accomplishing the studies of two years in one. A later account that he was excused from the commencement exercises cannot be confirmed from the college's records. He returned to Virginia in 1772 and studied law. In November 1772 he wrote to his college friend William Bradford: 'I am too dull and infirm now to look out for extraordinary things in this world, for I think my sensations for many months have intimated to me not to expect a long or healthy life'. The Miller Center describes these as some of the most morose letters of his life, written with failing health and the expectation of an early death; Hunt describes a solitary, purposeless period darkened by enfeebled health. In December 1774 he was appointed to the Orange County Committee of Safety, which oversaw the county militia; he worked to build up its strength. His health kept him from serving in the militia in the Revolution; a nineteenth-century biographer judged that his place, from temperament and want of physical vigor, was in the council, not the field. In 1776, at 25, he was elected to the Virginia Convention that framed the state's constitution and met Thomas Jefferson for the first time that October. Hunt describes him then as five feet six and a quarter inches tall, thin and delicate, with a countenance that 'bespoke the suffering of bad health'. After his presidency (1809 to 1817) there was no continuous period when his health was good. He had the prevailing bilious fever in the autumn of 1821 and recovered quickly; he suffered increasingly from rheumatism, which by his last years crippled his fingers; at 83 he was so disabled by it that he lived in one room, and he complained that one ear was deaf. His mind remained sharp. He died at Montpelier on the morning of 28 June 1836, aged 85, the last of the Founding Fathers, extremely emaciated and growing gradually weaker; he died alone in his chair over his breakfast, minutes after his niece brought it. At his father's death in 1801 he inherited Montpelier and more than 100 enslaved African Americans. He continued to hold enslaved people, became president of the American Colonization Society, and under debt sold some of them; his will did not free them but left them to his widow with the wish that they not be sold without their consent, a wish she did not honor.
- **R:** Later in life he suffered periodic bouts of an unknown malady that some historians, including his biographers Ralph Ketcham and Irving Brant, suspect may have been epilepsy. A physician writing in 2018 described an account of his sudden collapse at a militia drill in 1775 as an 'absence seizure' while noting that whether his later agitation came from a seizure disorder or from anxiety is not known.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
