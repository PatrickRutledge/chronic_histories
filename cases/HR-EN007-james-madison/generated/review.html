Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Calendar and dates. Virginia used the Julian (Old Style) calendar, with the legal year beginning on 25 March, until 1752. He was born on 5 March 1750/51 (Old Style), which is 16 March 1751 in the Gregorian calendar; the Library of Congress gives both, and his own family sketch of 1834 writes “5th of March, 1751 (O. S.)”, counting the year from 1 January. The record follows the Jefferson record: the Gregorian date is preferred and the Julian date is a superseded alternative. All his other documented dates fall after 1752 and are Gregorian. The conversion was checked by computing Julian day numbers in the build script.
  2. Index age and dates. The episode presents him at 23, in the spring of 1774, two years after his documented return from college and his letter of November 1772, and before his appointment to the Committee of Safety in December 1774. The synthetic date of birth is March 16, 2003, so the whole-year shift is 252 years and 2026 maps to 1774. The visits are April 7 (index), April 30 (video-EEG discharge and consultation), June 11, July 16 (preventive), August 20 (sick), and September 24, 2026; video-EEG ran from April 27 and the MRI was on April 14. In the true frame these fall between April and September 1774, within his lifetime. He is 23 throughout.
  3. Name and mentions. “James Madison” splits simply into given and family names, so `identity.name_parts` is not used, and no alias is recorded. Thomas Jefferson (HR-EN005) is named in one documented claim (their first meeting in October 1776), so HR-EN005 is listed in `identity.historical_mentions`; the modern episode does not name him.
  4. What was verified and what was not. His frail childhood, his overwork and infirm health at college, his morose letter of November 1772, his Committee of Safety appointment, the ill health that kept him out of the militia, his later bilious fever, rheumatism, and deafness in one ear, and his death are D, from the Library of Congress, Stagg, Montpelier, the Miller Center, Gay (1884), Hunt (1902), and the Princeton University Archives. The brief's wording from his autobiography, “a constitutional liability to sudden attacks, somewhat resembling epilepsy, and suspending the intellectual functions”, could not be verified: Founders Online (which holds “James Madison: Autobiography, December 1830”) did not return readable text, the 1945 Adair edition could not be read, and the phrase was found only on blogs and reading notes. It is therefore not quoted in any claim; the record says only that ill health kept him from military service and that historians suspect periodic attacks were epilepsy. The account of a collapse at a militia drill in 1775 appears in a 2018 newspaper column without a source and is recorded only as that column's retrospective interpretation (CLM-0008). The Ketcham, Brant, and Cheney biographies could not be read.
  5. Corrections to the roster. The roster says he had “documented epileptic seizures throughout life”. No source read documents epilepsy; the Princeton University Archives says only that some historians suspect it, so it is R, disputed (CLM-0008), and is not a historical diagnosis. The roster's “chronic GI illness” and “depression episodes” are not documented as such; the record keeps to his documented infirm health and morose letter (CLM-0005) and the bilious fever of 1821 (CLM-0010). “Smallest US president physically” is not used; Hunt's height of five feet six and a quarter inches is.
  6. Peer-reviewed proposals. No peer-reviewed article proposing functional (psychogenic nonepileptic) seizures, or any specific diagnosis, for his attacks was found; a search result that appeared to (Cavanna 2021) contains no mention of him. Abrams (2024) is cited only as context for his frequent illnesses.
  7. Enslavement. That his family's wealth rested on enslaved labor, that the enslaved built Montpelier's brick house, that he inherited more than 100 enslaved people in 1801, sold some under debt, and did not free them in his will are stated plainly as documented social history (CLM-0002, CLM-0012), without any health information about the people he enslaved.
  8. Presenting problem and diagnosis. The intake problem (R40.4) is recurrent sudden episodes of unresponsiveness, resolved on April 30, 2026, when functional/dissociative seizures (F44.5), generalized anxiety disorder (F41.1), and a moderate depressive episode (F32.1) are recorded. The diagnosis was chosen between epilepsy, functional/dissociative seizures, and syncope and decided for functional/dissociative seizures at the ILAE “documented” level: two habitual events on video-EEG with a normal waking EEG throughout, closed and resisting eyes, a fluctuating course, normal interictal EEG and MRI, normal ECG and orthostatic vital signs, and no response to levetiracetam. ICD-10-CM F44.5 is the code for functional seizures; the record uses the ILAE task force term “functional/dissociative seizures” in text and does not assert what his historical attacks were. F44.5 is placed both_with_link to the documented history.
  9. Antiseizure medicine. To teach avoiding unneeded antiseizure medicines, the synthetic episode begins with levetiracetam started at an emergency visit without an EEG; it is continued unchanged until video-EEG and then withdrawn over three weeks, with no driving during the taper (AAN 2025).
  10. Safety assessment. Required by the F-codes and a PHQ-9 of 13. No suicidal ideation (PHQ-9 item 9 scored 0; C-SSRS screen negative); a fatalistic expectation of an early death echoes, without asserting, his documented letter.
