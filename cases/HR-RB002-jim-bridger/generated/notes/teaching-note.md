# Teaching Note — Jim Bridger pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** James Bridger was born on March 17, 1804, in Richmond, Virginia, the son of James and Chloe Bridger. The family moved west to Missouri when he was a boy, and his mother, a brother and his father died there within a few years, leaving him an orphan at about fourteen with a younger sister. After an apprenticeship as a blacksmith, he joined William Ashley's fur-trapping expedition to the Rocky Mountains in 1822. He spent more than four decades in the West as a trapper, a partner in a fur company, a trader, and from 1849 to 1868 a guide and scout for emigrants, surveyors and the Army. In a fight with Blackfeet men in 1832 he was struck in the back by two arrows; the iron head of one could not be removed and stayed deep in his back. In August 1835, at the fur traders' rendezvous on the Green River, the missionary physician Marcus Whitman removed the arrowhead from his back. A companion of Whitman's described an iron arrowhead three inches long, hooked at the point where it had struck a large bone and surrounded by tissue that had grown around it; the operation was difficult, and Bridger bore it steadily while trappers and Indians watched. In 1843 he and his partner Louis Vasquez founded Fort Bridger, a trading post for emigrants on Blacks Fork of the Green River in what is now southwestern Wyoming. He married three Native women in succession: the daughter of a Flathead chief, who died in 1846; a Ute woman, who died in 1849; and in 1850 a Shoshone woman. He had several children, some of whom he sent to school in Missouri. He could not read, but he enjoyed having books read aloud to him; an army officer's account from his scouting years describes him listening to Shakespeare's plays read until midnight. By his early sixties the effects of arthritis, rheumatism and rugged frontier life had caught up with him; in 1867 at Fort Phil Kearny he was troubled by rheumatism that hampered his movements, and in 1868 he moved back to his farm near Westport, Missouri. In his later years he had a goiter and a rupture (hernia), which troubled him somewhat; the cause of the goiter is not recorded. His eyesight began to fail in the late 1860s, and from 1873 it failed rapidly, so that he could recognize friends only by the sound of their voices. The cause of his failing sight is not recorded in the sources read. By late 1874 or early 1875 he was almost totally blind. He rode his farm on an old horse and walked his fields with a cane and his dog, and spoke of wishing he could see the mountains again. The cause of his blindness is not recorded in the sources read. He spent his last years on his farm near Westport, proud of his apple orchard, and died there on July 17, 1881, at 77. He was buried on the farm; in 1904 his old friend General Grenville Dodge had his remains moved to Mount Washington Cemetery in Independence, Missouri, and raised a monument.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
