Assessment

  • Opioid use disorder, severe (F11.20). Seven DSM-5-TR criteria: larger amounts than intended, unsuccessful efforts to cut down, much time spent obtaining prescriptions, craving, continued use despite physical problems, tolerance and withdrawal, which count here because the opioids were not taken as prescribed. Buprenorphine lowers the risk of overdose and death and fits a touring life better than methadone, which needs daily clinic dosing. A standard start is suitable because his opioids are short-acting and he is already in moderate withdrawal; a low-dose start would be chosen for someone who cannot tolerate withdrawal or is on methadone. [S]
  • Sedative, hypnotic, or anxiolytic use disorder (benzodiazepines), moderate (F13.20). Five criteria, including withdrawal. Abrupt stopping risks seizures. The 2025 joint guideline allows a switch to a single longer-acting benzodiazepine, advises against tapering during other major changes, and suggests reductions of about 5 to 10% every two to four weeks, adjusted to symptoms. Taking a benzodiazepine is not a reason to withhold buprenorphine, but the combination raises the risk of sedation and slowed breathing, especially with sleep apnea. [S]
  • Obstructive sleep apnea (G47.33). Moderate obstructive sleep apnea with a small central component; CPAP is important while he takes buprenorphine and diazepam, and no additional night sedative should be used. [S]
