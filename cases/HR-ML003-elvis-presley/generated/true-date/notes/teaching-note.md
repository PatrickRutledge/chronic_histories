# Teaching Note — Elvis Presley pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Elvis Aron Presley was born in a two-room house in Tupelo, Mississippi, on January 8, 1935; his family moved to Memphis when he was 13. He made his first record in 1953, became a national star in 1956, served in the Army in West Germany from 1958 to 1960, and by the 1970s performed long engagements in Las Vegas and toured constantly. In 1974 he played Las Vegas from January 26 to February 9, toured through much of March, including four shows in Memphis on March 16 and 17 and a recorded show there on March 20, played Lake Tahoe from May 16 to 26, toured again in mid-June, and took a few weeks off at Graceland from early July. From October 15 to November 1, 1973, he was treated at Baptist Memorial Hospital in Memphis under an assumed name; at the 1981 trial of his physician, an internal medicine specialist who reviewed the records testified that the consulting physicians were "withdrawing him from narcotic drugs." He was treated at Baptist Hospital again from January 28 to February 13, 1975, when the admission note recorded abdominal problems and swelling of the face, hands and legs, and from August 21 to September 25, 1975, for shortness of breath and swelling. In 1977 the medical examiner said that the drugs he found were those prescribed for hypertension and a blockage of the colon, for which Presley had been hospitalized twice in 1975. In 1977 an unnamed Baptist Hospital employee told Rolling Stone that he had been treated there for "hypertension, enlarged colon, gastroenteritis, stomach inflammation," had "a severe liver condition," had received cortisone, and weighed at least 230 pounds; the hospital's doctors discounted a suggestion of lupus. He was about six feet tall and had once weighed about 175 pounds; in his last years he was reported to be overweight and at times appeared bloated on stage. He had glaucoma, including an episode of acute angle-closure glaucoma in 1971, and was under the care of one ophthalmologist from 1971 on. At the 1981 trial of his physician, George Nichopoulos, prosecutors showed that the physician had prescribed about 19,000 doses of stimulants, sedatives and painkillers for him in the last 30 months of his life, including 1,679 addictive pills in 205 days between the two 1975 hospital stays; the defense argued that he obtained drugs from many other sources, including amphetamines mailed from the West Coast, and a detective testified to collecting prescriptions from 140 Memphis pharmacies. Testimony at the trial described him as addicted to prescribed stimulants, sedatives and painkillers; the internal medicine specialist testified, "Absolutely. He was addicted." On November 4, 1981, the jury acquitted the physician of overprescribing, citing his efforts to substitute placebos and intercept drugs sent from elsewhere; the physician then said that drugs "may have played a part" in the death. It was believed that he neither smoked nor drank alcohol. On August 16, 1977, at 42, he was found unresponsive on the floor of his bathroom at Graceland, his home in Memphis, and was pronounced dead at Baptist Memorial Hospital. After the weeks off in July 1974 he played the Las Vegas Hilton from August 19 to September 2, talking on stage about karate, and toured from September 27 to October 14, ending at Lake Tahoe on October 11 to 14.
- **R:** A 2025 ophthalmology review interprets his glaucoma as steroid-induced, from lifelong use of steroids both prescribed and self-administered, with secondary angle closure most likely due to anterior uveitis. The cause of his death is disputed. The medical examiner gave cardiac arrhythmia, citing a history of mild hypertension and evidence of coronary artery disease, and later ruled it heart disease; the autopsy report was never released. Trial testimony said that 14 drugs, including codeine at ten times the maximum accepted level, were found in tissue samples, and later accounts attribute the death to multiple prescription drugs, to heart disease, or to straining with a severely enlarged colon. His physician later said that he had a severe form of megacolon, his colon about twice its normal diameter and impacted with stool at his death.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
