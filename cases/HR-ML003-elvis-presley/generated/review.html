Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Sources. The case relies on The New York Times obituary of August 17, 1977 (read in its On This Day archive), Graceland's official timeline, four UPI wire reports of the 1981 trial, the 1977 Rolling Stone report, a 2026 HISTORY article, and the PubMed abstract of Zegers et al. (2025). The Memphis Commercial Appeal could not be reached by the search and fetch tools, and Peter Guralnick's biographies were not read, so neither is cited; the 1977 Rolling Stone report quotes the Commercial Appeal once, but that quotation is not used. The 1980 action of the Tennessee medical board against his physician and the 1979 ABC 20/20 broadcast were found only in secondary sources and are not claims, apart from the UPI mention of the broadcast's conclusion, which is also not used.
  2. Recent-death rule. He died in 1977, so the rule does not apply. Drug and mental-health claims are nonetheless marked sensitive, and the modern episode is synthetic throughout.
  3. Index age and dates. The index visit on July 10, 2026 maps to July 10, 1974, when he was 39: nine months after the October 1973 hospital stay in which, by later testimony, he was withdrawn from narcotic drugs, and during the few weeks off at Graceland that Graceland's timeline places from early July 1974. The synthetic date of birth is January 8, 1987 (shift 52 years). The visits on July 17, August 7 and August 14 map to the weeks off before the Las Vegas engagement of August 19 to September 2, 1974; September 9 and September 23 to the gap between that engagement and the tour of September 27 to October 14; and October 28 to the weeks after the tour. The timeline does not say where he was in each gap; no visit falls on a documented engagement. Past medical history includes only events up to October 1973; the 1975 hospital stays, the prescribing totals, the death and the trial are linked to later timeline events and are left out of the intake documents.
  4. Presenting problem. Fatigue, weight gain, constipation, daytime sleepiness and high blood pressure (R53.83) is the intake problem; it ends at the one-week visit, when the consultation, the polysomnogram and the home readings give the diagnoses of opioid use disorder (F11.20), benzodiazepine use disorder (F13.20), stage 2 hypertension (I10), obstructive sleep apnea (G47.33) and opioid-induced constipation (K59.03), all recorded on that date. Obesity and glaucoma were evident at intake and are recorded then. Intake documents name no diagnosis made later and no later treatment choice.
  5. Difference from the panel's existing record of substance use disorders from prescribed drugs. That record teaches barbiturate and stimulant use disorders from drugs given to a minor, a hospital change to phenobarbital, depression care and CBT for insomnia, with naloxone not indicated. This record teaches a polypharmacy reconciliation across five prescribers, opioid use disorder treated with buprenorphine, two benzodiazepines combined and tapered alongside buprenorphine, naloxone from the first visit, and the cardiometabolic, sleep, bowel and eye consequences of the drugs, with care around touring. The appetite suppressant is stopped as a contraindicated drug, with no stimulant use disorder diagnosed.
  6. Drugs in the modern episode. The documented record names stimulants, sedatives and painkillers in large amounts from several sources. The synthetic drugs are present-day equivalents chosen for teaching (hydromorphone, oxycodone with acetaminophen, diazepam, alprazolam and phentermine); they are not claims about the drugs he took. The roster's “10,000+ doses in the final year” is not used; the case gives the 19,000 doses over about 30 months and the 1,679 pills in 205 days that UPI reported from the trial.
  7. Cause of death (CLM-0013, R, disputed). The medical examiner gave cardiac arrhythmia and later heart disease; trial testimony reported 14 drugs in tissue samples; later accounts emphasize multiple drugs, heart disease, or straining with an enlarged colon. The case records the conflict and assumes none of these. The megacolon account comes second-hand through a biographer quoted in a 2026 article and is R, possible (CLM-0014); the 1977 report of “a blockage of the colon” is D.
  8. Liver. The only source for liver disease is an unnamed hospital employee quoted by Rolling Stone in 1977 (CLM-0005, D, probable). The synthetic episode records mildly raised liver tests that normalize with weight loss and stopping the acetaminophen-containing opioid; no liver diagnosis is coded.
  9. Glaucoma (CLM-0007, D; CLM-0008, R, probable). The 1971 acute angle-closure episode and his ophthalmologist's care are from the Zegers abstract; the steroid explanation is that review's interpretation. The modern diagnosis, glaucoma secondary to drugs (H40.63X1), is synthetic. His ophthalmologist is not named. The modern teaching point is that phentermine, decongestants and anticholinergic sleep aids are avoided in glaucoma, and steroids are used only when essential.
  10. Real people. His physician, George Nichopoulos (who died in 2016), appears only in documented trial history; no modern decision, statement or health fact is attributed to him. The medical examiner and the expert witness are not named. No living person is named. His former wife and his daughter (who died in 2023) are not named and nothing is attributed to either; the social history mentions no family member. Friends who carry naloxone, the advance care planning agent and the household are unnamed and synthetic.
  11. Firearms. The safety assessment records synthetic firearms at home, stored locked and unloaded during the medicine changes. This is a means-safety teaching point and is not linked to any historical claim.
  12. DSM-5-TR counts. Tolerance and withdrawal are counted for both disorders because the drugs were not taken as prescribed (more than prescribed, from several prescribers); the opioid use disorder is severe (seven criteria) and the benzodiazepine use disorder moderate (five). ICD-10-CM codes both as dependence, uncomplicated.
  13. Clinical judgment calls. (1) Buprenorphine was not started at intake, by his choice to see the specialist first; the week in between was covered by naloxone, a single prescriber, seven-day supplies at the verified doses and a locked box. (2) A standard in-office start at COWS 12, rather than a low-dose start. (3) Alprazolam was combined into diazepam at 40 mg a day, a cautious equivalent, and held for three weeks before tapering; steps of about 6 to 11% every five to seven weeks, held during the engagement. (4) Buprenorphine was not withheld because of the benzodiazepine. (5) Amlodipine with valsartan as one pill for stage 2 hypertension more than 10 mmHg above the diastolic target; a thiazide was avoided because of his angle-closure history. (6) Tirzepatide rather than semaglutide because it is approved for obstructive sleep apnea with obesity, started only after buprenorphine was stable and the bowels regular. (7) In-laboratory polysomnography because chronic opioids make home testing unreliable. (8) Phentermine and the diphenhydramine sleep aid were stopped at intake. (9) Acute back pain was managed by splitting buprenorphine, with naproxen and no muscle relaxant or full-agonist opioid. (10) MME uses the CDC 2022 conversion factors (hydromorphone 5, oxycodone 1.5).
  14. Instruments.
