## Historical basis

- Martin Luther was born on 10 November 1483 (Julian calendar) in Eisleben, into a relatively prosperous mining family; his father, Hans, gave him a good education and intended him for law. **D**
- He entered the University of Erfurt in 1501 and became a Master of Arts in 1505; later in 1505, after a vow to St Anna made in a thunderstorm, he entered the Augustinian monastery at Erfurt against his father's wishes, and he was ordained in 1507. **D**
- As a monk, although he made full use of confession, he felt that he was 'a sinner before God with an extremely disturbed conscience', and this anxiety and fear meant that he underwent what he termed Anfechtungen, spiritual trials or temptations, in particular about predestination. **D**
- He became professor of the Bible at Wittenberg in 1512 and held the post until his death. He published the Ninety-Five Theses on 31 October 1517, refused to recant at the Diet of Worms in 1521, was declared an outlaw, was sheltered at the Wartburg, and returned to Wittenberg on 6 March 1522. **D**
- In 1525 he married Katharina von Bora, a former nun; they had six children, two daughters of whom died young. **D**
- In May 1521, at the Wartburg, he wrote to Philipp Melanchthon that the Lord had afflicted him with painful constipation, and that he had not slept all night. **D**
- In the autumn of 1510, in Bologna, he reported severe headache and whizzing in the ear; from 1523, according to his physician Matthias Ratzeberger, he had regular headaches with fainting spells and vertigo. **D**
- From 1526 on he had sporadic attacks of urinary stones, which reminded him of death because of the excruciating pain; the first stone, in 1526, was preceded by headache, heaviness in the head, vertigo and nausea and was passed through the urethra. **D**
- On 22 April 1527 (Julian) a severe attack of dizziness forced him to break off a sermon. **D**
- On the evening of 6 July 1527 (Julian), when he was 43, he had an attack that began with a roaring tinnitus in his left ear, which increased and seemed to fill the left half of his head, followed by nausea, pallor, coldness and a state of collapse without loss of consciousness; he prayed and believed he would not survive the night. After a night's rest all the symptoms had subsided except the tinnitus, which persisted. His friends Justus Jonas and Johannes Bugenhagen wrote accounts of it. **D**
- In the first half of 1527 he was deeply disturbed by disputes with other reformers over the Lord's Supper and suffered severe depression. **D**
- Feldmann concluded from Luther's letters and his friends' reports that the attacks with tinnitus and vertigo that began in 1527 were a typical case of Ménière disease of the left ear, more than 330 years before Ménière's description; earlier writers had read them as a psychiatric disorder or as chronic inflammation of the middle ear, and a later Japanese review reads them as chronic suppurative otitis media. **R**
