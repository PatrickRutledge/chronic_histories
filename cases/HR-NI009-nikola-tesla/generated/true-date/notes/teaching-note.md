# Teaching Note — Nikola Tesla pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Nikola Tesla was born on 10 July 1856 in the village of Smiljan, in what is now Croatia, to Serbian parents; his father was an Orthodox priest. In his memoir My Inventions (1919) he wrote that in boyhood he suffered from "a peculiar affliction due to the appearance of images, often accompanied by strong flashes of light", pictures of things he had really seen, which caused him great discomfort and anxiety; at about twelve he first banished an image by wilful effort, but he never controlled the flashes, which grew stronger until he was about twenty-five. He wrote that he had a violent aversion to women's earrings, that the sight of a pearl would almost give him a fit, and that he would not touch the hair of other people; in 1919 he wrote that he was still "not insensible to some of these upsetting impulses". He wrote that he counted the steps in his walks and calculated the cubical contents of soup plates, coffee cups, and pieces of food, or his meal was unenjoyable, and that all repeated acts had to be divisible by three, and if he missed he felt impelled to do it all over again, even if it took hours. He wrote that from about seventeen he could visualize his inventions with the greatest facility and needed no models, drawings, or experiments. At seventeen he contracted cholera. In 1881 he moved to Budapest to help install a telephone exchange; there, in his own account, he suffered "the complete breakdown of the nerves", with extreme sensitivity to sound, light, and vibration, a pulse he described as ranging up to 260 beats, and twitching and tremors; a physician called his illness unique and incurable, and he recovered with a powerful desire to live and the help of a friend. In 1884 he went to New York; by 1901 he was building a tower and laboratory for wireless transmission at Wardenclyffe on Long Island. He claimed that his working hours at his first job in New York were from 10:30 in the morning until 5 the next morning, and even into old age he said he slept only two or three hours a night. After the Wardenclyffe project was abandoned for lack of money in 1905, PBS reports, he had a complete nervous breakdown. In later years he rescued injured pigeons and nursed them in his hotel room; his aversion to germs heightened, and he began to wash his hands compulsively and would eat only boiled foods, which prompted speculation about his mental well-being. Financial difficulties continued as his patents expired; increasingly reclusive, he lived alone in a room in the New Yorker Hotel until he died there on 7 January 1943.
- **R:** A 2013 neurology review reads the experiences he described in his autobiography as including migraine auras, synesthesias, obsessions, and compulsions. The roster lists obsessive-compulsive disorder and probable autism spectrum features; no diagnosis was made in his lifetime, a diagnosis of obsessive-compulsive disorder is a later interpretation of his own accounts, and autism is speculative and is not diagnosed in this record.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
