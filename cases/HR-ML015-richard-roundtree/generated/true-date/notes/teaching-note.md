# Teaching Note — Richard Roundtree pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Richard Roundtree was born on July 9, 1942, in New Rochelle, New York. Roundtree was an actor best known as the private detective John Shaft in films beginning in the early 1970s, and is described as the first Black action hero. Roundtree told PEOPLE that at the end of September 1993, in the shower, he felt a lump under his left nipple, 'the size of a lima bean'; he was not overly concerned at first, because at 13 he had had gynecomastia (breast enlargement), and he 'just chalked it up to that.' In a 2019 television interview Roundtree said he found the lump in the shower while working in Costa Rica in 1993 and saw his doctor after returning to Los Angeles a week later; the doctor biopsied it and four or five days later told him he had breast cancer. Asked whether he had felt shame, he answered 'Embarrassment,' and he said men 'are so cavalier about their health issues and want to sweep it under the rug.' Roundtree told PEOPLE that his physician called him four or five days after the visit to tell him in person; that a day or two later he was in the hospital for a modified radical mastectomy, which removed the complete breast from the sternum to the underarm, including the nipple and areola; and that the cancer had spread to the lymph nodes. The same article describes the operation as removing his left breast. Sources differ on the extent of the operation: his own account in PEOPLE describes a modified radical mastectomy of the left breast, while the Associated Press obituary and an encyclopedia article (citing a 2001 USA Today interview) say he underwent a double mastectomy. Roundtree said that after the operation he had six months of chemotherapy, that he lost 18 pounds, and that 'the nausea is something you never, ever forget.' Roundtree said he kept his treatment secret for fear it would end his film career, keeping his undershirt on during the physical examinations required for each film; that 'nobody ever knew I was even in the hospital'; that about six years after treatment, when he had been given the all-clear, he decided to speak up, telling PEOPLE in 2000; and that he was 'not embarrassed,' urging men to speak about breast cancer. In the 2019 interview he said that the left side of his chest had no feeling under the arm. Roundtree died at his home in Los Angeles on October 24, 2023, aged 81; his longtime manager said he had been diagnosed with pancreatic cancer.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
