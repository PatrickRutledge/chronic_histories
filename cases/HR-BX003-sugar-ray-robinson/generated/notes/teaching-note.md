# Teaching Note — Sugar Ray Robinson pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** He was born Walker Smith Jr. on May 3, 1921. The Michigan Sports Hall of Fame gives his birthplace as Ailey, Georgia, with the family later moving to Detroit; the 1989 UPI obituary says he was born in Detroit. As a boy he moved with his family to Harlem in New York, and he won the Golden Gloves featherweight title in 1939. He held the world welterweight championship, won in 1946, and the world middleweight title five times, first in 1951. He boxed professionally from 1940 to 1965, about 200 bouts in all: the 1989 UPI obituary counts 201 fights, 109 won by knockout and 65 by decision; the 1987 UPI report gives a record of 174-19-6 and the Michigan Sports Hall of Fame 173-19-6. After boxing he settled in Los Angeles, living modestly in the top story of a duplex on West Adams Boulevard, and in 1969 founded the Sugar Ray Robinson Youth Foundation for inner-city children in the Los Angeles area. In July 1987, at 65, he was reported (in a Sports Illustrated article summarized by UPI) to have Alzheimer's disease, diabetes and hypertension, to depend on his wife for daily care, and to be virtually incapable of speech. He died on April 12, 1989, at 67, after paramedics brought him to Brotman Medical Center in Culver City, California, in full arrest; his wife told reporters he had suffered from Alzheimer's disease and diabetes for years and that his blood sugar had been very high that morning. His Alzheimer's disease and diabetes are documented in the press reports of his last years; when either was diagnosed is not stated in the sources read. Hypertension is documented only in the 1987 press report.
- **R:** The project roster describes his dementia as probable boxing-related dementia pugilistica; no source read records that diagnosis, chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or an autopsy finding.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
