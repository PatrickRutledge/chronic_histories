# Teaching Note — Susan B. Anthony pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Susan B. Anthony was born on February 15, 1820, in Adams, Massachusetts, the second child of Daniel and Lucy Read Anthony. Anthony voted in the 1872 presidential election, was arrested at her Rochester home, and was convicted at Canandaigua, New York; she refused to pay the $100 fine. Her father, Daniel Anthony, who had not had a day's illness in forty years, was suddenly stricken in November 1862 with what was called acute neuralgia of the stomach and died two weeks later, on November 25, 1862, at 69. Her 1898 biography describes a long-lived family: her mother died at eighty-six, her paternal grandfather lived past ninety-seven, and both maternal grandparents died in their eighty-fourth year. In January 1891, after weeks of what seemed a persistent la grippe, Anthony collapsed at the Garrison home in Brookline, Massachusetts; pneumonia threatened and she kept to her room for a week. On July 26, 1895, near the end of an address at Lakeside, Ohio, Anthony stopped abruptly and fainted; from July 30 she spent a month in bed with what was called nervous prostration, described as her first serious illness in seventy-five years. Her biographer described her daily habits in the late 1890s: a cold sponge bath each morning, simple food, tea and coffee in moderation, a rest after meals and an hour's rest each afternoon, and fresh air and exercise every day, walking from the station after a day on the train. In February 1906, although in failing health, Anthony traveled to Washington, D.C.; after returning to Rochester she went to bed, attended by Dr. Ricker and two nurses, and died about three weeks later. Anthony died at her home on Madison Street in Rochester, New York, at 12:40 a.m. on March 13, 1906, aged 86; the National Park Service records the cause as heart failure and pneumonia.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
