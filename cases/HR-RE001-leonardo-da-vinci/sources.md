## Historical evidence

1. Museo Galileo, Institute and Museum of the History of Science, Florence. “Leonardo's childhood” (itinerary). Quotes his grandfather Antonio's note: “My grandson was born, son of Ser Piero my son, on the day of April 15, a Saturday, at the hour of three in the night”; states that the note places the birth in Vinci and “confutes the hypothesis that Leonardo was born at Anchiano”; describes Ser Piero as a notary; and quotes the 1458 land-office return, “Lionardo son of the said Ser Piero, not legitimate, born of him and Chateri[n]a”. https://brunelleschi.imss.fi.it/itineraries/itinerary/Childhood.html
2. Museo Leonardiano di Vinci. “Leonardo's birthplace in Anchiano.” “Leonardo was born on April 15th, 1452, love child of Ser Piero da Vinci and Caterina”; the grandfather's annotation shows that he “certainly came to light in Vinci”, while the Anchiano house has a tradition as the birthplace “rooted in the centuries”. https://museoleonardiano.it/en/explore-the-museum/la-casa-natale-di-leonardo/
3. Château du Clos Lucé. “Explore Leonardo da Vinci's last home,” a story by the château on Google Arts & Culture. He “settled at the Château du Clos Lucé in the fall of 1516” at the invitation of Francis I, who “expressed a profound respect for da Vinci, who he called 'my father'”; he received Cardinal d'Aragon on October 10, 1517, and the cardinal's secretary, Antonio de Beatis, reported that he showed “a Florentine lady painted from life at the request of the late Giuliano de' Medici”; on April 23, 1519 he made his will, “bequeathing his manuscripts, his sketchbooks and his sketches to his beloved disciple, Francesco Melzi”; he died in his bed at Clos Lucé on May 2, 1519, aged 67. https://artsandculture.google.com/story/zwURhU_1pPuSIg
4. Lazzeri D, Rossi C. “The right hand palsy of Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519): new insights on the occasion of the 500th anniversary of his death.” *J R Soc Med* 2019;112(8):330-333. PMID 31051083; PMC6699018. The shading and hatching of his drawings slope “from upper left to lower right, leaving few doubts about his left-handedness”; a review concluded that “he used his right hand when creating work other than drawing and writing”, and “it is claimed that he never painted using his left hand”; de Beatis's diary of the 1517 visit, as quoted: “One cannot indeed expect any more good work from him, as a certain paralysis has crippled his right hand … And although Messer Leonardo can no longer paint with the sweetness which was peculiar to him, he can still design and instruct others”; his late drawings (the Deluge, cats, horses, dragons, water, machines, anatomy); Vasari's statement that “with his right hand he could bend the ring of an iron door knocker or a horseshoe as if they were lead” and the 1505 Raimondi engraving of him playing the lira da braccio; Vasari's account of his last months and death after a “paroxysm, the messenger of death”, in the King's arms; the Figino red-chalk portrait showing “his right-hand suspended in a stiff, contracted position”, which “does not depict the typical clenched hand seen in post-stroke muscular spasticity”, so that “ulnar palsy more plausible”, most likely “an acute post-traumatic ulnar neuropathy rather than a stroke”, perhaps sustained in a syncope; focal dystonia as another possibility; and Dupuytren's disease as unlikely. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6699018/
5. de Campos D, Coutinho Rodrigues D, Buso L. “The right hand of Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519): ulnar or median nerve palsy?” *J R Soc Med* 2019;112(11):452. PMID 31710828; PMC7164313. The hand in Figino's drawing is in the position of the “hand of benediction”, “the result of an injury to the median nerve”, not the claw hand of an ulnar palsy. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7164313/
6. Schott GD. “Mirror writing: neurological reflections on an unusual phenomenon.” *J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry* 2007;78(1):5-13. PMID 16963501; PMC2117809. His left-handed mirror writing “dated from age 21 years or earlier” and was “written for himself and not for others”; “His probable stroke”: “paralysis of the right arm”, “unimpaired speech”, “unimpaired left-handed painting and mirror writing”. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2117809/
7. Catani M, Mazzarello P. “Grey Matter Leonardo da Vinci: a genius driven to distraction.” *Brain* 2019;142(6):1842-1846. PMID 31121603; PMC6536914. “Could Leonardo have had attention deficit and hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)?”; “He was left-handed and aged 65 he suffered a severe left hemisphere stroke, which left his language abilities intact”; “his notebooks show mirror writing and spelling errors that have been considered suggestive of dyslexia”. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6536914/
8. Ozturk S. “Leonardo Da Vinci (1452–1519) as a stroke victim: hemiparesis: a result of a vegetarian diet?” *J Med Biogr* 2009;17(1):7. PMID 19190190 (bibliographic record only). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19190190/
9. Longstaff A. *Calendars from around the world.* Royal Museums Greenwich. The Gregorian reform of 1582 removed ten days. https://www.rmg.co.uk/sites/default/files/Calendars-from-around-the-world.pdf
10. HISTMED Master Roster v3. Internal project source. Its entries were provisional and were not treated as independent corroboration.

## Clinical guidance

1. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons. “Cubital Tunnel Syndrome (Ulnar Nerve Entrapment at the Elbow).” OrthoInfo. “Leaning on your elbow for long periods of time can put pressure on the nerve”; “Prior fracture or dislocation of the elbow” is a risk factor; “Most causes of compression of the ulnar nerve cannot be seen on an X-ray”; nerve conduction studies “help identify where it is being compressed”; “a padded brace or splint to wear at night to keep your elbow in a straight position”; “Avoid leaning on your elbow”; nerve gliding exercises; surgery when “Nonsurgical methods have not improved your condition”, “The ulnar nerve is very compressed”, or “Nerve compression has caused muscle weakness or damage”; options include cubital tunnel release and ulnar nerve transposition; “Once this happens, muscle wasting cannot be reversed.” https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/diseases--conditions/ulnar-nerve-entrapment-at-the-elbow/
2. Caliandro P, et al. “Treatment for ulnar neuropathy at the elbow.” *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2025;4:CD006839 (PMID 40298125), updating the 2016 review (PMID 27845501). Simple decompression and decompression with transposition are about equally effective, including when nerve impairment is severe, and transposition has more wound infections; in mild cases, advice on movements and positions to avoid may reduce discomfort, with no added benefit from night splinting and nerve gliding in one small trial; corticosteroid injection was no better than placebo at three months; the evidence does not show when to treat conservatively or surgically. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40298125/

## Editorial resolutions

- **Calendar and dates.** Tuscany and France used the Julian calendar throughout his life (the Gregorian reform came in 1582), and the sources give Julian dates. The record converts them to the proleptic Gregorian calendar for the true frame, as other records with Old Style dates do; the difference was nine days in 1452 and ten days from 1500, checked by computing Julian day numbers in the build script. His birth on 15 April 1452 (Julian) is 24 April 1452; the cardinal's visit on 10 October 1517 is 20 October 1517; his will of 23 April 1519 is 3 May 1519; and his death on 2 May 1519 (Julian) is 12 May 1519. The Florentine year began on 25 March, which does not change the year of an April birth. The preferred birth and death dates are the Gregorian ones, with the Julian dates recorded as superseded alternatives; timeline displays give both. The synthetic date of birth keeps 24 April.
- **Birthplace (correction to the brief).** The brief gives his birthplace as Anchiano near Vinci. The Museo Galileo and the Museo Leonardiano both say that his grandfather's note places the birth in Vinci itself, with Anchiano only a long tradition; the record follows the sources and records both.
- **Index age and dates (adjustment to the brief).** The brief asked for an index age of 65 in late 1517. The synthetic date of birth is April 24, 1961, so the whole-year shift is 509 years and 2026 maps to 1517; because every visit must fall on or before November 1, 2026 (1 November 1517 in the true frame), an index visit after the documented report of 20 October 1517 would leave only twelve days for the whole episode. The index visit is therefore August 3, 2026 (true frame 3 August 1517, Gregorian; 24 July, Julian), in his first year at Amboise, when he was 65; the follow-ups are August 17 (consultation), September 14, and October 20, 2026; the sick visit is September 24 and the wellness visit October 1. The last visit falls on the true-frame date of the cardinal's visit and de Beatis's report (20 October 1517, Gregorian), which is linked to the diagnosis and to later timeline events, not to any intake document. The onset of the real paralysis is not documented, so a modern episode beginning in the summer does not contradict the record.
- **Name.** “da Vinci” means “from Vinci”: he was Leonardo, son of Ser Piero, and “da Vinci” is a toponymic used by his father's family, not a family name in the modern sense. A modern record still needs a family name, and the modern usage (including the peer-reviewed sources, which write “da Vinci's hand”) treats “da Vinci” as one, so `identity.name_parts` is given ["Leonardo"], family “da Vinci”, family_first false, rather than a single given name with no family name; the notes address him as Mr. da Vinci, and the C-CDA and FHIR names read “Leonardo da Vinci”. The alias “Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci” (his full patronymic form, from Lazzeri and Rossi) appears in no other record.
- **What was verified and what was not.** Left-handed drawing and mirror writing are **D**; which hand he painted with is **D**, probable (Lazzeri and Rossi report a review that he used the right for work other than drawing and writing). De Beatis's report of a paralysis of the right hand, with continued drawing and teaching, is **D**, quoted from Lazzeri and Rossi; the diary itself was not read. The brief's wording “could no longer paint with ease” is not the source's: the quoted translation says he could no longer paint “with the sweetness which was peculiar to him”, and the record uses the source's words. The Royal Collection Trust pages (biography and drawings) returned HTTP 403 and are not cited; left-handedness is sourced instead to Lazzeri and Rossi and to Schott. The de Beatis remark that he was over 70 appears only in a secondary French page and is not used. The cause of his death is not known; Vasari's account is recorded as Vasari's.
- **Stroke, ulnar palsy, and other readings.** Stroke (Schott 2007; Catani and Mazzarello 2019; Ozturk 2009), post-traumatic ulnar palsy or focal dystonia (Lazzeri and Rossi 2019), and median nerve palsy (de Campos et al. 2019) are all **R**: stroke and median palsy disputed, ulnar palsy possible. The modern episode uses ulnar neuropathy at the elbow as its teaching diagnosis, placed both_with_link to the de Beatis report and the ulnar hypothesis, and does not assert that he had it. The site (cubital tunnel after a fall onto the bent elbow) was chosen because Lazzeri and Rossi propose an acute post-traumatic ulnar neuropathy and note that the nerve at the cubital tunnel is predisposed to trauma; it also lets the record teach the clinical and electrodiagnostic localization, and the exclusion of stroke, a C8 radiculopathy, plexopathy, and a lesion at Guyon's canal.
- **ADHD, dyslexia, and autism.** The roster's “probable ADHD” and “dyslexia” are retrospective speculation; Catani and Mazzarello (2019) raise both, so they are recorded in CLM-0013 as **R**, possible, and are not problems or diagnoses. The roster's “possible autism spectrum” was not found in any reputable source read and is left out. A 2026 article in the *Journal of the History of the Neurosciences* (PMID 42834700) offers an ADHD-informed model of his unfinished works and states that it is not a retrospective clinical diagnosis; it is not cited, because its comparison names another figure with a record in the dataset.
- **Presenting problem.** Weakness, numbness, and clawing of the right hand (R29.818) is the intake problem, resolved on August 17, 2026, when ulnar neuropathy at the elbow (G56.21) and hypercholesterolemia (E78.00) are recorded after the nerve studies and lipid results. The conservative plan and the statin carry `recorded_date` August 17, and the surgical plan October 20; the intake documents show only the stroke workup, the protective measures, the risk screen, the falls plan, and the referral.
- **Stroke excluded.** An older man with new focal hand weakness needs a stroke excluded, because a small cortical infarct can mimic a peripheral hand palsy. The modern episode does so with a normal neurological examination outside the ulnar territory (NIH Stroke Scale 0, normal language), a same-day MRI of the brain, and electrodiagnosis, and screens stroke risk factors (blood pressure with home readings, lipids and lipoprotein(a), HbA1c, ECG and pulse for atrial fibrillation). The hypercholesterolemia and the statin are synthetic risk-factor findings with no historical basis. The record states that the Pooled Cohort Equations (13.5 percent, meeting the USPSTF threshold) and PREVENT (6.9 percent, borderline) disagree, and that he chose a statin after discussing a coronary calcium scan as an alternative.
- **Treatment choices.** Conservative treatment for about three months (night splint, elbow pad, anti-claw splint, hand therapy, adapted tools; no corticosteroid injection), then, with persistent intrinsic weakness and clawing at about fifteen weeks, a shared decision for in situ decompression (as effective as transposition, with fewer infections, in the Cochrane review), with transposition only if the nerve subluxes and tendon transfers deferred for a persistent claw. Surgery is booked after the episode ends; no operation is recorded. He keeps drawing, writing, and teaching with his left hand throughout, which echoes the documented report that he could still design and instruct others.
