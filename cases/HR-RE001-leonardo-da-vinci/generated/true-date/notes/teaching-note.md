# Teaching Note — Leonardo da Vinci pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** A note by his grandfather Antonio records that his grandson, son of Ser Piero, was born on Saturday 15 April [1452] at the third hour of the night (Julian calendar; 24 April 1452 in the proleptic Gregorian calendar). The museums of Vinci read the note as placing his birth in Vinci itself; a long tradition places it in a farmhouse at nearby Anchiano. He was the son of Ser Piero da Vinci, a notary, and a woman named Caterina; a land-office return of 1458 lists 'Lionardo son of the said Ser Piero, not legitimate, born of him and Chaterina'. He was left-handed: the shading and hatching of his drawings slope from upper left to lower right, and his private notes, from his early twenties onward, are written in mirror script with the left hand. A review of the documents concluded that he used his right hand for work other than drawing and writing, and it has been claimed that he never painted with his left hand. The biographer Giorgio Vasari wrote that he was physically so strong that with his right hand he could bend the ring of an iron door knocker or a horseshoe as if they were lead; an engraving of about 1505 shows him playing the lira da braccio. At the invitation of King Francis I, who called him 'my father', he settled at the Château du Clos Lucé, at Amboise, in the autumn of 1516. On 10 October 1517 (Julian; 20 October, Gregorian) he received Cardinal Luigi d'Aragona at Clos Lucé and showed him his paintings, among them a Florentine lady painted from life. The cardinal's secretary, Antonio de Beatis, wrote in his diary that no more good work could be expected of him 'as a certain paralysis has crippled his right hand', and that although he could no longer paint with the sweetness that was peculiar to him, 'he can still design and instruct others'. His late work includes drawings of the Deluge, of cats, horses, and dragons, of water, of machines, and anatomical studies, which scholars take as evidence that his left-handed drawing was unimpaired. On 23 April 1519 (Julian) he made his will, leaving his manuscripts and drawings to his pupil Francesco Melzi, and he died in his bed at Clos Lucé on 2 May 1519 (Julian; 12 May 1519, Gregorian), aged 67. Vasari wrote that, having grown old, he remained ill for many months, and that he died after a paroxysm, the 'messenger of death', in the arms of the King.
- **R:** Several physicians have interpreted the paralysis of his right hand as the result of a stroke: a 2007 review listed a probable stroke with paralysis of the right arm and unimpaired speech; a 2019 essay described a severe left-hemisphere stroke at 65; and a 2009 paper related the stroke to a vegetarian diet. In 2019 Lazzeri and Rossi argued from a sixteenth-century red-chalk portrait attributed to Giovan Ambrogio Figino, which shows his right arm in folds of clothing like a bandage and the hand in a stiff, contracted position, that the hand does not show the clenched spasticity of stroke and that an ulnar nerve palsy (claw hand), most likely after an acute injury, is more plausible; they also raised focal dystonia, and judged Dupuytren's disease unlikely. In reply, de Campos and colleagues argued that the hand in the Figino drawing is in the 'hand of benediction' position of a median nerve injury, not the claw hand of an ulnar palsy. In 2019 Catani and Mazzarello proposed that attention deficit and hyperactivity disorder might explain his lifelong procrastination and difficulty finishing works, and noted that his mirror writing and spelling errors have been considered suggestive of dyslexia.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
