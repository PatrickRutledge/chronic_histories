Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Calendar and dates. Tuscany and France used the Julian calendar throughout his life (the Gregorian reform came in 1582), and the sources give Julian dates. The record converts them to the proleptic Gregorian calendar for the true frame, as other records with Old Style dates do; the difference was nine days in 1452 and ten days from 1500, checked by computing Julian day numbers in the build script. His birth on 15 April 1452 (Julian) is 24 April 1452; the cardinal's visit on 10 October 1517 is 20 October 1517; his will of 23 April 1519 is 3 May 1519; and his death on 2 May 1519 (Julian) is 12 May 1519. The Florentine year began on 25 March, which does not change the year of an April birth. The preferred birth and death dates are the Gregorian ones, with the Julian dates recorded as superseded alternatives; timeline displays give both. The synthetic date of birth keeps 24 April.
  2. Birthplace (correction to the brief). The brief gives his birthplace as Anchiano near Vinci. The Museo Galileo and the Museo Leonardiano both say that his grandfather's note places the birth in Vinci itself, with Anchiano only a long tradition; the record follows the sources and records both.
  3. Index age and dates (adjustment to the brief). The brief asked for an index age of 65 in late 1517. The synthetic date of birth is April 24, 1961, so the whole-year shift is 509 years and 2026 maps to 1517; because every visit must fall on or before November 1, 2026 (1 November 1517 in the true frame), an index visit after the documented report of 20 October 1517 would leave only twelve days for the whole episode. The index visit is therefore August 3, 2026 (true frame 3 August 1517, Gregorian; 24 July, Julian), in his first year at Amboise, when he was 65; the follow-ups are August 17 (consultation), September 14, and October 20, 2026; the sick visit is September 24 and the wellness visit October 1. The last visit falls on the true-frame date of the cardinal's visit and de Beatis's report (20 October 1517, Gregorian), which is linked to the diagnosis and to later timeline events, not to any intake document. The onset of the real paralysis is not documented, so a modern episode beginning in the summer does not contradict the record.
  4. Name. “da Vinci” means “from Vinci”: he was Leonardo, son of Ser Piero, and “da Vinci” is a toponymic used by his father's family, not a family name in the modern sense. A modern record still needs a family name, and the modern usage (including the peer-reviewed sources, which write “da Vinci's hand”) treats “da Vinci” as one, so `identity.name_parts` is given ["Leonardo"], family “da Vinci”, family_first false, rather than a single given name with no family name; the notes address him as Mr. da Vinci, and the C-CDA and FHIR names read “Leonardo da Vinci”. The alias “Leonardo di ser Piero da Vinci” (his full patronymic form, from Lazzeri and Rossi) appears in no other record.
  5. What was verified and what was not. Left-handed drawing and mirror writing are D; which hand he painted with is D, probable (Lazzeri and Rossi report a review that he used the right for work other than drawing and writing). De Beatis's report of a paralysis of the right hand, with continued drawing and teaching, is D, quoted from Lazzeri and Rossi; the diary itself was not read. The brief's wording “could no longer paint with ease” is not the source's: the quoted translation says he could no longer paint “with the sweetness which was peculiar to him”, and the record uses the source's words. The Royal Collection Trust pages (biography and drawings) returned HTTP 403 and are not cited; left-handedness is sourced instead to Lazzeri and Rossi and to Schott. The de Beatis remark that he was over 70 appears only in a secondary French page and is not used. The cause of his death is not known; Vasari's account is recorded as Vasari's.
  6. Stroke, ulnar palsy, and other readings. Stroke (Schott 2007; Catani and Mazzarello 2019; Ozturk 2009), post-traumatic ulnar palsy or focal dystonia (Lazzeri and Rossi 2019), and median nerve palsy (de Campos et al. 2019) are all R: stroke and median palsy disputed, ulnar palsy possible. The modern episode uses ulnar neuropathy at the elbow as its teaching diagnosis, placed both_with_link to the de Beatis report and the ulnar hypothesis, and does not assert that he had it. The site (cubital tunnel after a fall onto the bent elbow) was chosen because Lazzeri and Rossi propose an acute post-traumatic ulnar neuropathy and note that the nerve at the cubital tunnel is predisposed to trauma; it also lets the record teach the clinical and electrodiagnostic localization, and the exclusion of stroke, a C8 radiculopathy, plexopathy, and a lesion at Guyon's canal.
  7. ADHD, dyslexia, and autism. The roster's “probable ADHD” and “dyslexia” are retrospective speculation; Catani and Mazzarello (2019) raise both, so they are recorded in CLM-0013 as R, possible, and are not problems or diagnoses. The roster's “possible autism spectrum” was not found in any reputable source read and is left out. A 2026 article in the Journal of the History of the Neurosciences (PMID 42834700) offers an ADHD-informed model of his unfinished works and states that it is not a retrospective clinical diagnosis; it is not cited, because its comparison names another figure with a record in the dataset.
  8. Presenting problem. Weakness, numbness, and clawing of the right hand (R29.818) is the intake problem, resolved on August 17, 2026, when ulnar neuropathy at the elbow (G56.21) and hypercholesterolemia (E78.00) are recorded after the nerve studies and lipid results. The conservative plan and the statin carry `recorded_date` August 17, and the surgical plan October 20; the intake documents show only the stroke workup, the protective measures, the risk screen, the falls plan, and the referral.
  9. Stroke excluded. An older man with new focal hand weakness needs a stroke excluded, because a small cortical infarct can mimic a peripheral hand palsy. The modern episode does so with a normal neurological examination outside the ulnar territory (NIH Stroke Scale 0, normal language), a same-day MRI of the brain, and electrodiagnosis, and screens stroke risk factors (blood pressure with home readings, lipids and lipoprotein(a), HbA1c, ECG and pulse for atrial fibrillation). The hypercholesterolemia and the statin are synthetic risk-factor findings with no historical basis. The record states that the Pooled Cohort Equations (13.5 percent, meeting the USPSTF threshold) and PREVENT (6.9 percent, borderline) disagree, and that he chose a statin after discussing a coronary calcium scan as an alternative.
  10. Treatment choices. Conservative treatment for about three months (night splint, elbow pad, anti-claw splint, hand therapy, adapted tools; no corticosteroid injection), then, with persistent intrinsic weakness and clawing at about fifteen weeks, a shared decision for in situ decompression (as effective as transposition, with fewer infections, in the Cochrane review), with transposition only if the nerve subluxes and tendon transfers deferred for a persistent claw. Surgery is booked after the episode ends; no operation is recorded. He keeps drawing, writing, and teaching with his left hand throughout, which echoes the documented report that he could still design and instruct others.
  11. Real people. Antonio da Vinci, Ser Piero, Caterina, Francis I, Cardinal d'Aragona, de Beatis, Melzi, Vasari, and Figino appear only in documented history claims. The modern episode names none of them; his patron, students, assistant, and health care agent are unnamed and synthetic, and no statement, decision, or health fact is attributed to any of them.
  12. Family and past history. His father was a notary and his mother is known by her first name; their health and causes of death are not recorded in the sources read. Past history holds only his left-handedness with mirror writing (from his early twenties). The fall that injures the nerve is synthetic (Z91.81) and asserts no historical fall.
  13. Instruments. PHQ-2 (55758-7) is a coded survey. The QuickDASH, NIH Stroke Scale, Mini-Cog, Timed Up and Go, grip and pinch dynamometry, two-point discrimination, MRC grades, and the nerve conduction and ultrasound measurements are kept in the note text and procedures, because no observation code was chosen for them.
  14. Sick and wellness visits. The sick visit is a bilateral adenoviral conjunctivitis caught from a student; it teaches the red flags that separate it from keratitis, iritis, and acute glaucoma, no antibiotic or steroid drops, and hygiene in a shared studio. The wellness visit is `annual_wellness_subsequent` because he is 65; it gives high-dose influenza, COVID-19, and PCV20 vaccines, screens cognition, mood, and falls, and records advance care planning that names no real person.
  15. Focused visits. The three chronic-care follow-ups (two, six, and eleven weeks after intake) are documented as focused visits on the AAOS guidance, the Cochrane review, and the AANEM practice parameter for the ulnar neuropathy, and on the CMID hyperlipidemia monograph with the 2018 cholesterol guideline: the ulnar motor and sensory examination, grip and pinch, the QuickDASH, nerve studies and ultrasound at diagnosis, splint use, hand therapy, corticosteroid injection recorded as not indicated, baseline lipids, ALT, and lipoprotein(a), creatine kinase not indicated, a lipid panel 4 to 12 weeks after the statin (done at nine weeks: LDL down 44 percent), blood pressure at every visit, and the risk estimate. Two deviations are recorded as `deferred`, each with its outcome and corrective action, and no result was invented: the QuickDASH was not taken at intake (first score at the diagnosis visit, before treatment), and hand therapy began two weeks after the referral rather than within the week.
  16. Consultation. The hand surgery referral and consultation (Matteo Lindqvist-Obi, MD, a fictional consultant, on the day of the second visit) are S. The consultant confirms ulnar neuropathy at the elbow with partial conduction block and some axonal loss, excludes a wrist lesion, radiculopathy, and plexopathy, and recommends what the record then shows: three months of splinting and hand therapy without injection, a surgical review at about three months, in situ decompression if strength does not improve, tendon transfers only for a lasting claw, and continued work with the left hand.
