# Teaching Note — Flannery O'Connor pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Mary Flannery O'Connor was born in Savannah, Georgia, on March 25, 1925, the only child of Edward Francis O'Connor and Regina Cline O'Connor. She later dropped her first name for her writing. Her father was diagnosed with lupus in 1937; his illness began with a whitish patch of skin on his forehead and arthritis, and was first diagnosed as rheumatoid arthritis. He died of systemic lupus erythematosus in 1941, a month after his 45th birthday, when she was 15. O'Connor graduated from Georgia State College for Women in 1945 and then studied at the Writers' Workshop of the State University of Iowa, where she earned a Master of Fine Arts; she was writing her first novel by the late 1940s. In December 1949, at 24, she fell seriously ill on a holiday visit to Georgia and spent about a month in hospital, where she had an operation for a floating kidney. In 1950, while living in Connecticut and writing Wise Blood, O'Connor developed joint pain; rheumatoid arthritis was suspected, a diagnosis that later proved wrong. In December 1950 she traveled home to Georgia by train and arrived looking very ill. She was admitted to hospital in Milledgeville, kidney disease was suspected, and on the advice of an Atlanta kidney specialist who suspected lupus she was moved to Emory Hospital in Atlanta, where a positive LE cell test confirmed systemic lupus erythematosus. Fearing the shock of her having the disease that had killed her father, her mother chose not to tell her the diagnosis; O'Connor learned that she had lupus from a friend in mid-1951. She was discharged on daily injections of ACTH, which she learned to give herself, and was later treated with cortisone and prednisone. In early 1953 she wrote that she was nearly bald on top and had a "watermelon face". From 1951 until 1964 O'Connor lived with her mother at Andalusia, the family farm outside Milledgeville, Georgia, where she wrote and kept peafowl and other birds. By the spring of 1954 she had a limp and progressive hip pain, and she used crutches for most of her remaining years. She later wrote that her doctors had found that her "bone disintegration is being caused by the steroid drugs" she had taken for years. Her steroid treatment was tapered in the first half of 1961, when she was also taking chloroquine. In early 1964 she was anemic from a bleeding fibroid tumor and had abdominal surgery to remove it; the tumor was benign, but the operation reactivated her lupus, which had been in remission, and her kidneys were affected. Her novel Wise Blood was published in 1952 and A Good Man Is Hard to Find and Other Stories in 1955; The Complete Stories won the National Book Award after her death. O'Connor died of kidney failure from lupus in the Baldwin County Hospital in Milledgeville on August 3, 1964, at 39.
- **R:** Physician authors attribute her hip disease to avascular necrosis of the femoral head from steroid treatment, and suggest that her jaw may have been affected in the same way. Physician authors suggest from her letters and photographs that she may have had a malar facial rash early in her illness and the hand deformities of Jaccoud arthropathy.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
