Judgment calls (dossier editorial resolutions)

  1. Sources. The case relies on English Heritage, the Mary Seacole Trust, her own 1857 memoir, the Orlando profile published by Cambridge University Press, and, for two details only, London Remembers. The National Portrait Gallery and Europeana pages returned errors (403), the National Archives (UK) education page has been removed (410), and the Oxford Dictionary of National Biography could not be fetched; Jane Robinson's biography was not read, and no source quoting it was used. Wikipedia, Find a Grave, WikiTree and popular sites found in searches were not used.
  2. Recent-death rule. She died in 1881, so the rule does not apply; a sick visit is included.
  3. Name and alias. The display name is Mary Seacole. Her birth name, Mary Jane Grant (English Heritage), is the alias; it appears in no other record. “Grant” is a word in another record's display name, but the cross-case check matches whole names, and that record's name is never written here. The record also never names the nurse leader who has her own record; the Trust's account of the War Office's refusal is recorded without her. The nickname “Mother Seacole” is recorded in a claim but not used as an alias. identity.name_parts is not used. In the modern episode she is called Mrs. Seacole, as she asks.
  4. Date of birth. The year 1805 is documented (English Heritage, the roster; her memoir says only that she and the century were young together). The day, 23 November, comes only from London Remembers (CLM-0002, certainty “possible”), so the preferred birth date has year precision and the day is recorded as an unresolved alternative. It is used for the synthetic date of birth, 1954-11-23, a shift of 149 years.
  5. Index age and dates. The index visit on March 9, 2026 maps to March 9, 1877, when she was 71. She was back in London by 1869 (English Heritage) or 1870 (London Remembers) and died at home in Paddington in 1881; nothing in the sources read places her away from London in 1877, but her whereabouts that year are not specifically documented. The other visits (March 16, April 13, May 11, June 15, July 13 and September 14, 2026) map to the same days of 1877, before her 72nd birthday.
  6. Health in later life. Little is documented. Her memoir says she came home from the Crimea “shaken in health” and tired easily (CLM-0009, D); London Remembers says she returned “with poor health and poor finances.” Nothing read describes blood pressure, joint disease or falls; the modern hypertension, knee osteoarthritis, fall risk and obesity are entirely synthetic, as the brief asked. Her self-description as “stout” is documented; her weight is not.
  7. Death and apoplexy. That she died on May 14, 1881 at home in Paddington is D (CLM-0015). That she had “an apoplexy” two weeks before and three days in a coma rests on one scholarly secondary source (Orlando), so the claim is D with certainty “probable.” Reading “apoplexy” as a stroke is R (CLM-0016, “possible”); its type and cause are unknown. The modern hypertension problem is linked to these claims only as the reason for stroke-prevention teaching; the record does not claim she had hypertension.
  8. Choice of presenting problem. The presenting problem is morning headaches with a very high clinic reading (R03.0, verification provisional), resolved at the one-week review when home readings confirmed stage 2 hypertension (I10, recorded then). Knee pain with a past fall is recorded at intake as bilateral knee osteoarthritis (M17.0, clinical diagnosis) and fall risk (Z91.81). This keeps the record distinct from the hypertensive heart failure and stroke records in the dataset: the echocardiogram shows hypertrophy without heart failure, and no stroke occurs.
  9. Drug choice. Judgment call: amlodipine with chlorthalidone, two first-line classes effective in Black patients, for stage 2 hypertension without albuminuria. The consultant warned of thiazide hyponatremia in an older woman; it occurred during a cold with poor intake, chlorthalidone was stopped, and losartan was added at three months instead of raising amlodipine, because of ankle swelling. Managing a sodium of 129 mmol/L without confusion as an outpatient, with a one-week recheck, is a judgment call.
  10. Statin. Atorvastatin 20 mg (moderate intensity) was started at four weeks for a 10-year cardiovascular risk of about 14% (narrative; no calculator output is coded) with LDL 146 mg/dL.
  11. Her own remedies. Her ginger tea, liquorice root infusion and camphor and wintergreen liniment are synthetic, anchored to her documented training in Creole medicine (CLM-0003, CLM-0014) and her work as a masseuse (CLM-0012); they are not claims about remedies she used. They are respected and checked: liquorice is paused by her choice because it raises blood pressure and lowers potassium; ginger in food amounts is fine; the salicylate liniment is used separately from the diclofenac gel. No remedy is mocked or forbidden.
  12. Equity and social needs. She is described as living alone on a modest fixed income, careful with money since her documented bankruptcy. Because Orlando records that she died “in prosperous circumstances,” the record does not describe poverty. Access barriers (a cuff, transport, the stairs, the cost of medicines) are addressed by the clinic. The 3-item UCLA loneliness scores (7 and 5 of 9), the Timed Up and Go, 30-second chair stand and balance results, the ankle-brachial index, home readings and the DXA, mammogram, stool test and hepatitis C results are narrative.
  13. Real people. Her parents, husband, siblings, Thomas Day, W. H. Russell, the Princess of Wales and Count Gleichen appear only in claims. No relative or historical person is given a modern statement, decision or health fact. Her friends, her neighbour, the social prescribing link worker and her health care agent are synthetic and unnamed.
  14. Setting. The project facility (St. Posthumous Health Center) is modeled as a clinic near her home in Paddington, London, and the stroke warning tells her to call 999. The visit structures and guidelines are the U.S. ones used throughout the dataset (Medicare annual wellness visit, AHA/ACC, ACR, USPSTF, CDC); this is a modeling choice.
  15. Synthetic clinical picture. Everything in the modern episode is synthetic: symptoms, every blood pressure, all laboratory values, the ECG and echocardiogram, the knee findings, KOOS JR, pain scores, PHQ-9, PHQ-2, Mini-Cog and AUDIT-C, medicines and outcomes. Height (157 cm) and weights (78.0 to 76.0 kg) are synthetic. Reference ranges are typical adult female ranges.
  16. Sick visit. Thiazide-associated hyponatremia and hypokalemia with orthostatic hypotension and a near-fall during a cold, chosen because it follows from the treatment and the falls theme and insinuates no undocumented diagnosis.
  17. Annual wellness visit. A subsequent annual wellness visit at 71 (HCPCS G0439 and CPT 99497 in plain text). Judgment calls: PCV20 and zoster dose 1 that day; RSV vaccine not indicated at 71 without a risk condition; Tdap, high-dose influenza and zoster dose 2 at the six-month review; DXA, mammography, a stool immunochemical test (her choice) and hepatitis C screening ordered; advance care planning with an unnamed friend as agent, allowing her own remedies and massage for comfort.
  18. Focused visits. The four chronic-care follow-ups (one week, four weeks, three months and six months) are documented as focused visits on the CMID hypertension, osteoarthritis and osteoporosis monographs, the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline, the ACR/AF 2019 guideline, CDC STEADI and the USPSTF falls recommendation. One deviation is recorded instead of an invented result: the sodium and potassium recheck due one week after the sick visit was missed because the bus trip to the laboratory was too hard with her knees; five weeks later sodium was 138 mmol/L and potassium 4.2, so no harm resulted, and the corrective action is blood tests drawn at home by the community nurse. Synthetic findings introduced: the left ventricular hypertrophy, the hyponatremia, the ankle swelling on amlodipine and the osteopenia below the treatment threshold.
  19. Consultation. The geriatric medicine referral and the consultation by Benedict Osei-Hargreaves, MD (a fictional consultant), at the one-week review, are S. The consultant answers the five questions in line with what the record shows afterwards: amlodipine and chlorthalidone to a target below 130/80 with sodium and standing-pressure checks and an ARB if sodium falls (as happened); a STEADI assessment with physical therapy, a cane, tai chi and an occupational therapy visit; knee care without oral NSAIDs, with an injection as an option (offered at three months and declined); liquorice paused and her other remedies safe as used; and social prescribing, a lunch club and help with costs.
