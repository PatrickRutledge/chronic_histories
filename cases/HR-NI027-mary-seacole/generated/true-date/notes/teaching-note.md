# Teaching Note — Mary Seacole pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** She was born Mary Jane Grant in Kingston, Jamaica, in 1805, the daughter of James Grant, a white Scottish army officer, and a free Black Jamaican woman who kept a boarding house; she was born free, at a time when many Black people in the Caribbean were enslaved, and had a sister, Louisa, and a brother, Edward. In her memoir she wrote that "the century and myself were both young together" and called herself a Creole "with good Scotch blood." A London commemorative database gives her date of birth as November 23, 1805; English Heritage, the Mary Seacole Trust and her memoir give no day. Her mother was a healer, "like very many of the Creole women, an admirable doctress," and taught her traditional Jamaican medicine; as a child she practised on her doll, and she later learned "a great deal of Creole medicinal art" in her mother's house. She visited England in the early 1820s and gained some knowledge of European medicine. She married Edwin Horatio Hamilton Seacole on November 10, 1836, and they kept a store at Black River; he was delicate, she nursed him, and he died in 1844, followed closely by the death of her mother. She had no children. In 1850 she nursed victims of the cholera epidemic in Kingston, learning about its treatment from a doctor lodging in her house; in 1851 she went to Cruces, on the Isthmus of Panama, where she treated cholera with mustard emetics, warm fomentations, mustard plasters and calomel, and was called the "yellow doctress." In Cruces she had cholera herself: "I felt an unpleasant chill come over me … and, before nightfall, I knew full well that my turn had come at last, and that the cholera had attacked me." She recovered. In 1853 she cared for victims of a yellow fever epidemic in Kingston; she was invited by the medical authorities to supervise nursing at Up-Park Camp, the British Army's headquarters, and turned her mother's former lodging house into a hospital. At the outbreak of the Crimean War she went to London and asked the War Office to send her as an army nurse; she was refused. She paid her own way to the Crimea and, with Thomas Day, a relative of her husband, built the British Hotel at Spring Hill near Balaclava, a place of respite for sick and recovering soldiers; she nursed the wounded on the battlefield, sometimes under fire, and soldiers called her "Mother Seacole." She returned from the Crimea in 1856 in poor health and poor finances; in her memoir she wrote: "I returned from it shaken in health … a little labour fatigues me now--I cannot watch by sick-beds as I could--a week's want of rest quite knocks me up now." She described herself as "a hearty, strong woman--plain-spoken people might say stout." With unpaid bills and unsold stock at the end of the war, she was declared bankrupt in November 1856. The Times and Punch campaigned for her, a Seacole Fund Grand Military Festival was held in Kennington in July 1857, and her autobiography, Wonderful Adventures of Mrs. Seacole in Many Lands, was published the same month. W. H. Russell, war correspondent of The Times, wrote of her in 1857: "I trust that England will not forget one who nursed her sick, who sought out her wounded to aid and succour them, and who performed the last offices for some of her illustrious dead." She travelled between England and Jamaica in the 1860s and was back in London by about 1869 to 1870, where she mixed with royalty and acted as a personal masseuse to the Princess of Wales; she was painted by Albert Charles Challen around 1869 and sculpted by Count Gleichen in 1871. Her last known photograph was taken around 1873; little else is documented about her health or daily life in her last decade in London. Her memoir describes her lifelong practice of nursing and healing, including the remedies she carried, and her belief that the Creoles seek out "the simple remedies which are available for the terrible diseases": "beside the nettle ever grows the cure for its sting." She died on May 14, 1881, at her home in Paddington, London, aged about 75; a literary history records that she had suffered "an apoplexy" two weeks before and spent three days in a coma, that she died in prosperous circumstances with an estate of more than £2,500, and that she was buried, as she had asked, in the Roman Catholic section of Kensal Green Cemetery. She was largely forgotten for about a century, until Caribbean nurses visited her grave; in 2004 she was voted the greatest Black Briton, and in 2016 a statue of her was unveiled at St Thomas' Hospital in London. The Mary Seacole Trust continues her legacy.
- **R:** "Apoplexy" in nineteenth-century usage meant a sudden loss of consciousness and is usually read today as a stroke; whether hers was a hemorrhage or an infarct, and what caused it, cannot be known from the sources.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
