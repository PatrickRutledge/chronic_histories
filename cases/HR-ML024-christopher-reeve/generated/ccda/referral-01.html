Historical basis. These claims are historical evidence, not findings from a modern examination.

  • Christopher Reeve was born on September 25, 1952, in New York City. [D]
  • He was an actor who became a household name in the title role of the 1978 film Superman. [D]
  • On a Saturday at the end of May 1995, at 42, he was thrown headfirst when his horse stopped abruptly at the third obstacle of a cross-country event at an equestrian competition in Culpeper, Virginia. He was wearing a helmet. He broke his first and second cervical vertebrae, injured his spinal cord, and could not breathe on his own, as the University of Virginia neurosurgeon treating him reported. [D]
  • After months of therapy he was able to breathe for short periods without a respirator. He became an advocate for people with disabilities and for spinal cord research, lobbied Congress for better insurance protection against catastrophic injury, and directed the film In the Gloaming in 1997. [D]
  • The Christopher Reeve Paralysis Foundation, built on his advocacy and fundraising, became a leading source of money for spinal cord research, and he helped establish the Reeve-Irvine research center at the University of California, Irvine. [D]
  • He wrote about his life after the injury in an autobiography, Still Me, and in Nothing Is Impossible: Reflections on a New Life (Random House, 2002), which described his activity-based therapy and his return to work. [D]
  • His physicians at Washington University reported his case in 2002: his injury was classified as C-2, ASIA grade A, with no substantial recovery in the first five years. An activity-based recovery program begun in 1999 was followed over three years by improvement to ASIA grade C, with motor scores rising from 0 to 20 of 100, sensory scores from 5-7 to 58-77 of 112, and voluntary control of some muscles including the right half of the diaphragm. Reversal of osteoporosis, more muscle mass and less spasticity were associated with the recovery, medical complications fell, and infections and antibiotic use fell by more than 90 percent. [D]
  • In September 2002 the Associated Press reported that the first sign of change had been a movement of his left index finger almost two years earlier. He still used a wheelchair and a ventilator all but one hour a day and needed around-the-clock nursing care. He could feel about two-thirds of normal touch, which let him know when to shift his weight, so that he could sit in his wheelchair up to 16 hours a day without getting pressure sores. His program included electrically stimulated cycling for an hour three times a week and weekly pool therapy. Before it, his muscles had withered, his bones had thinned, and he had had repeated infections and life-threatening complications. [D]
  • In early 2003 surgeons at University Hospitals of Cleveland led by Dr. Raymond Onders implanted a diaphragm pacing system through small abdominal incisions. His phrenic nerves had not been injured. After eight years on a respirator, breathing through a tracheostomy, he could breathe for more than two hours without the respirator, and his doctors hoped the stimulation would let him wean further. [D]

Plan of Treatment

  • Autonomic dysreflexia: a written plan and wallet card (sit him up, loosen clothing, check the catheter and bladder, then the bowel and skin; check blood pressure every 2 to 5 minutes; nitroglycerin ointment only if systolic pressure stays at 150 mmHg or higher), his baseline blood pressure recorded, and training for every nurse. Guideline: Evaluation and Management of Autonomic Dysreflexia and Other Autonomic Dysfunctions: Preventing the Highs and Lows (Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine) (2021) [S]
  • Pressure injury prevention: skin checks morning and night by his nurses, tilt-in-space pressure relief every 30 minutes in the chair, turning every 2 hours in bed on a pressure-redistributing mattress, cushion and seating review, nutrition, and the Braden scale at each visit. Guideline: Pressure Ulcer Prevention and Treatment Following Spinal Cord Injury: A Clinical Practice Guideline for Health-Care Providers, Second Edition (Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine) (2014) [S]
  • Neurogenic bladder: catheter care with monthly changes and closed drainage, no treatment of bacteria in the urine without symptoms, kidney function by cystatin C, and kidney and bladder ultrasound yearly. Guideline: Bladder management for adults with spinal cord injury: a clinical practice guideline for health-care providers (Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine) (2006); The AUA/SUFU Guideline on Adult Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction: Treatment and Follow-up (2021) [S]
  • Neurogenic bowel: a daily morning program with a suppository and digital stimulation, senna at night, fiber and fluids, reviewed at each visit. Guideline: Management of Neurogenic Bowel Dysfunction in Adults after Spinal Cord Injury: Clinical Practice Guideline for Health Care Providers (Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine) (2021) [S]
  • Respiratory care: tracheostomy care, suctioning, daily mechanical insufflation-exsufflation for secretion clearance, a pacer conditioning schedule with oxygen saturation checks, a backup ventilator, a spare tracheostomy tube, a generator and battery plan for power cuts, and registration with the power company and emergency services. Guideline: Respiratory management following spinal cord injury: a clinical practice guideline for health-care professionals (Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine) (2005) [S]
  • Bone health and cardiometabolic risk: calcium, 25-hydroxyvitamin D and PTH, DXA of the hip and knee regions, continued electrically stimulated cycling, careful transfers; lipids, A1c and blood pressure yearly. Guideline: Bone Health and Osteoporosis Management in Individuals with Spinal Cord Injury: Clinical Practice Guideline for Health Care Providers (Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine) (2022); Identification and Management of Cardiometabolic Risk after Spinal Cord Injury: Clinical Practice Guideline for Health Care Providers (Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine) (2018); CMID: Clinical Monitoring Intelligence Database, osteoporosis monograph (2026) [S]

Guideline: Management of Mental Health Disorders, Substance Use Disorders, and Suicide in Adults with Spinal Cord Injury: Clinical Practice Guideline for Healthcare Providers (Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine) (2021); Prevention of Venous Thromboembolism in Individuals with Spinal Cord Injury: Clinical Practice Guidelines for Health Care Providers, 3rd ed.
