# Teaching Note — Christopher Reeve pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Christopher Reeve was born on September 25, 1952, in New York City. He was an actor who became a household name in the title role of the 1978 film Superman. On a Saturday at the end of May 1995, at 42, he was thrown headfirst when his horse stopped abruptly at the third obstacle of a cross-country event at an equestrian competition in Culpeper, Virginia. He was wearing a helmet. He broke his first and second cervical vertebrae, injured his spinal cord, and could not breathe on his own, as the University of Virginia neurosurgeon treating him reported. After months of therapy he was able to breathe for short periods without a respirator. He became an advocate for people with disabilities and for spinal cord research, lobbied Congress for better insurance protection against catastrophic injury, and directed the film In the Gloaming in 1997. The Christopher Reeve Paralysis Foundation, built on his advocacy and fundraising, became a leading source of money for spinal cord research, and he helped establish the Reeve-Irvine research center at the University of California, Irvine. He wrote about his life after the injury in an autobiography, Still Me, and in Nothing Is Impossible: Reflections on a New Life (Random House, 2002), which described his activity-based therapy and his return to work. His physicians at Washington University reported his case in 2002: his injury was classified as C-2, ASIA grade A, with no substantial recovery in the first five years. An activity-based recovery program begun in 1999 was followed over three years by improvement to ASIA grade C, with motor scores rising from 0 to 20 of 100, sensory scores from 5-7 to 58-77 of 112, and voluntary control of some muscles including the right half of the diaphragm. Reversal of osteoporosis, more muscle mass and less spasticity were associated with the recovery, medical complications fell, and infections and antibiotic use fell by more than 90 percent. In September 2002 the Associated Press reported that the first sign of change had been a movement of his left index finger almost two years earlier. He still used a wheelchair and a ventilator all but one hour a day and needed around-the-clock nursing care. He could feel about two-thirds of normal touch, which let him know when to shift his weight, so that he could sit in his wheelchair up to 16 hours a day without getting pressure sores. His program included electrically stimulated cycling for an hour three times a week and weekly pool therapy. Before it, his muscles had withered, his bones had thinned, and he had had repeated infections and life-threatening complications. In early 2003 surgeons at University Hospitals of Cleveland led by Dr. Raymond Onders implanted a diaphragm pacing system through small abdominal incisions. His phrenic nerves had not been injured. After eight years on a respirator, breathing through a tracheostomy, he could breathe for more than two hours without the respirator, and his doctors hoped the stimulation would let him wean further. On October 9, 2004, he went into cardiac arrest at his home in New York and fell into a coma; he died on October 10, 2004, at 52. His publicist said he had been treated for a pressure wound, a common complication of paralysis, which had become severely infected and led to a serious systemic infection.
- **R:** None recorded.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
