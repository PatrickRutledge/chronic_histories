# Teaching Note — Jane Austen pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Jane Austen was born at the Rectory in Steventon, a village in north-east Hampshire, on 16 December 1775, the seventh child and second daughter of the rector, the Revd George Austen, and his wife Cassandra (née Leigh). Steventon Rectory was her home for the first 25 years of her life; after some years in Bath and Southampton she moved in 1809 with her mother and sister to a cottage at Chawton, on one of her brother Edward's Hampshire estates, where she revised her early novels and wrote Mansfield Park, Emma and Persuasion. Her only sister, Cassandra, like Jane herself, never married. In 1783, as a young child at school in Southampton with her sister, she caught a putrid fever and was very ill and nearly died. The chronology in the 1913 family biography dates the first break in her health to about March 1816. In May 1816 she and Cassandra spent three weeks at Cheltenham, stopping at Steventon and at Kintbury on the way; the family biographers judged that she 'must have been decidedly out of health, for the change in her did not escape the notice of her friends.' She wrote 'Finis' at the end of the first draft of Persuasion on 18 July 1816; the family memoir quoted in the 1913 biography speaks of the weak state of her health at the time. In a letter to Cassandra on 8 September 1816 she wrote: 'Thank you, my back has given me scarcely any pain for many days. I have an idea that agitation does it as much harm as fatigue, and that I was ill at the time of your going from the very circumstance of your going.' On 20 February 1817 she wrote to her niece Fanny Knight that she was 'almost entirely cured of my rheumatism—just a little pain in my knee, now and then'. On 23 March 1817 she wrote to Fanny Knight: 'I certainly have not been well for many weeks, and about a week ago I was very poorly. I have had a good deal of fever at times, and indifferent nights; but I am considerably better now and am recovering my looks a little, which have been bad enough—black and white and every wrong colour.' On 6 April 1817 she wrote to her brother Charles that she had been 'suffering from a bilious attack attended with a good deal of fever', that she lived upstairs for the present, and that she was not strong enough to travel except in a closed carriage. Her niece Caroline, who saw her in the spring of 1817, recalled that she was very pale, her voice weak and low, with 'a general appearance of debility and suffering', and had been told that she never had much acute pain. On 24 May 1817 she moved to Winchester to be under the care of Mr. Lyford; she died there on 18 July 1817 at lodgings at No. 8 College Street, and was buried a few days later in the north aisle of Winchester Cathedral. Her father died on 21 January 1805 after an illness of only forty-eight hours.
- **R:** The Jane Austen Society's biography says she fell ill in 1816, possibly with Addison's disease. In 1964 the surgeon Sir Zachary Cope proposed in the British Medical Journal that her final illness was Addison's disease, which in his time was usually tuberculous; he relied heavily on her description of her colouring. Annette Upfal argued in 2005 that the evidence tends to exclude Addison's disease and that her fatal illness was quite possibly Hodgkin's disease, a lymphoma, with an immune deficiency perhaps present by January 1813; she also noted that Cassandra destroyed letters containing medical detail and that the cause cannot now be established conclusively. Katherine White argued in 2009 that symptoms reported by Addison's patients of the period (mental confusion, generalised pain, weight loss and anorexia) are absent from her letters, so Addison's disease is unlikely, and that disseminated bovine tuberculosis would explain her symptoms coherently. Sanders and Graham argued in 2021 that the rheumatism, facial skin changes, fever and fluctuating symptoms in her letters fulfil current classification criteria for systemic lupus erythematosus. A British Library curator noted in 2017 that her early death has been attributed to Addison's disease, cancer and tuberculosis, and that in 2011 a crime writer suggested arsenic poisoning on the basis of her facial pigmentation; the curator presented cataracts, possibly from arsenic, only as a theory raised by her spectacles.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
