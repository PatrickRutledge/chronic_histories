## Assessment

1. **Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease)** (E27.1). Confirmed. Cortisol does not rise after cosyntropin (peak 3.9 ug/dL, against a threshold of 18), and the ACTH of 548 pg/mL shows the failure is in the adrenal glands rather than the pituitary. The high renin with low aldosterone shows mineralocorticoid deficiency too, which accounts for the low sodium, high potassium, salt craving and postural drop. The pigmentation, the raised eosinophils, the mild anemia and the back ache all fit. Treatment should start today without waiting for the cause. **S**
2. **Cause and differential** (E27.1). In a woman of 40 in this country autoimmune adrenalitis is by far the commonest cause, and 21-hydroxylase antibodies will show it in most cases. If they are negative, the adrenal glands should be imaged by CT to look for enlargement or calcification, and tuberculosis tested for, because tuberculosis remains the main infectious cause worldwide. Lymphoma can rarely infiltrate both adrenals, and her weight loss and evening feverishness deserve to be taken seriously; but with no nodes or enlarged spleen, a normal LDH and blood count apart from the eosinophils, and a normal chest X-ray, it is unlikely. Low-grade feverishness and weight loss are common in untreated adrenal insufficiency and should settle on replacement; if they do not, or if nodes appear, CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis is needed. **S**
3. **Mild normocytic anemia** (D64.9). With normal iron, vitamin B12 and folate, this is the anemia of untreated adrenal insufficiency and should recover on replacement. **S**

## Recommendations

1. Start hydrocortisone today: 10 mg on waking and 5 mg at 2 pm (15 mg a day), adjusted by weight, energy, appetite, pigmentation and blood pressure rather than by cortisol levels.
2. Start fludrocortisone 0.1 mg each morning; check sodium and potassium at two weeks and renin with lying and standing blood pressure at about six weeks, aiming for renin in the upper normal range; she should salt food to taste.
3. 21-hydroxylase antibodies at the two-week visit (they are not affected by treatment); adrenal CT and tuberculosis testing only if they are negative.
4. Keep lymphoma in the differential until weight and symptoms have recovered: weight, fevers, night sweats, lymph nodes and spleen at each visit, and CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis if weight loss, fevers or nodes persist once replacement is established.
5. Sick-day rules: double the hydrocortisone for fever above 38 °C and triple it above 39 °C until a day after the fever settles; inject 100 mg into the thigh and call 911 for vomiting, inability to take tablets, or feeling faint. For surgery under general anesthesia or a major illness in hospital, hydrocortisone 100 mg intravenously at induction and then 200 mg a day until she is eating, tapering back over one to two days.
6. An emergency hydrocortisone injection kit with training today, a steroid emergency card, a medical ID, and a written emergency letter.
7. Screen for associated autoimmune disease: TSH (normal today) at least yearly; A1c and celiac serology with total IgA at the two-week visit, and vitamin B12 (normal at intake) yearly; ask about periods at each visit and check FSH if they become irregular or stop.
8. Repeat the blood count at two and six weeks; no further anemia work-up unless it fails to recover.
9. Endocrinology review at least yearly.
