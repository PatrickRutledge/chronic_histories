# Teaching Note — Abraham Lincoln pilot

> **SYNTHETIC RECORD, NOT A REAL MEDICAL RECORD**

## Why this case matters

This case shows how historical evidence can support a transparent fictional modern episode without presenting interpretation or generated content as documented fact.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish documented (**D**), retrospective (**R**), and synthetic (**S**) content.
2. Keep historical and active problems distinct.
3. Connect measurable goals and interventions to guidelines.
4. Preserve strengths, function, identity, and agency.

## Destigmatization message

A health condition is one part of a person’s life and does not erase achievement, relationships, purpose, or agency.

## Discussion questions

- Which facts support concern without establishing a modern diagnosis?
- Which entries belong in active problems, historical problems, or both?
- What additional evidence would change an evidence grade?
- How should an EHR distinguish synthetic and historical content?

## Evidence-grade summary

- **D:** Abraham Lincoln was born February 12, 1809, near what is now Hodgenville, Kentucky. Lincoln was mortally wounded at Ford’s Theatre on the night of April 14, 1865, and died at the Petersen House at 7:22 a.m. on April 15, 1865, aged 56. In 1859 Lincoln described himself as "six feet, four inches, nearly" in height. In his tenth year Lincoln was kicked by a horse and, in his own account, "apparently killed for a time." On January 23, 1841, Lincoln wrote to John T. Stuart, "I am now the most miserable man living." Lincoln’s mother, Nancy Hanks Lincoln, died of milk sickness on October 5, 1818, when he was nine. Lincoln’s older sister, Sarah Lincoln Grigsby, died on January 20, 1828, at age twenty-one, from complications during delivery that also took her infant’s life. Lincoln’s son Edward ("Eddie") died on February 1, 1850, after a long illness, at three years and ten months. Lincoln’s son William ("Willie") died in the White House on February 20, 1862, probably of typhoid fever, at age eleven.
- **R:** Lincoln’s recurrent melancholy is retrospectively interpreted by modern clinicians as chronic depression that did not prevent him from leading the nation. Marfan syndrome, or another inherited marfanoid disorder such as multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2B, has been proposed retrospectively from Lincoln’s build; the diagnosis is disputed and has never been genetically confirmed. Lincoln’s November 1863 illness after the Gettysburg Address has been retrospectively interpreted as unmodified smallpox, more serious than his physicians reported publicly; the interpretation is disputed.
- **S:** Shifted identity, fictional care team, modern encounters, results, plans, and outcomes.
